## 1. Hook

Here is a bargain no other chapter will offer twice. Yesterday you paid full price for the
क-row: five slots, the tissue test, the breathy hum — a whole lesson to build the machine.
Today the same machine slides one step forward in your mouth, onto the hard palate, and five
new letters drop into the slots almost by themselves: च छ ज झ ञ. Same row, new address —
that was the chapter's promise, and today it starts paying. But this row hides two things
the velars could not give you. A warning: Hindi's dotted ज़ is a border-crossing Sanskrit
never made. And a prize: among today's words sits चलति, "moves" — the first Sanskrit verb
you will read with your own eyes. For two chapters your words have sat still, naming things.
Today one of them gets up and walks.

2. The tissue test: which of क/ख throws the tissue across the room — and what exactly is

3. **Write from memory:** खग and मुख. What does खग literally say about birds?
4. Which velar letter is the [ŋ] of English *sing* — and where does that letter mostly

5. English *kite* begins closer to which Sanskrit sound, क or ख — and why does that battle

<details><summary>उत्तराणि · Answers</summary>

1. क voiceless — ख voiceless aspirated — ग voiced — घ voiced breathy — ङ nasal. Five slots,

2. ख throws it. The puff is aspiration: a burst of voiceless breath released after the

   will push English speakers from च toward छ — the un-aspirating battle transfers with
   the row.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — one row, two honest descriptions, and a promise kept.

Since the sound map of v1.c01.l02, this row has carried the address label तालव्य — "of the
palate". Today the label becomes muscle. But this particular row also carries the course's
most interesting honesty note, promised when the grid was first drawn: the row where the
ancient description and the modern mouth genuinely differ.

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The Pāṇinīya Śikṣā and the traditional sound catalogue file the
> च-row as तालव्य (tālavya) "of the palate" — the same address the tradition assigns to the
> vowels इ and ई. Sorting every sound by its place in the mouth, from the throat forward,
> is the tradition's own organizing idea, and modern phonetics maps onto it cleanly.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** Pāṇinian phonetics describes the च-row with the same
> complete-contact manner as every other stop row — in modern terms, palatal stops, [c ɟ].
> The pronunciation current across the Indic world, shared by the modern Indo-Aryan
> languages and by recitation practice as we have it, renders the row as affricates: a stop
> released into friction. [REF:cardona1997]

Both descriptions are true to their object. The old grammarians described a system in which
this row sat as pure stops; the living tradition of pronunciation moved, as pronunciations
do, and every mouth you will hear — including this course's audio — says affricates. This
course teaches the affricate as the target, matching pan-Indic pronunciation; that decision
is documented in the style contract, and the Pāṇinian description keeps its place of honor
here, in the HISTORY register, where a fact about the past belongs.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — what an affricate is, and why this row hisses.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** A stop closes the vocal tract completely and releases cleanly; an
> affricate closes completely and releases *into friction* — the seal peels open into a
> narrow channel and the escaping air hisses. Two phases, one sound, written in IPA with a
> tie-bar: /t͡ɕ/. [REF:ladefoged2015]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The palatal row as this course teaches it: च /t͡ɕ/, छ /t͡ɕʰ/, ज /d͡ʑ/,
> झ /d͡ʑʱ/, ञ /ɲ/ — tongue body arched against the hard palate, lips neutral, and the same
> five manner slots as the velar row. [REF:ohala1994]

Three consequences for the learner:

**Nothing about manner is new.** Voiceless, aspirated, voiced, breathy, nasal — the whole
row you built yesterday reapplies unchanged, and so do its physical checks: the tissue
barely stirs on च, flies on छ; the throat hums on ज and adds a warm sigh on झ; the air
reroutes through the nose on ञ. Only the *place* moved. This is the chapter's engine
working as designed: learn the row once, pay less each day.

**The English anchors, with their flaws stated.** The *ch* of *church* and the *j* of
*judge* are genuinely close — English also makes these as two-phase stop-plus-hiss sounds.
But they differ twice. English rounds the lips on *ch* — watch a mirror — and gives it a
puff of aspiration, so English *church* actually starts nearer छ than च. Sanskrit च is
unrounded and unaspirated: flat lips, no puff. And the fine print of place: English uses
the tongue tip-and-blade at the ridge behind the teeth, while Sanskrit arches the tongue
*body* on the palate with the tip resting down (§11).

**The nasal has a famous cousin.** ञ /ɲ/ is close to Spanish ñ (*señor*) and to the *ny*
of English *canyon* — with the imprecision stated: *canyon* holds a two-sound sequence
[nj], while ञ is a single nasal made at the palate, one gesture, not two.

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतव्याख्या — the lesson's idea in the simplest possible Sanskrit.

च इति तालव्यः वर्णः।
(ca iti tālavyaḥ varṇaḥ.) "*ca* is a palatal sound." — च तालव्य वर्ण है।

जल अत्र अस्ति।
(jala atra asti.) "Water is here." — जल यहाँ है।

खग चलति।
(khaga calati.) "The bird moves." — पक्षी चलता है।

Savor the last two lines: every Devanagari sign in them is yours. जल and खग stand in their
bare stem form, the citation shape this course's word lists have used since Chapter 1; in
fully dressed sentences they would wear endings (the खः-style visarga you can already read,
and a -म् tail that waits for Chapter 5's machinery). The dress rehearsal is staged for the
chapter's sentence milestone in lesson 5. And खग चलति contains the course's first readable
verb — a word that *does* something. Returning terms today: इति (iti) the quoting word,
तालव्य (tālavya) "of the palate", वर्ण (varṇa) "speech sound".

## 7. Hindi Explanation

हिन्दी व्याख्या

आज की पंक्ति पर आपका जन्मसिद्ध अधिकार है। च से चलना, छ से छाता, ज से जल, झ से झूला — ये
ध्वनियाँ आप बचपन से बोलते आए हैं, और पाँचों अक्षरों की लिखावट भी आपके हाथ में बैठी हुई है।
कल की क-पंक्ति वाला पूरा ढाँचा — पाँच खाने, तिनके वाली साँस की परीक्षा, गले की गूँज — आज
ज्यों-का-त्यों एक क़दम आगे सरक आया है। इसलिए आज की असली कमाई दो बारीक सावधानियाँ हैं।

पहली सावधानी — नुक़्ते वाला ज़। हिन्दी में ज़रूरत, ज़्यादा, बाज़ार का ज़ [z] फ़ारसी-अरबी से आया
मेहमान है — हिन्दी में वह बिलकुल सही है, पर संस्कृत में वह ध्वनि है ही नहीं। संस्कृत का ज
हमेशा पूरे स्पर्श वाला ज है: जीभ तालु से पूरी तरह जुड़ती है, फिर घर्षण के साथ खुलती है। जल
को "ज़ल" पढ़ना संस्कृत में चूक है — नियम एक पंक्ति का है: संस्कृत पढ़ते समय नुक़्ता घर पर
छोड़ आइए। (उसका जोड़ीदार फ़ भी यही कहानी है — उसकी बारी प-वर्ग में आएगी।)

दूसरी सावधानी — अकेला ञ। ध्वनि आप जानते हैं: पञ्च, चञ्चल जैसे शब्दों के भीतर नाक से बजती
वही झंकार। पर हिन्दी में ञ प्रायः संयुक्त रूपों के भीतर ही दिखता है, इसलिए उसकी अकेली
शक्ल — बिना डण्डे की, चौड़ी तश्तरी जैसी — आँख को अजनबी लगेगी। आज का काम केवल पहचान और
ध्वनि है; पञ्च जैसे जुड़े रूपों को पढ़ना अध्याय 5 का विषय है। और श्रुतलेख में ज/ञ की
अदला-बदली पर नज़र रखिए — ज खड़ा है, ञ लेटा हुआ।

बाक़ी अनुशासन पुराने हैं: छ की साँस पूरी (छल बोलने पर तिनका उड़े), झ की गूँज गहरी और
भरी हुई, और शब्द के अन्त का अ हमेशा पूरा — जल ज-ल है, "jal" नहीं; चल च-ल है, "chal" नहीं।

वह पहले से आपकी अपनी भाषा में धड़क रही थी।

## 8. English Explanation

Two storylines today: a **transfer** (the five-slot row you own, at a new place) and a
**first** (a verb you can read). Both run on yesterday's machinery.

### The row, transferred

| Slot (manner) | क-वर्ग — yesterday | च-वर्ग — today |
|---|---|---|
| 1 voiceless | क k | च c |
| 2 voiceless aspirated | ख kh | छ ch |
| 3 voiced | ग g | ज j |
| 4 voiced breathy | घ gh | झ jh |
| 5 nasal | ङ ṅ | ञ ñ |

Read down yesterday's column, then down today's: the *relationships* are identical. छ is to
च exactly what ख is to क — the same letter plus a thrown tissue. झ is to ज what घ is to ग.
The only genuinely new ingredient is in the release: velars pop open cleanly; palatals peel
open through a hiss (§5). Once your ear catches that hiss-trail, the whole row sounds like
family.

### The five cards — equal citizens

**च (ca) /t͡ɕ/ — slot 1: voiceless, unaspirated.** The *ch* of *church* minus two English
habits: unround the lips, cancel the puff. The tissue should barely tremble. Meet it in
चल "moving" — and in चलति, the star of today.

**छ (cha) /t͡ɕʰ/ — slot 2: the tissue-thrower.** Same gesture as च plus a full burst of
voiceless breath. English speakers: your everyday *ch* already leans this way, which is
exactly why च needs the discipline — keep the two slots apart. Meet it in छल "deceit".

**ज (ja) /d͡ʑ/ — slot 3: voiced.** The *j* of *judge*, unrounded, with the throat humming
through the closure. Meet it in जल "water" — and read the warning below before Hindi habit
reads it for you.

**झ (jha) /d͡ʑʱ/ — slot 4: voiced breathy.** The murmured one: ज with the warm, sighing
release you built for घ. Honest frequency note: few native Sanskrit words *begin* with झ
at your current glyph stage, so today झ earns its keep in syllable drills — an equal card,
frankly described, exactly like ङ yesterday.

**ञ (ña) /ɲ/ — slot 5: nasal.** One nasal, made where the whole row is made. Like ङ, it is
a conjunct-dweller: in real text it lives pressed against its own varga stops — you have
glimpsed it inside पञ्च "five" — and reading such knots is Chapter 5's business. Today you
learn its sound, its standalone shape, and its slot.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The dotted ज़ of Hindi — ज़रूरत, बाज़ार — spells [z], a fricative that
> entered Hindi through Perso-Arabic loanwords. Sanskrit has no [z]: ज is always the full
> voiced affricate /d͡ʑ/, so जल is *jala*, never "zala". [REF:masica1991], [REF:ohala1994]

One famous knot, shown and not taught: ज् + ञ makes the celebrated conjunct ज्ञ (jña), the
one inside ज्ञान "knowledge" — its modern pronunciations vary widely across regions, and
both the reading and the debate wait for Chapter 5. Today, simply enjoy knowing which two
letters live inside it — both of them yours as of this lesson.

### The payoff — reading, worked out

- **जल** = ज + ल → ja-la "water"
- **जलज** = ज + ल + ज → ja-la-ja "water-born" — the lotus, self-assembled (§13)
- **चलति** = च + ल + ति → ca-la-ti "moves" — see below
- **कूजति** = कू + ज + ति → kū-ja-ti "coos, warbles" — the cuckoo's own verb (§13, §17)

### The verb moment

For two chapters every word you could read has been a *thing*: a family, an owl, a
riverbank. चलति is different: it *happens*. Read खग चलति and you have not named the world —
you have reported an event in it. How Sanskrit builds its verbs — roots, endings, persons —
is Volume 2's story, told properly; today चलति is glossed as one whole word, "moves", and
that is all you need to enjoy the first sentence that moves. One more honest line: the
audio also lets you *hear* गच्छति "goes" 🔊 — its च्छ knot is Chapter 5 material, so it
stays an ear-word for now. You can hear it; चलति you can read.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — four drills, in order. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l02-varga-drill.mp3` (drills 1–3) and
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l02-word-list.mp3` (drill 4).

**Drill 1 — the recital, transferred.** Say the row with yesterday's labels, one beat each:
च (plain) — छ (puff) — ज (hum) — झ (hum + sigh) — ञ (nose). Three rounds: first bare;
second with the tissue held before your lips — it should barely stir, fly, stay calm, stay
calm, stay calm; third with eyes closed, listening for the hiss-trail on every release.

**Drill 2 — slow-motion release.** Freeze the start of च: tongue body sealed on the palate,
tip down, no sound. Hold two seconds. Now release *slowly* — the seal peels into a hiss
before the vowel lands: च. Contrast with yesterday: alternate का — चा, का — चा, and hear
the clean pop against the hiss-trail. That trailing friction is the affricate signature.

**Drill 3 — the syllable grid, mixed mātrās.** Read across, then down; long vowels (े ो
and ा) get their two full beats:

| with ि | with ु | with े | with ो |
|---|---|---|---|
| चि /t͡ɕi/ | चु /t͡ɕu/ | चे /t͡ɕeː/ | चो /t͡ɕoː/ |
| छि /t͡ɕʰi/ | छु /t͡ɕʰu/ | छे /t͡ɕʰeː/ | छो /t͡ɕʰoː/ |
| जि /d͡ʑi/ | जु /d͡ʑu/ | जे /d͡ʑeː/ | जो /d͡ʑoː/ |
| झि /d͡ʑʱi/ | झु /d͡ʑʱu/ | झे /d͡ʑʱeː/ | झो /d͡ʑʱoː/ |
| ञि /ɲi/ | ञु /ɲu/ | ञे /ɲeː/ | ञो /ɲoː/ |

The ञ row is muscle practice, not vocabulary — do it anyway; the rare letters earn their
slots in the mouth before they earn them on the page.

**Drill 4 — words, and the lesson's pair.** From §14: जल, चल, छल, अचल, जलज, जित, चलति,
कूजति — block by block, then whole. Finish with the aspiration pair, keeping everything
identical except the puff: चल — छल · चल — छल. One burst of breath separates "moving" from
"deceit"; your pair bank gains its first consonant pair.

## 10. IPA

The new consonants, formally: च **/t͡ɕ/** · छ **/t͡ɕʰ/** · ज **/d͡ʑ/** · झ **/d͡ʑʱ/** ·
ञ **/ɲ/**.

**The tie-bar.** /t͡ɕ/ is two symbols wearing one bow: stop phase (t-like closure) plus
fricative phase (the hiss ɕ), bound into a single sound. IPA needs the bar precisely so
that a one-sound affricate is never mistaken for a two-sound cluster.

**ʰ vs ʱ, again.** As in the velar row: the raised ʰ of छ is a voiceless puff; the raised
ʱ of झ is breathy voice, the murmured sigh. The notation is telling you the tissue test and
the throat-hum test in superscript.

**Why not [c ɟ]?** Those symbols name pure palatal stops — the Pāṇinian description of
this row (§4). Our target values are the affricates of pan-Indic pronunciation, so the
course prints the tie-bar forms. Narrow note for the curious: Sanskrit's affricates are
alveolo-palatal (tongue *body*, [t͡ɕ]), while English *church/judge* are palato-alveolar
(tip-and-blade, [t͡ʃ] and [d͡ʒ]) and usually lip-rounded — close neighbors, not twins.

**The symbol that stays away.** IPA has [z], and Hindi uses it in ज़-words; the Sanskrit
inventory simply has no [z] anywhere. If a recording of your जल shows a smooth buzz with
no closure, the nuqta habit is driving (§12).

**One IAST footnote.** In IAST, *ch* is not the English digraph: it spells छ, the aspirated
partner of *c* (च). Romanized Sanskrit is code, not English spelling.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — finding the palatal place (see the mouth inset on the letter-card sheet,
`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c03l02-ca-varga-cards.svg`).

1. **Tongue tip: down.** Rest it lightly behind your lower front teeth and leave it there.
   This is the row's signature move — the tip is off duty today.
2. **Tongue body: up.** Arch the middle of the tongue and press it against the hard
   palate — the smooth dome behind the ridge, the place the tradition calls तालु (tālu)
   "palate". Full contact, a real seal.
3. **Lips: neutral.** Flat and relaxed. If the mirror shows the little forward pucker of
   English *church*, undo it — rounding belongs to उ/ऊ and to English, not to this row.
4. **The ई doorway.** Say a long ई and freeze: your tongue body is already high and
   forward, at the palatal address — इ, ई and this row are all तालव्य (§4). From that
   frozen ई, press up the last few millimeters to a full seal, then release: च. The vowel
   you have owned since Chapter 2 is the doorway to the consonants of today.
5. **The release.** Do not snap open like क — let the seal peel into its brief hiss. Stop
   phase, friction phase, vowel.

**Checks.** Mirror: lips flat on every syllable of drill 3. Tissue: barely stirs on च and
ज, flies on छ, and on झ the hum arrives *with* warm breath. Nose-pinch for ञ: pinch your
nostrils shut and try to hold ञ — the sound should choke immediately, because ञ lives in
the nose; if it sails on, you are saying ज.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **The rounded, puffed *ch* (English speakers).** Importing *church* wholesale gives a
   च with rounded lips and a burst of breath — which is a छ in costume. *Fix:* mirror for
   the lips, tissue for the puff. Build च downward from your own *sky*-trick of yesterday:
   slot 1 is always the bare, unpuffed member. Then drill चल — छल until the two slots
   refuse to merge.
2. **ज read as [z] (Hindi speakers' nuqta habit).** Years of ज़रूरत and बाज़ार train the eye
   to soften ज on sight, and जल comes out "zal". In Hindi the dot is correct; in Sanskrit
   the sound does not exist. *Fix:* feel the seal — a true ज blocks the air completely
   before releasing; a [z] never closes. Say जल with the tongue body glued to the palate
   first. No contact, no ज.
3. **छ under-thrown.** If your छ tissue-test looks like your च tissue-test, the contrast
   has collapsed — the commonest version is an English *ch* standing in for both. *Fix:*
   exaggerate: over-breathe छल until the tissue flies, then scale back to natural. The
   velar cure transfers: whatever fixed ख fixes छ.
4. **ञ read as न, or written as ज.** The unfamiliar standalone shape sends readers to the
   nearest familiar nasal (न, the dental) and writers to its curly look-alike ज. *Fix:*
   for the sound — tip down, body up, nose-pinch check (§11). For the shape — the §15
   chant: ज stands (full right stem), ञ lies low (no stem, a broad bowl). In dictation,
   ask "did I write the stander or the lier?" before moving on.

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — a word that builds itself, a pattern that returns, and a sentence that sings.

### जलज — the lotus, self-assembled

**जल** (jala) "water" — जल, पानी · /d͡ʑɐlɐ/
**जलज** (jalaja) "water-born; lotus" — कमल · /d͡ʑɐlɐd͡ʑɐ/
[REF:apte1890 s.v. जल, जलज]

Sanskrit routinely welds meaning from parts, and here is a first taste you can read whole:
जल "water" plus the bound element -ज "born (of)" gives "the water-born one" — the lotus.
Chapter 1's कमल just gained a poetic synonym, and you just read your first transparent
derivation. The full compound-building machinery is Volume 2's story; today, one flower.

### अचल — the अ- switch returns

**चल** (cala) "moving, unsteady" — चंचल, अस्थिर · /t͡ɕɐlɐ/
**अचल** (acala) "unmoving; mountain" — अचल; पर्वत · /ɐt͡ɕɐlɐ/
[REF:apte1890 s.v. चल, अचल]

You have seen this switch before: मृत "dead" flipped to अमृत "the deathless" in Chapter 2.
The same negative prefix अ- now flips चल "moving" to अचल "unmoving" — and Sanskrit's word
for a mountain is exactly that: *the one that does not move*. Two sightings make a pattern:
start watching for अ- doing this everywhere.

**कोकिलः कूजति।**
(kokilaḥ kūjati.) "The cuckoo sings." — कोयल कूकती है।

The कोकिल of Chapter 2 returns wearing its full nominative dress — and every sign here is
readable: the visarga since v1.c02.l06, the verb since today. A complete, fully correct
Sanskrit sentence, previewed ahead of the chapter's official milestone in lesson 5.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule**: the grid grows a second row, and five
new shapes join your hand.

### The grid, two rows strong

| वर्ग | place | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| क-वर्ग | velar (कण्ठ्य) | क | ख | ग | घ | ङ |
| च-वर्ग | palatal (तालव्य) | च | छ | ज | झ | ञ |
| ट-वर्ग | — lesson 3 | · | · | · | · | · |
| त-वर्ग | — lesson 4 | · | · | · | · | · |
| प-वर्ग | — lesson 5 | · | · | · | · | · |

Column numbers are the manner slots: 1 voiceless, 2 voiceless aspirated, 3 voiced,
4 voiced breathy, 5 nasal. Two rows down, three to go — and every future row costs less
than the last, because the columns never change.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The mātrā system is letter-blind: every vowel sign attaches to the
> new letters exactly as it does to क, म, त, ल — च + ि = चि, ज + ू = जू, छ + े = छे. New
> consonants plug into the existing machine; nothing about the signs themselves changes.
> [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

### Stroke order — च छ ज झ ञ

One common convention, described here; teaching traditions vary in details, and your
teacher's consistent habit is fine. (Card: `planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c03l02-stroke-order.svg`;
tracing: `planned:assets/worksheets/volume-1/v1c03l02-tracing.pdf`.)

- **च** — the belly first: start mid-height, sweep left and down into the round belly and
  close it back on itself; then the vertical stem on the right; headline last.
- **छ** — a stemless letter: from the top, the small closed ring; then, without lifting,
  swing down and right into the open tail that curls back under itself; headline last.
- **ज** — the upper arm curving down from the headline; then the lower arm reaching left,
  ending in its small upward curl; then the full vertical stem closing the right side;
  headline last.
- **झ** — the left body first, two stacked bowls like the numeral ३; then the connecting
  arm; then the right vertical stem; headline last.
- **ञ** — the top arm with its leftward curl first; beneath it the broad, upward-opening
  bowl; headline last. Nothing stands vertical.

### ज vs ञ — the discrimination check

The two curliest letters of the day are genuine look-alikes, and dictation will test you.
The chant: **ज stands, ञ lies low.** ज carries a full stem from headline to baseline — the
letter you have written in जल a thousand times. ञ has no stem at all; its parts stack flat,
arm over bowl, hugging the line. When in doubt mid-dictation, ask which of the two your
hand just made — the stander is ज, the lier is ञ.

## 18. Exercises

अभ्यासाः — six exercises; 1, 2, 4 and 6 are production tasks. Do them in order.

**अभ्यासः 1 · वर्ग-पाठः (recite and record).** Record yourself reciting both rows with
manner labels: क ख ग घ ङ, then च छ ज झ ञ — three rounds each, tissue in hand on round two.

hear the columns hold.

**अभ्यासः 2 · श्रुतलेखनम् (dictation).** Have a partner read the eight readable words of

(`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l02-word-list.mp3`), pausing after each word's first

**अभ्यासः 3 · श्रवणभेदः (which one is Sanskrit? listening).** A partner — ideally a Hindi
speaker, who owns both sounds — says *jala* six times in random mixture: sometimes with
the full affricate ज, sometimes with the loan-sound [z] as in ज़. For each, write S
(Sanskrit ज) or H (the Hindi loan habit). Then two rounds of *cala*: once flat-lipped and
bare, once with English-style rounding and puff — which is the Sanskrit च? 6/8 is a pass.
No partner? The drill audio's final band plays the contrasts 🔊.

**अभ्यासः 4 · लेखनम् (tracing and writing).** Print or open the tracing worksheet ✍

each following the stroke arrows, then the ज/ञ contrast row slowly, saying the chant.
Final row: write जल, छल, चल, जलज, चलति unaided.

**अभ्यासः 5 · क्रिया-अन्वेषणम् (verb hunt).** Below are twelve readable words. Exactly two
of them are verbs — words that *happen*. Circle both, read them aloud, and say what each
means:

> जल · चल · गीत · कूजति · मुख · जित · चलति · खग · छल · जलज · मेघ · अचल

Bonus: what do the two verbs share at their tails? (Notice it, enjoy it, and file it for
Volume 2.)

**अभ्यासः 6 · वाक्य-रचना (sentence assembly, tiles).** Make word tiles: खग, कोकिलः, जल,

अत्र अस्ति। Then swap subjects: can the bird coo? Can the cuckoo move? (Yes and yes — say
them.) Write down the two sequences you like best, using ear-spelling only for the 🔊
tiles if you attempt them, and read your favorites to someone.

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**This is the transfer lesson — stage it as one.** Before showing a single new letter,
draw the 5×5 grid with only the क-row filled, point at the empty second row, and ask the
class to *predict* its five slots. They can, and they should discover that they can — that
prediction is objective 4 happening live, and it converts the lesson from "five more
letters" into "the system works". Only then reveal च छ ज झ ञ landing in the predicted
slots.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → grid prediction + hook: the verb
promise (5) → affricate work: slow-motion release, क/च contrast, tissue transfer (8) →
five cards + board grid row 2 + ज्ञ aside (10) → syllable grid and word list in pairs (8) →
exercises 3 and 6 (6) → homework briefing incl. the 1-day re-dictation (3).

**Board work:** the 5×5 grid lives on the side board all chapter; fill row 2 today in
front of the class, slot by slot, saying the manner labels aloud each time. Under the
grid keep a two-word "verb shelf": चलति, कूजति — students will glance at it all week, and
its -ति rhyme plants Volume 2 without a word of grammar.

**The ज़ conversation needs respect, not correction.** Hindi speakers in the room own both
sounds; the dot is *right* in Hindi. Frame the skill as code-switching, never as fixing an
error, and hand the demonstration to them: a Hindi speaker alternating जल/ज़ल for the class
is the best discrimination model available (exercise 3), and it makes the expert visible.
English speakers meanwhile fight the mirror-image battle: their *ch* is a rounded, puffed
छ-alike, so drill lips-flat च against चल/छल pairs and let the tissue referee.

**The rare letters:** झ and ञ trigger the "do I really need this?" face. Answer honestly —
few word-initial jobs now, real jobs coming (झ word-internally, ञ inside conjuncts from
Chapter 5 on) — and then drill them with full seriousness anyway; the equal-card policy is
the course's promise. The nose-pinch check for ञ gets a laugh and works.

**Dictation logistics:** read each word twice at natural speed, 10-second writing gap.
Collect error types, not just scores: च/छ slot errors, ज/ञ shape swaps, and any ज़-tinted
reading aloud each want different remediation (tissue drill, chant + tracing row, and the
जल seal-check respectively).

**What students find hard:** believing that च differs from English *ch* until the mirror
shows them the rounding; keeping छ and च apart under speed (normalize the 1-day retest —
contrasts settle after sleep); the standalone ञ shape (the "lies low" chant sticks fastest
when students say it while writing); and resisting the urge to ask about verb endings —
promise them Volume 2 and enjoy that they asked.

- **04:30** kokilaḥ kūjati [koːkilɐh kuːd͡ʑɐt̪i]. The cuckoo sings. Every letter, every
