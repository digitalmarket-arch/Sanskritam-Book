## 1. Hook

Four rows of the grid have hidden their work behind your teeth. The last row is the one you
can *see*: lips doing everything, in plain view — put a mirror up and Sanskrit happens on
your face. And the moment this row clicks into place, something bigger happens than five
new sounds: the grid you first saw in Chapter 1 is complete, and with it come the first
full Sanskrit sentences you read entirely by yourself — no glue words, no audio crutches.
A boy walks. A monkey walks. A cuckoo sings. Small sentences — and every word, yours.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — the tour ends where the course began.

In Chapter 1's sound map you walked the tongue back to front — क च ट त प — and the walk
ended on the lips. The tradition's ordering of the vargas takes the same path, from the
throat outward: ओष्ठ्य (oṣṭhya) "of the lips" is its label for today's row, the last stop
on the road. And waiting at that stop is an old friend: म, one of the first two consonants
you ever wrote (v1.c01.l03), a helper since the day you learned अ. It served three chapters
without a home row; today the tour ends by giving it one — the final cell of the grid. One
more homecoming rides along: the closing [m] you have been reading in ॐ [oːm] since
Chapter 2 gets its letter-home today too.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** Hindi's [f] arrived with Perso-Arabic loanwords: the nuqta
> letters क़ ख़ ग़ ज़ फ़ were added to Devanagari precisely to write such borrowed sounds.
> Sanskrit's sound inventory was fixed long before that contact — it has no [f] at all,
> and फ never wears a nuqta on a Sanskrit page. [REF:masica1991], [REF:ohala1994]

Keep that history in your pocket: it is why today's most important drill exists (§9), and
why the letter फ asks a small act of un-learning from nearly everyone.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — the row your mirror can check.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The प-वर्ग is bilabial: both lips make the closure, and the tongue
> takes the day off. The five members are /p pʰ b bʱ m/ — voiceless, voiceless aspirated,
> voiced, voiced breathy, nasal: the same five manner slots as every row before it.
> [REF:ladefoged2015]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** फ is [pʰ] — a p released with a puff of voiceless breath from the
> *lips*. [f] is a different gesture entirely: labiodental friction, the lower lip against
> the upper teeth. Sanskrit has no [f]. The tissue test tells them apart instantly: फ
> throws the tissue with one burst from a full lip-closure; [f] makes a long hiss and the
> tissue only trembles. [REF:ladefoged2015], [REF:ohala1994]

Three consequences. **This is the fifth run of the manner row** — by now it is machinery,
not memory: only the place is new, and the place is "both lips". Expect the fastest
sound-day of the chapter; that speed is the varga system working as designed. **The row is
fully visible** — every previous articulation hid inside the mouth; a bilabial closure
happens on your face, so the mirror is a genuine measuring instrument today (§11). **The
physics transfers untouched:** aspiration is a voiceless puff after release, breathy voice
is murmured voicing through it — the tissue-and-throat checks you calibrated on ख/घ and
थ/ध run on फ/भ without adjustment.

## 8. English Explanation

The last row, and then the payoff: five sounds first — the fifth run of a row you know by
heart — then the sentences the whole chapter has been building toward.

### The five cards — equal citizens, all workhorses

- **प (pa) /p/ — voiceless.** Lips close, release cleanly, *no puff*. English "s**p**in"
  has it; "**p**in" does not (that p is aspirated — it is फ's sound). Hindi प is exact.
- **फ (pha) /pʰ/ — voiceless aspirated.** प plus a burst of breath from the lips — this
  row's tissue-thrower. Anchor: English "**p**in"; trap: English "**ph**one" (§12). Hindi
  anchor: फल said *without* the borrowed [f] habit.
- **ब (ba) /b/ — voiced.** Lips closed, voice already buzzing. English b is close, though
  its buzz often starts late — the throat-hand should feel voicing through the whole
  closure. Hindi ब is exact.
- **भ (bha) /bʱ/ — voiced breathy.** ब released through murmured, breathy voice — warm air
  on the palm *while* the throat buzzes. No English anchor exists; Hindi भ is exact.
- **म (ma) /m/ — nasal.** Lips closed, air through the nose. English and Hindi m are
  genuinely the same sound — the row's one free gift, and your oldest letter, home at last.

For once, no honesty-footnote about rare letters: unlike ङ and ञ, every member of this row
is a high-frequency workhorse. Five cards, five careers.

### Worked readings

- **फल** = फ + ल → pha-la — /pʰɐlɐ/ "fruit; result". The puff, then a full final /ɐ/.
- **पिता** = पि + ता → pi-tā — /pit̪ɑː/ "father". The left-standing ि — written before प,
  spoken after it.
- **बालक** = बा + ल + क → bā-la-ka — /bɑːlɐkɐ/ "boy". Three full akṣaras, no shortcuts.
- **भूमि** = भू + मि → bhū-mi — /bʱuːmi/ "earth". The below-hook ू riding भ.
- **गोपाल** = गो + पा + ल → go-pā-la — /goːpɑːlɐ/ "cowherd". A velar, a labial and ल
  cooperating — a word only a finished grid can spell.

### The milestone — the first sentences you read yourself

One small sign makes it possible. In v1.c02.l06 you met the visarga as the "dictionary
coat" — मतिः. That same ः rides the *subject* of a Sanskrit sentence: बालक steps into a
sentence as बालकः. Why subjects wear ः is Volume 3's story; today, read it and enjoy it.
One honest convention first:

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** In connected speech Sanskrit applies sandhi — regular sound
> adjustments where words meet; that system is Volume 3's subject. At this stage the
> course reads sentences **word by word, in pause form**: each word spoken fully and
> separately, exactly as in a word list — the traditional style for reciting word lists
> and dictation. Nothing read this way will need to be unlearned later.

Now — the three sentences, built from parts you own: बालकः and कपिः from today, कोकिलः
from v1.c02.l05, चलति and कूजति from v1.c03.l02.

**बालकः चलति।**
(bālakaḥ calati.) "The boy walks." — बालक चलता है।

**कपिः चलति।**
(kapiḥ calati.) "The monkey walks." — बन्दर चलता है।

**कोकिलः कूजति।**
(kokilaḥ kūjati.) "The cuckoo coos." — कोयल कूकती है।

Read them slowly: word fully, tiny pause, next word, and the daṇḍa । to close. Every
akṣara, mātrā and rider on those lines is something you learned on purpose, one lesson at
a time. This is the course's first true reading — not letters, not lists: Sanskrit saying
something, to you, through your own eyes.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — four drills, in order. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l05-varga-drill.mp3` (drills 1–2 + pair test),
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l05-word-list.mp3` (drill 3 + dictation track),
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l05-sentences.mp3` (drill 4).

**Drill 1 — the row recital, fifth edition.** Tissue before the lips, hand on the throat,
mirror up: प — फ — ब — भ — म. Checks: प barely stirs the tissue; फ throws it; ब buzzes the
throat, tissue calm; भ buzzes *and* flutters warmly; म buzzes the nose (fingertip on its
side to confirm). Five slow rounds, then the cumulative chant — all five rows, क-वर्ग to
प-वर्ग, one breath per row. The whole grid now lives in your mouth.

**Drill 2 — the फ laboratory.** (1) Say English *fun* and freeze the first sound: upper
teeth on lower lip, a long hiss — that is [f], today's forbidden gesture. (2) Now फ: both
lips sealed, pressure, release — one burst, tissue flies, teeth touching nothing. (3)
Alternate at the mirror: [f] — फ — [f] — फ, watching the lower lip. (4) Words: फल, फेन —
puff first, full final vowel after.

**Drill 3 — syllables, then words.** Read across, then down, one beat per short vowel, two
per long: प पा पि पू · फ फा फे फू · ब बा बि बू · भ भा भू भो · म मा मि मू. Then the §14
list from the audio, each word twice. Close with the discrimination sets: प-फ, पा-फा
(aspiration); ब-भ, बा-भा for breath; and the length pair बल — बाल, one beat apart.

**Drill 4 — the sentence protocol.** With the sentences audio: **बालकः चलति। कपिः चलति।
कोकिलः कूजति।** The rule of this stage: *say each word completely — final vowel, final
breath and all — take a tiny pause, then the next word.* Three rounds: block by block;
word by word; then at a calm reading pace, pause form intact. This protocol is how all
Volume 1 sentences will be read.

## 10. IPA

The new row completes the stop chart: प /p/, फ /pʰ/, ब /b/, भ /bʱ/, म /m/.

**The two superscripts, one last time:** ʰ marks voiceless aspiration (फ /pʰ/ — a breath
puff, vocal folds open); ʱ marks breathy voice (भ /bʱ/ — murmured voicing through the
release). You have now met each five times — the notation should read like an instruction.

**The trap has a symbol too:** the sound of English *fun* and Hindi फ़ is [f], a voiceless
labiodental fricative — it appears in this course only inside brackets, as an error to
avoid; no Sanskrit phoneme corresponds to it.

**Narrow note — the sentences:** बालकः चलति in careful pause-form reading is
[bɑːlɐkɐh t͡ɕɐlɐt̪i] — the visarga a clean voiceless breath, the pause a genuine brief
silence. Word-final IPA keeps the full [ɐ] everywhere: /pʰɐlɐ/, never [pʰɐl].

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — for the first time, the whole row is on camera. Mirror up; the card sheet
(`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c03l05-pa-varga-cards.svg`) shows the shared closure
and the five releases.

1. **The shared start:** all five begin identically — lips sealed, teeth apart behind
   them, tongue at rest. In the mirror this is one and the same face for प फ ब भ म.
2. **The releases differ:** silent seal → clean pop = प; silent seal → pop + burst = फ;
   buzzing seal → pop = ब; buzzing seal → pop through murmur = भ; buzzing seal that never
   pops, air through the nose = म.
3. **The [f] guard:** at no point today do the upper teeth touch the lower lip — watch the
   lower lip through every drill; it meets only the other lip.
4. **The instrument panel:** tissue (flies on फ, flutters on भ), throat-hand (silent for
   प फ, buzzing for ब भ म), nose-fingertip (buzzing only on म). Three touches, five
   sounds, zero guesswork.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **फ as [f] — everyone's mistake.** English readers see IAST *pha* and think "phone";
   Hindi speakers carry the casual फ/फ़ merger into Sanskrit. Both produce a labiodental
   hiss where Sanskrit wants a bilabial burst. *Fix:* the फ laboratory (§9 drill 2) —
   lips sealed, teeth exiled, tissue must *jump*, not tremble; re-test with फल and फेन
   daily for a week.
2. **भ under-breathed into ब (mostly English speakers).** English has no breathy stops, so
   भाग slides toward बाग and a meaning-bearing contrast collapses. *Fix:* palm before the
   lips — भ must land warm air while the throat buzzes; drill ब-भ, बा-भा until the palm
   can referee blind.
3. **प aspirated by English habit.** English aspirates initial voiceless stops — "pin"
   begins with [pʰ] — so an English-habit प becomes फ and पल becomes फल. *Fix:* steal the
   *spin*-p; the tissue before प must barely stir.
4. **The ब/व blur (preview).** Next chapter brings व /ʋ/, and speakers of Hindi (where the
   b/v line runs looser in places) sometimes let ब soften early. *Fix:* today ब is full
   lip-against-lip closure, always. Build ब clean now and व will land beside it, not on
   top of it.

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — four small stories the word list is hiding.

### पिता — the family pattern completes

**पिता** (pitā) "father" — पिता · /pit̪ɑː/

In Chapter 1 you read माता; in v1.c02.l03 you met her dictionary form मातृ and the
stem-vs-sentence idea. Today the pattern pays off a second time: the dictionary lists
**पितृ** (pitṛ), and in a sentence the father appears as पिता — the मातृ/माता
relationship, learned once, applied free. The family receipt travels further: पितृ/पिता
stands beside English *father*, Latin *pater* — the same cognate family story as
माता/*mother* [REF:mallory2006].

### पथ and path — ancient kin

**पथ** (patha) "path" — पथ, रास्ता · /pɐt̪ʰɐ/

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit पथ and English *path* are genuine ancient kin within the
> Indo-European family — parallel inheritances, not a borrowing in either direction.
> [REF:mallory2006]

Feel the dental थ as you say it — tongue on the teeth, then a full final vowel: *patha*.

### गोपाल — a compound you can see through

**गोपाल** (gopāla) "cowherd" — ग्वाला, गोपाल · /goːpɑːlɐ/

गो "cow" + पाल "protector": a word that is a picture of its meaning — your first fully
transparent compound fused into a single dictionary word [REF:apte1890 s.v. गोपाल].

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** गोपाल "protector of cows" is among the beloved names of
> Kṛṣṇa, the divine cowherd of the devotional tradition — a name still sung today. In this
> lesson it is vocabulary, doing honest secular work.

### फल — fruit, and the fruit of actions

**फल** (phala) "fruit; result" — फल, परिणाम · /pʰɐlɐ/

One word, two senses Sanskrit refuses to separate: the fruit on the branch and the fruit
*of* something — outcome, result [REF:apte1890 s.v. फल]. English does it too ("the fruits
of your labor"). When, volumes from now, you meet the tradition's discussions of the fruit
of action (karma-phala), it will be this everyday word doing the philosophical lifting.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule**: three new letters, and the grid's
completion certificate.

### Stroke order — फ, ब, भ

Course convention as ever — body first, stem next, headline last; the stroke card
(`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c03l05-stroke-order.svg`) is the authority, and
traditions vary in details.

- **प** (from Chapter 1's tongue-walk, now official) — the body: one curve starting below
  headline height, sweeping down and right into the stem-line; then stem; headline last.
- **फ** — a complete प, then the breath-curl: a small open loop leaving the stem's right
  side. As with आ and ऊ, *more sound is drawn as more ink*. Never add a dot below: फ with
  a nuqta is Hindi's फ़ [f], not a Sanskrit letter (§4).
- **ब** — the body: a closed loop on the left, down, around, and back up to join the
  stem-line *cleanly*; the small diagonal tick inside the loop is what will separate ब
  from next chapter's व; then stem, headline. **ब vs क, the loop logic:** क's bowl crosses
  back through its own stroke and ties a *knot*; ब's loop joins without crossing — a
  closed room, not a knot. If your pen crossed itself, you wrote toward क.
- **भ** — the body: from under the headline at the left, a curve down and leftward, then
  along the base and up into the stem; then stem, headline. **भ vs म:** म ties a small
  closed loop at its lower left; भ keeps that corner a smooth open curve — loop = म, no
  loop = भ. Fonts vary; the stroke card is the model.

   चलता है।
2. कपिः चलति!
   (kapiḥ calati!) "A monkey comes walking!" — एक बन्दर चला आता है!

your sentences. Six lines of Sanskrit, a complete tiny drama, read by you alone — the
first of many.

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**Two summits, in order.** First the row (fast — the fifth run of known machinery), then
the milestone. Do not let the sentences leak into the sound work early; their impact
depends on the grid feeling *finished* first.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → hook + mirror: one closure, five
releases (5) → sound work: tissue row, फ laboratory, ब/भ palm pairs (8) → writing फ ब भ:
board demo, air-writing, tracing start (8) → word list in pairs (6) → THE MILESTONE:
build बालकः on the board, state the pause-form convention, choral then solo reading (6) →
exercises 2 and 6 (4) → homework briefing incl. the 1-day re-dictation (3).

**Stage the milestone deliberately.** Write बालकः चलति। on the board in silence. Wait.
Someone will read it — and the room will realize what just happened. Then name it: the
first complete Sanskrit sentence anyone in this room has read unaided. Choral reading
twice, then every student reads one sentence solo, pause form. Do not rush these five
minutes; they are the chapter's payday.

**The फ battle is everyone's battle — through two doors.** English-habit learners arrive
via the "ph = f" spelling reflex; Hindi-literate learners via the casual फ/फ़ merger. The
tissue referees both: [f] can hiss all day without moving it; a true [pʰ] cracks it like a
flag. Run the deliberate-error alternation ([f]–फ–[f]–फ) as a class, laughing allowed.

**Differentiation:** Hindi-literate learners breeze through the glyphs — load them with
nuqta discipline, full final vowels, and the recorder role in pair work. English-habit
learners need the *spin*-trick for प and palm work for भ; pair them with Hindi-speaking
partners for the ब/भ drill. Heritage learners who chant at home may read the sentences
fluently in connected style — honor it, then ask for pause form explicitly: "dictation
style today; the flowing style gets its rules in Volume 3."

**What students find hard:** believing फ is not [f] (the tissue converts them; argument
does not); keeping भ's breath *voiced* (whispered भ is just फ — the throat-hand catches
it); and slowing down for pause form once excitement hits. The tile exercise (§18.5)
channels that excitement into two more correct sentences instead of speed.
