## 1. Hook

One of today's five letters has been working for you since Chapter 1. त built माता, carried
ततः through every story, stood inside तिल and तुला — always with the modest job title
"helper consonant", always with a promise attached: *the full dental family arrives in
Chapter 3.* Today the promise comes due. त walks home to its row, brings four siblings —
थ द ध न — and hands you a test you can do with one finger: touch a fingertip to your upper
front teeth and say त. If your tongue arrives on the fingertip, you have just made a sound
that no ordinary English *t* has ever been. And waiting at the end of the lesson: कथा, the
story-word itself, and नमः — the first half of नमस्ते, readable at last.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — the row the tradition named after the teeth.

Walk the tongue walk from the sound map (v1.c01.l02) and you arrive at station four:
कण्ठ्य → तालव्य → मूर्धन्य → **दन्त्य** (dantya) "of the teeth". The name is not a
metaphor; it is an address, taken from दन्त (danta) "tooth" — दाँत. (The written form of
दन्त hides a joined न्त that waits for Chapter 5; the sound of it you can already say.)

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The Pāṇinīya Śikṣā's tour of the mouth assigns each varga a
> station from the throat forward; the fourth is named from the teeth themselves. It is a
> physical claim about where the tongue goes — and modern articulatory phonetics signs off
> on it without edits.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** The mismatch with English is not a modern discovery: Whitney's
> 1889 grammar, describing the Sanskrit dental series, already warns English readers that
> their own t and d — made behind the teeth, not on them — are not the same sounds.
> [REF:whitney1889 §§47–48]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** True dentals are the world's common default: Spanish, Italian, French,
> Russian and Hindi all make their t-sounds at or on the teeth. English, with its alveolar
> habit, is the outlier here — Sanskrit sides with the majority. [REF:ladefoged2015]

So if you speak Hindi, or have Spanish or Italian in your ear, today is a homecoming twice
over. If English is your only anchor, today is the day your *t* finally moves house.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — one new address, zero new manners.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The त-वर्ग is the dental row: /t̪ t̪ʰ d̪ d̪ʱ n̪/ — a complete closure
> made with the tip of the tongue against the upper front teeth (touching them, even
> slightly between them), released into the vowel. The little bridge under each IPA symbol
> means exactly "at the teeth". [REF:ladefoged2015]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** English t, d and n are alveolar: the tongue lands on the gum ridge
> *behind* the teeth and never touches the teeth themselves. To ears trained on a
> Sanskrit-type system, English t and d actually sound closer to the retroflex row — which
> is why English loanwords land in Hindi spelling with ट and ड (टिकट, डॉक्टर), never with
> त and द. [REF:ladefoged2015] [REF:masica1991]

Read that second block twice, because it deletes a comfortable assumption: your English *t*
is not "basically त with an accent". It is closer to *yesterday's* letter than to today's.
That is exactly why this lesson exists as the chapter's contrast lesson — three addresses
now live on one small strip of your mouth's roof, and your tongue must learn to feel them
apart: the **teeth** (त, today), the **ridge** (English habit), the **curl** behind it
(ट, yesterday). The three-address drill in §9 makes this a physical skill, not an idea.

The manners, meanwhile, cost you nothing. The row runs the same five slots you have now
played three times — voiceless, voiceless aspirated, voiced, voiced breathy, nasal — so the
tissue test, the throat-hand and the nose-hum all transfer unchanged [REF:whitney1889
§§39–50]. One warning, though, for readers of English spelling: nothing in this row is a
fricative. The *th* of *thin* and the *th* of *the* both let air hiss continuously through
a narrow gap. थ and ध have no hiss anywhere: full silent closure, clean release — then
breath (थ) or murmur (ध). And the contrast you are training is not cosmetic: त/ट and द/ड
keep real words apart, so a drifted tongue changes meanings, not just accents
[REF:masica1991].

"row, class" and दन्त्य (dantya) "dental" from the sound map, and इति keeps its quoting
job whenever we name a sound.

## 8. English Explanation

Yesterday the tongue curled back; today it slides all the way forward and lands on the
teeth. Same five-slot row, fourth address on the map — and one member of the family has
been living in your notebook for two chapters already.

### The homecoming of त

Since v1.c01.l04, त has been a "helper consonant": glyph and sound only, on loan so that
vowels and mātrās could be practiced in real words. Look at what it built while it waited —
माता, लता, ताल, तिल, तुला, इति, गति, गीत, ततः, अतः, इतः, कुतः, मतिः, and yesterday's तट.
Nothing needs to be unlearned today: every audio model you have echoed already said /t̪/,
tongue on teeth. What त gets today is its family, its full letter card, and its formal
name: first member of the दन्त्य row.

### The five cards — equal members, equal depth

| Letter | IAST | IPA | Manner slot | The one thing to hold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| त | ta | /t̪ɐ/ | voiceless | Spanish/Italian-style t ON the teeth — not English *t* |
| थ | tha | /t̪ʰɐ/ | voiceless aspirated | त + the tissue-throwing puff — never the *th* of *thin* |
| द | da | /d̪ɐ/ | voiced | full closure, throat buzzing — never the *th* of *the* |
| ध | dha | /d̪ʱɐ/ | voiced breathy | द + murmur; watch its page-twin घ (§15) |
| न | na | /n̪ɐ/ | nasal | the hum with the tongue on the teeth — vs ण's curl |

No honesty-asterisks today: unlike ङ ञ or ठ ढ, all five of these letters will be in your
words constantly from here on. This is the busiest kind of row there is.

### The payoff — reading, worked out

- **कथा** = क + था → ka-thā "story" — the ā-sign riding a brand-new letter
- **नदी** = न + दी → na-dī "river"
- **मधु** = म + धु → ma-dhu "honey" — Chapter 2's below-hook on ध
- **नमः** = न + मः → na-ma-ḥ "homage" — the visarga riding the finished म, exactly as
  v1.c02.l06 taught

### The नमः moment

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** नमस्ते (namaste) is two words joined: नमः (namaḥ) "homage, a bow" +
> ते (te) "to you". When they join, the ः changes shape by a sound-joining rule — sandhi,
> Volume 3's story. [REF:apte1890 s.v. नमस्]

You can now read नमः. You can even read ते on its own — त wearing lesson v1.c02.l04's
e-sign. What you cannot yet read is the way स् and ते fuse on the page in नमस्ते: the
letter स arrives in Chapter 4, and Chapter 5 teaches the joined writing of स्ते. The
promise made in the first lesson of this course — *one day you will read नमस्ते* — is now
exactly two chapters from kept.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — three drills, in order. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l04-varga-drill.mp3` (drills 1–2) and
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l04-word-list.mp3` (drill 3).

**Drill 1 — the row recital, checks attached.** Fingertip on the biting edge of the upper
front teeth. Speak the row slowly: त — थ — द — ध — न. The tongue must touch the fingertip
on all five. Now add the manner checks you own from three vargas: tissue before the lips —
it barely stirs on त and *jumps* on थ; hand on throat — silent for त थ, buzzing from द
onward; on ध the buzz comes wrapped in heavy warm air; on न the nose hums. Then chain
yesterday into today, same tune, new address: ट ठ ड ढ ण → त थ द ध न. Three rounds.

**Drill 2 — the syllable grid.** Read across, then down; every syllable one clean beat,
tongue touching the teeth at the start of each:

| त /t̪ɐ/ | ता /t̪ɑː/ | ती /t̪iː/ |
|---|---|---|
| थ /t̪ʰɐ/ | था /t̪ʰɑː/ | थी /t̪ʰiː/ |
| द /d̪ɐ/ | दा /d̪ɑː/ | दी /d̪iː/ |
| ध /d̪ʱɐ/ | धा /d̪ʱɑː/ | धी /d̪ʱiː/ |
| न /n̪ɐ/ | ना /n̪ɑː/ | नी /n̪iː/ |

Then walk one member across the whole mātrā compass — द दा दि दी दु दू दे दै दो दौ दं दः —
and finish by assembling words from §14: कथा, नदी, मधु, धन, दिन, दान, नमः.

**Drill 3 — the three-address ladder (the heart of the lesson).** One manner, three
addresses, one breath. Round 1: say English *ta* (as in "tar") and *feel* the tongue land
on the ridge; then टा — curl it back; then ता — slide it onto the teeth, fingertip
confirming. Round 2: English *da* → डा → दा. Round 3: English *na* → णा → ना. Three slow
rounds per family. This ladder is the lesson: do it once per day all week, and the three
addresses stop being ideas and become places you can find in the dark.

## 10. IPA

The new row: **/t̪ t̪ʰ d̪ d̪ʱ n̪/** — five symbols, one diacritic doing all the teaching.
The little bridge under each letter is the IPA's dental mark: "made at the teeth". Now read
the chapter so far straight off the symbols: plain t d n with no mark — alveolar, the
English default; ʈ ɖ ɳ with the retroflex tail — yesterday's curl; t̪ d̪ n̪ with the
bridge — today. Three addresses, three spellings, no ambiguity.

**Narrow notes.** The manner marks carry over unchanged: superscript /ʰ/ is the voiceless
puff of थ; /ʱ/ is the breathy murmur of ध — voicing continues *through* the breath. And a
warning bracket for English eyes: the *th* sounds of *thin* and *the* are the fricatives
[θ] and [ð] — continuous hiss, no closure. They appear nowhere in this row, and nowhere in
Sanskrit at all: थ is /t̪ʰ/, a stop; द is /d̪/, a stop.

Phonemic weight, as always in slashes: /t̪/ vs /ʈ/ and /d̪/ vs /ɖ/ are separate phonemes —
categories that keep words apart — which is why §18's ear test exists.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — walkthrough of the diagram (planned:
`assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c03l04-dental-vs-retroflex.svg` — two sagittal panels: tongue
tip on the teeth for त, curled to the roof for ट, with the English ridge contact dotted in
between for contrast).

1. **Anchor:** rest a clean fingertip flat against the biting edge of your upper front
   teeth.
2. **Say त.** The tongue tip must arrive at the teeth — you feel it graze the fingertip.
   If the tip peeks slightly *between* the teeth, that is allowed and even helpful while
   you learn: it guarantees true tooth contact.
3. **Say English *t*** (as in "tea"). Nothing reaches the fingertip; the tongue lands on
   the ridge above and behind it. Feel the gap between the two habits — that gap is the
   lesson.
4. **Say ट.** The tip lifts away, curling up and back — farther from the fingertip still.

**Mirror check:** for त and न, a mirror shows the tongue tip right at the tooth line
(perhaps just visible between the teeth). English t and ट show nothing at all — the mouth
keeps its secret. **Parking exercise:** न is the row's only holdable sound. Hum a long न
and *keep* the tongue parked on the teeth for the whole hum; if it drifts back to the
ridge, the hum changes color. Park, hold, listen.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **Alveolar backsliding (English speakers — the big one).** The first careful त is
   perfect; by word three of a list, the tongue slinks home to the ridge. To a
   varga-trained ear the drifted sound leans toward the ट-row — a different row, different
   words. *Fix:* fingertip check at the start of every drill, and say the first syllable of
   every word slowly. Speed is earned, never assumed.
2. **थ as the *th* of *thin*.** English spelling habits turn *tha* into a hiss. थ is a
   stop: complete silent closure on the teeth, clean release, *then* the puff. *Fix:*
   tissue before the lips — it must jump once, not flutter continuously.
3. **द or ध as the *th* of *the*.** Same spelling trap, voiced: [ð] leaks air through a
   gap. द seals completely — throat buzzing behind a closed door. *Fix:* no air during the
   hold; for ध, murmur *after* the release: buzz throughout, warm air on the palm.
4. **The न/ण collapse (Hindi speakers most of all).** Everyday Hindi lets ण relax into न
   (गुण said as "गुन"), and dictation shows the reverse too — न written where ण belongs.
   *Fix:* the pair drill ना — णा with fingertip: touch = न, no-touch-and-curl = ण. In
   dictation ask of every n-sound: did it bend backward?
5. **Final-vowel deletion (Hindi speakers).** दिन and धन are daily Hindi words, so the
   habit is strongest exactly here: "din", "dhan". Sanskrit gives every अ its beat —
   di-na, dha-na, ga-ga-na. *Fix:* finger-count the syllables; the word-list audio holds
   every final /ɐ/ audibly.

> Proto-Indo-European word for the honey-drink. Cognates, not loans — no word was borrowed;

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule** section: the grid grows a row, four new
letters learn to stand on the line, and a promised lookalike gets settled.

### The grid — row four filled

| वर्ग | Place | Voiceless | + breath | Voiced | + murmur | Nasal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| क-वर्ग | velar | क k | ख kh | ग g | घ gh | ङ ṅ |
| च-वर्ग | palatal | च c | छ ch | ज j | झ jh | ञ ñ |
| ट-वर्ग | retroflex | ट ṭ | ठ ṭh | ड ḍ | ढ ḍh | ण ṇ |
| **त-वर्ग** | **dental** | **त t** | **थ th** | **द d** | **ध dh** | **न n** |
| प-वर्ग | labial | — | — | lesson 5 | — | — |

Twenty slots owned, five to go. The manner columns have not changed since the क-row — that
is the chapter's engine working as designed.

### Stroke order — थ, द, ध, न (and त, at home)

One common convention, described here; teaching traditions vary in details, and the stroke
card is the authority. (Card: `planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c03l04-stroke-order.svg`;
tracing: `planned:assets/worksheets/volume-1/v1c03l04-tracing.pdf`.)

- **त** — as you have written it since Chapter 1: the left curve, then the stem, headline
  last. Nothing changes today but its title.
- **थ** — the body in one motion: a small closed loop at the top left, opening into the
  rounded bowl that sweeps down and rises to the stem-line; then the stem; headline last.
- **द** — the row's *stemless* letter, like yesterday's ड and ढ: one stroke — the small
  beak at the top, then the deep belly curving down and left, hooking back beneath itself;
  its short headline last.
- **ध** — write द's beak and belly, but let the belly's end climb into a full vertical
  stem; full headline last. ध is द given a roof and a spine.
- **न** — the short hook at the upper left, then the sweep right into the foot of the
  stem; stem; headline last.

### ध vs घ — the promised lookalike, settled

The क-row lesson flagged that घ would one day meet a near-twin. Here it is. Side by side —
घ ध — the cues to check, pen in hand: **ध wears द's beak** at its top left; घ has no beak.
**घ's left arm hangs from the headline itself** and closes a loop at the base; ध's bowl
floats free below the headline, open air at its top left. And the ear settles what the eye
debates: both are breathy voiced stops, but घ murmurs at the back of the mouth, ध at the
teeth. The stroke card shows the pair enlarged; the tracing sheet makes you write them in
alternation until the hand stops asking.

## 17. Story

कथा — कथा! · A Story, At Last. Micro-fiction by the river, built from §14 plus the
established cast. Bold = readable; glue words are glossed 🔊 below. Each line: Devanagari,
IAST, English, Hindi.

1. **माता** अत्र अस्ति। **नदी** अत्र अस्ति।
   (mātā atra asti. nadī atra asti.) "Mother is here. The river is here." —
   माँ यहाँ है। नदी यहाँ है।
2. **कमला** — **नमः**! **नमः**!
   (kamalā — namaḥ! namaḥ!) "Kamalā: Greetings, greetings!" — कमला — नमस्ते, नमस्ते!
3. **माता** — आम्, **नमः**!
   (mātā — ām, namaḥ!) "Mother: Yes — greetings!" — माँ — हाँ, नमस्ते!
4. **कमला** — **कथा**? **कथा**?
   (kathā? kathā?) "Kamalā: A story? A story?" — कमला — कहानी? कहानी?
5. **माता** — आम्, **कथा**। "**उलूकः** अत्र अस्ति। **मधु** अत्र अस्ति।"
   (mātā — ām, kathā. "ulūkaḥ atra asti. madhu atra asti.") "Mother: Yes — a story. 'An
   owl is here. Honey is here.'" — माँ — हाँ, कहानी। "एक उल्लू है। शहद है।"
6. **कमला** — **ततः**? **ततः**?
   (kamalā — tataḥ? tataḥ?) "Kamalā: Then? Then?" — कमला — फिर? फिर?

Glue words 🔊, all established: अत्र (atra) "here" — यहाँ; अस्ति (asti) "is" — है; आम्
(ām) "yes" — हाँ. Everything else is yours — including **उलूकः** whole: in Chapter 2 the
owl's tail ः needed the audio's help; since lesson 6 it is fully yours. Growth, measured in
one word. Mother's story opens the way every story you have ever read here opens — अत्र
अस्ति — and the owl in it is Chapter 2's owl, hopping from your vocabulary into a story
*inside* a story. And yes, enjoy the joke: after fifteen lessons of stories filed under a
heading you could not read, your first fully-owned story-word arrives in a story about
*asking for* a story — and Kamalā's ततः? ततः? is every learner at the end of every lesson.
The telling pauses here; lesson 5 brings the words that let it move.

## 18. Exercises

अभ्यासाः — six exercises; 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6 are production tasks. Do them in order.

**अभ्यासः 1 · वर्गपाठः (row recital, record).** Record yourself: the row त थ द ध न three
times slowly with the checks running — fingertip on the teeth, tissue, throat-hand,
nose-hum — then the four-row chain from memory: क-row, च-row, ट-row, त-row. Compare
against 🔊 `planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l04-varga-drill.mp3`. Written checklist:
did the tongue touch the fingertip on all five? Did the tissue jump only on थ (and flutter
after ध's release)? Did the nose hum only on न?

**अभ्यासः 2 · श्रवणभेदः (contrast test, written answers).** Play 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c03l04-contrast-test.mp3`: sixteen items, each a single
syllable — ता or टा, ती or टी, दा or डा, दी or डी, ना or णा — in random order. Write each
in Devanagari. This is the chapter's hardest ear test: 12/16 passes today. Retest
tomorrow regardless — contrast hearing improves sharply after sleep.

**अभ्यासः 3 · श्रुतलेखनम् (dictation).** Have a partner read ten of the fourteen §14 words
in random order, or use the word-list audio 🔊 with pauses. Write each in Devanagari.
Self-check three questions per word: right family — त-row or ट-row? Right breath letter —
द or ध? Final अ heard *and* written? 8/10 is a pass; wrong words open tomorrow's warm-up.

**अभ्यासः 4 · लेखनम् (tracing & writing).** Print or open ✍
`planned:assets/worksheets/volume-1/v1c03l04-tracing.pdf`: trace थ द ध न five times each

the blanks: __न "wealth", मे__ "cloud" (one takes ध, one takes घ). Final row: write कथा,
नदी, मधु, नमः, दधि unaided.

**अभ्यासः 5 · स्थानत्रयाभ्यासः (three-address drill, spoken + written).** Run §9 drill 3 —
English sound → retroflex → dental, all three families — three full rounds at a mirror
with the fingertip anchor. Then write three honest sentences in your notebook, one per
address, recording what your fingertip and mirror actually reported (not what the textbook
says they should have). Repeat tomorrow and compare notes: the observations converge as
the tongue learns.

**अभ्यासः 6 · शब्दयोजना (assembly, written).** From the tiles नमः · कथा · आम् · नदी ·
मधु · ततः · अत्र · अस्ति, build and write in Devanagari: (a) a two-turn greeting; (b) a
story-request and its answer; (c) one scene-setting line on the §17 pattern (X अत्र
अस्ति।). Read your build aloud in careful pause form — each word said fully, the way word
lists are traditionally recited. (गगन "sky" is readable now too — but resist inventing
endings to put things *in* the sky; what an ending does to a word is Volume 3's story.)

पुनरावृत्तिः — one screen to remember.

> **The place:** the teeth — tongue tip ON them, fingertip check; not the English ridge,
> not yesterday's curl. **The row:** त /t̪/ · थ /t̪ʰ/ · द /d̪/ · ध /d̪ʱ/ · न /n̪/ — the

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**This is the contrast lesson** — the phonetic centerpiece of the chapter. Everything else
in the room serves the moment a learner *feels* teeth, ridge and curl as three different
places. Do the fingertip demo yourself, facing the class, before any theory.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written, incl. ट-row recital (5) → hook + fingertip
demo on नमस्ते (4) → three-address drill, whole class in unison, then pairs (10) → five
cards + row recital with manner checks (8) → writing block: थ द ध न + the ध/घ panel (8) →
word list + the कथा/नमः payoff, savored aloud (5) → contrast test, exercise 2 (3) →
homework briefing incl. the 1-day retest (2).

**Board work:** the 5×5 grid poster grows its fourth row *live*, letter by letter, as each
card is taught — the grid filling week by week is the syllabus. Beside it, keep one simple
side-view mouth with three arrows: teeth / ridge / curl, labeled त / "English" / ट.

**Choreography of the three-address drill:** English word first ("tea", "day", "knee") so
the habit is audible and owned without shame; then yesterday's letter; then today's. Name
the direction, not the error: "slide it forward". Unison first — thirty tongues fail more
comfortably than one — then pairs with fingertip observation and swapped roles.

**The contrast test needs morale management.** Announce in advance that it is the
chapter's hardest ear test, that 12/16 passes, and that everyone retests tomorrow — scores
reliably jump after sleep. Collect error *types* from dictation too: place errors (त/ट),
breath errors (द/ध), nasal errors (न/ण) and dropped final vowels each get their own
remediation — ladder drill, tissue, pair drill, finger-counting respectively.

**Differentiation — Hindi speakers:** the row is native; deploy them as pronunciation
models in pair work (modeling sharpens their own ear). Their genuine work sits elsewhere:
final vowels (दिन/धन are exactly the words where the deletion habit bites hardest), the ण
discipline, and holding ध's murmur at recitation slowness. Hand them §7 and make them the
explainers of the "th"-spelling trap to English-speaking partners.

**What students find hard:** alveolar backsliding the moment speed rises — build speed
only after three clean slow rounds; ध's murmur fading to plain द mid-list — re-anchor with
the palm; and hearing ना/णा, the least meaning-loaded contrast for English ears — let the
retest policy do its quiet work.
