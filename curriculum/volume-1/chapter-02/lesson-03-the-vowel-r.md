## 1. Hook

Fold your arms, pretend you are freezing, and make the English winter noise: *brrr*. Now do
it again — without the *b*. Just the buzzing hum, tongue fluttering behind your teeth. Hold
it for one beat. You have just pronounced a vowel made entirely of *r* — no *a*, no *i*, no
*u* anywhere inside it. English files that noise under "sound effects". Sanskrit listened
more carefully, measured it, and gave it a letter of its own: ऋ. Today you learn the vowel
that is an r — and अमृत, a word your ears have carried since Chapter 1, finally opens to
your eyes.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — a vowel among vowels, and what the centuries did to it.

When the Pāṇinian tradition drew up its master inventory of sounds, ऋ was no afterthought.
It stands in the front row, among the simplest vowels, before ए ओ ऐ औ ever appear.

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The Māheśvara sūtras — the fourteen sound-lists that open the
> Pāṇinian tradition — begin with the simple vowels: first अइउण् (aiuṇ), then immediately
> ऋऌक् (ṛḷk). In this ordering ṛ and ḷ sit with अ, इ and उ: vowels among vowels, listed
> before the compound vowels. [REF:maheshvarasutras 1–2]

The classification is not a quirk of list-making. The tradition's phoneticians heard ऋ doing
exactly what अ, इ and उ do — carrying the beat of a syllable — and filed it accordingly.
Modern articulatory phonetics agrees with the filing (§5).

What changed was not the letter but the mouths around it. The spoken languages of north
India gradually lost the syllabic r; readers kept the letter and substituted the nearest
sound their own language offered.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** Describing Sanskrit for Western readers in 1889, Whitney defined
> ऋ as r "used with vowel-value" — and recorded, already then, that reciters of his day
> commonly read it as *ri*, with *ru* usual in Maharashtra. The classical value and its
> regional replacements have coexisted for a long time. [REF:whitney1889 §24]

Say this part plainly, because this course exists to say it: the reciters who read ऋषि
(ṛṣi) as "rishi" are not careless. They belong to real, living Sanskrit lineages, and their
habit is the regular sound-change of their first languages carried into recitation — the
same kind of process as the Hindi schwa-drop you studied in Chapter 1. Many textbooks —
including the first edition of this very course — went one step further and printed "ri"
*as the value of* ऋ. That step is the error: it promotes a regional habit to the classical
target. Here we train the target /r̩/ and name the variants for what they are — habits,
noted with respect.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — what makes a vowel a vowel, and how an r can be one.

A vowel is not defined by "open mouth" or by the letters a e i o u. Phonetically, what makes
something a vowel is its *job*: it sits at the centre of a syllable — the nucleus — and
carries the beat. Usually vowels do this job. But other sounds can be hired.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Consonants can serve as syllable nuclei. Ordinary English does it
> daily: *button* and *bottle* end in syllabic [n̩] and [l̩] — a full second syllable with
> no vowel sound in it at all. [REF:ladefoged2015]

Test it on yourself: say *button* naturally. The second syllable is not "ton" — your tongue
goes to the n-position and simply hums there. You have been producing syllabic consonants
your whole life; you just never wrote one down. Sanskrit did.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** ऋ = /r̩/ — a syllabic r: the tongue takes the position of the tap
> r and holds it, voiced, as the beat-carrying nucleus of the syllable. It is hrasva —
> one mātrā, exactly like अ, इ, उ. [REF:whitney1889 §24], [REF:ladefoged2015]

The small vertical stroke under the r in /r̩/ is the IPA's "syllabic" mark — "this consonant
is doing a vowel's job" (§10). English even has a closer cousin than *button*: in American
English *bird* is, for many speakers, one long r-colored vowel [ɝ] — a vowel that is
(almost) an r. The anchor's imprecision: the American sound is a bunched approximant, made
with the tongue body; Sanskrit ऋ is made at the tap position, tongue-tip up (§11). Use
*bird* to believe the idea; use the brrr-ladder (§9) to build the sound.

## 7. Hindi Explanation

हिन्दी व्याख्या

आज का पाठ इस पूरी किताब के सबसे ज़रूरी पाठों में से है, और ख़ास तौर पर आपके लिए — क्योंकि ऋ
को आप बरसों से *लिखते* आ रहे हैं: ऋषि, ऋतु, कृपया, अमृत, मातृभाषा। अक्षर जाना-पहचाना है;
आज की नयी चीज़ उसकी ध्वनि है।

सीधी बात पहले। हिन्दी में हम ऋ को "रि" पढ़ते हैं — ऋषि यानी "रिषि", कृपा यानी "क्रिपा",
अमृत यानी "अम्रित"। यह ग़लती नहीं है। जैसे हिन्दी ने शब्द के अन्त का अ धीरे-धीरे छोड़ दिया
(पाठ 1.4 का अ-लोप याद कीजिए), वैसे ही उसने सदियों पहले स्वर ऋ खो दिया और उसकी जगह अपनी
सबसे नज़दीकी ध्वनि "रि" रख ली। मराठी भाषियों ने "रु" रखी — इसीलिए महाराष्ट्र में "रुषि"
सुनाई देता है। यह हिन्दी और मराठी की अपनी ध्वनि-व्यवस्था है; भाषाएँ ऐसा करती ही हैं।

पर संस्कृत पढ़ते समय हम शास्त्रीय लक्ष्य साधते हैं, और अच्छी ख़बर यह है कि खोया हुआ स्वर
पाँच मिनट में वापस मिल जाता है। तरकीब सुनिए: ठण्ड में काँपते हुए अंग्रेज़ी में जो "brrr"
निकलता है, उसे बोलिए। अब वही आवाज़ *ब के बिना* निकालिए — होंठ नहीं, सिर्फ़ जीभ की गूँजती
थरथराहट। एक मात्रा भर रोकिए। बस — यही ऋ है। ध्यान रखिए: इसमें इ की कोई झलक नहीं है। जीभ
र की जगह पर टिकी रहती है और स्वर वहीं गूँजता है; जीभ हिली और "रि" बन गया, होंठ गोल हुए
और "रु" बन गया। शीशे के सामने अभ्यास कीजिए — होंठ बिलकुल शान्त रहें।

अभ्यास के शब्द भी आपके अपने हैं। अमृत — amṛta — अ + मृ + त — तीन अक्षर, हर स्वर पूरा। मातृ —
"मातृभाषा" वाला मातृ — असल में माता का मूल (कोशगत) रूप है (§13)। और जब कोई किताब ऋ = "रि"
छापे — कई पुरानी किताबें, इस पाठ्यक्रम का पहला संस्करण भी, यही छापती थीं — तो अब आप जानते
हैं कि वह आधुनिक आदत को शास्त्रीय लक्ष्य बता रही है।

अन्तिम बात, और सबसे ज़रूरी: जिनके घर या मन्दिर की परम्परा में "रि" या "रु" चलता है, उनका
संस्कृत झूठा नहीं है। परम्परा सच्ची है; शास्त्रीय लक्ष्य-ध्वनि भी सच्ची है। हम दोनों को
उनके सही नाम से पहचानते हैं — और अभ्यास शास्त्रीय ध्वनि का करते हैं।

## 8. English Explanation

Two new things today: a sound with no everyday English letter (a vowel made of r), and the
smallest mātrā yet (a curl at the consonant's foot). Then the reward: two of the best words
in the course become readable.

### Part one — the sound /r̩/, step by step

1. **Find the tap position.** Say American-style *butter* or *city*: the *tt* is a quick
   flick of the tongue-tip against the ridge behind the upper teeth — a tap. (British
   speakers who don't tap: make the lightest, fastest d-like flick you can.) Hindi speakers:
   it is exactly your र position. That ridge is home base.
2. **Stay instead of leaving.** A tap touches and leaves within a flash. This time, bring
   the tongue-tip up to that position, keep the contact light and loose — loose enough to
   buzz — and switch your voice on. Hum *through* the position.
3. **Give it one beat.** Cut the hum down to a single hrasva beat, the same length as अ:
   **ऋ**. That short voiced buzz, tongue in r-position, carrying its own beat — that is the
   whole sound.

What ऋ is *not*: it is not "ri" — no /i/ or /ɪ/ glides out of it; if your tongue relaxes
downward at the end, you have added a second vowel. It is not "ru" — if your lips round,
you have added one too. And it is not a Spanish-style drum-roll — a light flutter may
happen, but the target is a smooth hum, not a performance (§12).

### Part two — the letter ऋ and the mātrā ृ

Like every vowel, ṛ has two written forms. Word-initially it is the independent letter ऋ:
ऋत (ṛta), ऋतु (ṛtu). After a consonant it becomes a mātrā — the smallest one you have met:

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The ṛ-mātrā ृ attaches at the foot of the consonant's vertical stem —
> below and to the right: कृ (kṛ), मृ (mṛ), तृ (tṛ). Like every mātrā, it replaces the
> inherent अ. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

Your mātrā map now covers four of the five positions: **right** (ा ी), **left** (ि),
**below** (ु ू), **below-right at the stem's foot** (ृ) — and lesson 4 finally sends mātrās
**above** the headline (े ै).

One more thing: you have been writing this mātrā's sound since day one without knowing it.
Look at the middle of संस्कृतम् (saṃskṛtam) — there it is, ृ, inside the name of the
language itself: *saṃ-s-kṛ-ta*, "put together well, refined", built on the root *kṛ* "to
make, do" — the same root as today's word कृत (kṛta) "done".

### Part three — completing the family: ॠ and ऌ

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** ऋ has a long partner ॠ /r̩ː/ — the same syllabic r held for two
> beats — and a cousin ऌ /l̩/, a syllabic l. Both are real and rare: ॠ appears almost only
> inside certain grammatical endings, ऌ only in forms of one verbal root (√कॢप् "to be
> fit"). [REF:whitney1889 §§23–26]

They exist mainly for the system's symmetry: every simple vowel gets a length partner —
अ/आ, इ/ई, उ/ऊ, ऋ/ॠ — and the grammarians even paired ऌ with a theoretical ॡ that no real
word uses. Their mātrās (ॄ, ॢ) exist too. Learn to *recognize* ॠ and ऌ on sight; the course
asks nothing more of them — no vocabulary, no drills. When ॠ finally appears inside a word
in Volume 3, you will nod at it like an old acquaintance.

### The payoff

With ऋ and ृ in hand, अमृत (amṛta) and मातृ (mātṛ) unlock — the first an ear-word since
Chapter 1, lesson 3, the second the dictionary's own form of माता. Their stories are worth
a section of their own: §13.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — three drills, in order. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c02l03-r-drill.mp3` (drills 1–2) and
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c02l03-word-list.mp3` (drill 3).

**Drill 1 — the brrr-ladder.** Four rungs, three rounds:

1. Full shiver: *brrr* — lips and all, enjoy it.
2. Drop the *b*: bare buzzing hum, tongue-tip up, one slow breath long.
3. Cut it to one beat: ऋ — same length as अ. Check: lips still? no *i* leaking out the end?
4. Alternate on a steady beat: अ — ऋ — अ — ऋ — अ — ऋ. Both are one-beat vowels; only the
   tongue position changes. Rest position for अ, tap position for ऋ.

**Drill 2 — the syllable column.** Read down each column, steady beat; the right column
simply swaps the inherent अ for ऋ:

| क /kɐ/ | कृ /kr̩/ |
|---|---|
| म /mɐ/ | मृ /mr̩/ |
| त /t̪ɐ/ | तृ /t̪r̩/ |
| ल /lɐ/ | लृ /lr̩/ |

(लृ is a drill syllable, not a dictionary word — like कम and मक in Chapter 1.) Then the
"freeze test" on each: say कृ and freeze mid-sound — your tongue-tip should still be up at
the ridge when the sound ends. If it has dropped, "kri" crept in.

**Drill 3 — words.** From §14: ऋत — ऋतु — कृत — मृत — मातृ — अमृत, each twice, one beat per
akṣara. Then the discrimination sets: the initial-consonant pair कृत/मृत; the syllable-count
pair ऋत/ऋतु; and the chapter's best triple, slow then at speed: मत — मित — मृत. Same
skeleton, three different vowels doing the same job.

## 10. IPA

The new symbol: ऋ = **/r̩/** — an r carrying the syllabicity mark, the small vertical stroke
beneath. That mark is the IPA's promotion stamp: "this consonant is serving as a syllable's
nucleus." You met its work already in English [n̩] (*button*) and [l̩] (*bottle*) — §5.

The family, for recognition: ऋ /r̩/ · ॠ /r̩ː/ (the length mark ː you know from आ /ɑː/,
ई /iː/, ऊ /uː/) · ऌ /l̩/.

**Narrow note — habits in brackets.** The regional realizations are written in brackets
because they are realizations, not the phoneme: [ɾɪ] (the northern "ri"), [ɾu] (the Marathi
"ru"). So ऋषि: classical target /r̩ʂi/, common habit [ɾɪʂi]. Same word, two registers of
pronunciation — the brackets keep the bookkeeping honest.

**Coming attraction.** Sanskrit also has a consonant r — the tap /ɾ/ of र, arriving with the
semivowels later in Volume 1. Same articulator, different job: /ɾ/ touches and leaves
between vowels; /r̩/ stays and *is* the vowel. The syllabicity mark is what tells them apart
on paper.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — walkthrough of the diagram (planned:
`assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c02l03-mouth-position-r.svg` — sagittal view, tongue-tip raised
to the ridge behind the upper teeth, voicing marked at the larynx, lips neutral).

1. **Jaw:** nearly closed — this is a small-mouth vowel, the opposite of आ.
2. **Tongue:** tip up and lightly curled toward the ridge just behind the upper teeth. The
   contact is loose — air must still buzz through. Think "resting at the tap position",
   not "pressing".
3. **Lips:** completely neutral. Rounding is the "ru" drift — the mirror will catch it
   before your ears do.
4. **Voice:** on, for the whole beat. Back of your hand on your throat: it should buzz from
   the first instant — there is no vowel "before" the r; the r *is* the vowel.

**Two mirror checks.** (1) Rounding watch: say ऋ five times watching only your lips — any
circle forming means Marathi-style [ɾu] is sneaking in. (2) The freeze test (§9): say ऋ and
freeze at the end — the tongue-tip should still be up. If it has flopped down, an [ɪ] or [ə]
escaped and you said "ri".

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with a fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **The full "ri" substitution (Hindi and other north-Indian-language speakers — and
   anyone raised on charts that print "ri").** ऋषि becomes [ɾɪʂi], मृत becomes [mrɪt̪ɐ]. It
   is a regional habit, not a character flaw — but it erases the vowel this lesson exists
   to teach. *Fix:* re-anchor on the brrr-ladder (§9 drill 1) before every practice
   session; run the freeze test on every ृ syllable. One shiver resets the target.
2. **Inserting a vowel before the r (mostly English speakers).** ऋतु comes out "er-tu" or
   "ir-tu" [əɾt̪u] — the speaker warms up with a schwa and only then finds the r. *Fix:* the
   r *is* the start. Put the tongue-tip up **first**, in silence, then switch the voice on:
   the buzz should begin already in position.
3. **Over-trilling.** Five proud Spanish-style taps — rrrr! — turn the quiet vowel into a
   drum-roll: it eats the beat and becomes a consonant performance. *Fix:* it is a hum, not
   a roll. Say it softly, lazily even; a faint flutter is fine, a fanfare is not. One beat,
   smooth.
4. **Writing ऋ where ृ belongs — or the curl as a floating mark.** Learners write कऋ for कृ
   (kṛ), or draw the little curl detached, hovering beside the letter; some grab ु instead
   of ृ. *Fix:* the mātrā discipline of lessons 1–2 applies unchanged: after a consonant,
   always the attached mātrā, never the independent letter. Finish the consonant, then hang
   the curl **on the foot of its stem**. Chant while writing: "letter, then curl."

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — the payoff section: two famous words and one perfect triple.

### अमृत — the word you have been hearing since Chapter 1

Build it, block by block: अ + मृ + त — a, mṛ, ta.

**अमृत** (amṛta) "deathless; nectar" — अमृत · /ɐmr̩t̪ɐ/ [REF:apte1890 s.v. अमृत]

Since Chapter 1, lesson 3, this word has been a 🔊 ear-word — pronounceable, unreadable, a
promise. The promise is now kept: three akṣaras, every one of them yours. And the word
repays the wait, because it is built like a tiny sentence: मृत (mṛta) "dead" — today's
vocabulary — with the negative prefix *a-* in front: **a-mṛta, "the not-dead, the
deathless"** — the nectar of immortality. You can watch Sanskrit grammar work with your own
eyes now: मृत → अमृत, dead → deathless, one letter of difference.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit अमृत (amṛta) and Greek *ámbrotos* "immortal" — source of
> *ambrosía*, the gods' food — are parallel descendants of the same Proto-Indo-European
> root \*mer- "die", each with the inherited negative prefix. Cognates, not loans: English
> *ambrosia* did not come from Sanskrit, nor amṛta from Greek; they are siblings from one
> ancestral family. [REF:mallory2006], [REF:burrow2001]

The mātā/mother receipt from Chapter 1 was no fluke. Here is a second one, richer: your
new readable word and the English word *ambrosia* are the same ancient "deathless",
grown apart for millennia and still recognizable.

### मातृ — mother, in dictionary dress

**मातृ** (mātṛ) "mother (stem)" — मातृ · /mɑːt̪r̩/

In lesson 1.4 you read माता (mātā). Open any Sanskrit dictionary, though, and the entry is
filed under **मातृ** — ending in today's vowel. One honest paragraph on why: Sanskrit words
have a bare base form, the **stem**, which dictionaries list and compounds use — and
inflected forms, which sentences use; माता is the stem मातृ dressed for its role as a
sentence's subject. Hindi keeps the stem alive in compounds you already know: मातृभाषा
(mātṛbhāṣā) "mother tongue". How stems put on their endings is a Volume 3 story — for now
it is enough to recognize the pair: मातृ in the dictionary, माता in the sentence, one word.
[REF:whitney1889 §370ff], [REF:apte1890 s.v. मातृ]

### The triple — three vowels, one skeleton

| देवनागरी (IAST) | Meaning | IPA |
|---|---|---|
| मत (mata) | "thought, opinion" — मत, विचार | /mɐt̪ɐ/ |
| मित (mita) | "measured, moderate" — नपा-तुला | /mit̪ɐ/ |
| मृत (mṛta) | "dead" — मृत | /mr̩t̪ɐ/ |

One frame, म_त, and three vowels taking turns at its centre: /ɐ/, /i/, /r̩/ — each carrying
the syllable exactly the same way. That is the whole argument of this lesson in three words:
ऋ is not an r *plus* some vowel; it stands where vowels stand. If you can say and hear these
three apart, ऋ is yours. (They return in §18, exercise 5, and in the chapter review.)

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule**, and today's completes a familiar pattern
with an unfamiliar shape.

### The distribution rule

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The vowel ṛ is written with its independent letter ऋ where it begins
> the akṣara — in practice word-initially: ऋत (ṛta), ऋतु (ṛtu) — and with the mātrā ृ after
> a consonant, where it replaces the inherent अ: क + ऋ = कृ (kṛ), never कऋ. The two
> spellings never compete for the same position. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

Same decision procedure as ा, ि, ी, ु, ू — only the address is new. The mātrā map so far:

| Position | Mātrās | Example |
|---|---|---|
| right | ा ी | का की |
| left | ि | कि |
| below | ु ू | कु कू |
| below-right, at the stem's foot | ृ | कृ |
| above | े ै — lesson 4 | — |

### Stroke order — ऋ and ृ

One common convention, described here; teaching traditions vary in details, and your
teacher's consistent habit is fine too. (Card:
`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c02l03-stroke-order.svg`; tracing:
`planned:assets/worksheets/volume-1/v1c02l03-tracing.pdf`.)

- **ऋ** — three movements, then the line. (1) The neck and arm: from where the headline
  will sit, drop a short stem and let it sweep out into the arm that runs down to the
  **left**, like a pennant in wind. (2) The right-side curl: start in the small ball
  touching the stem, swing out to the right and around into the open hook that hangs below.
  (3) Headline last, across the top. Check your result against the letter card: short neck,
  left arm, right curl-and-hook.
- **ृ** — after the consonant is *complete*: one short motion at the foot of its vertical
  stem — drop from the stem, swing right, and curl back under so the tip points up-left.
  A tight little curl, hanging from the stem like a tendril. "Letter, then curl."
- **ृ vs ु — don't swap the basement tenants.** Both live below the letter, but ु is a
  wider, open swing under the body, while ृ is a small closed curl at the stem's foot.
  Fast-reading check: open swing = u, tight curl = ṛ.

### The walkthrough — अमृत and friends

- **अमृत** = अ + मृ + त → a + mṛ + ta = amṛta. Three akṣaras; the middle one is म with its
  inherent vowel replaced by the curl.
- **मातृ** = मा + तृ → mā + tṛ = mātṛ. Two akṣaras, two mātrās, two different addresses:
  ा at the right shoulder, ृ at the stem's foot.
- **ऋतु** = ऋ + तु → both of today's rules in one word: independent letter to start,
  and lesson 2's ु below the त.

## 16. Conversation

सम्भाषणम् — tiny, honest, and weather-powered. 🔊 audio recorded into the Chapter 2 drill
pack (§27). Two friends at a window; the first rain of the season is falling.

— *(pointing outside, eyebrows up)* ऋतु?
(ṛtu?) "The season? (Has it turned?)" — मौसम (बदल गया)?

— आम्, ऋतु!
(ām, ṛtu!) "Yes — the season!" — हाँ, मौसम बदल गया!

— *(arms wide, delighted, catching the rain-smell)* मम ऋतु!
(mama ṛtu!) "My season!" — मेरा (पसन्दीदा) मौसम!

— आम्, तव ऋतु।
(ām, tava ṛtu.) "Yes — your season." — हाँ, तुम्हारा मौसम।

Honesty box, as usual: real connected Sanskrit would inflect these words (you will meet
ऋतुः when the visarga arrives in lesson 6 — soon!). For now we speak in bare citation forms
plus gestures, because sounds and words are what we are training. Glue by ear: आम् (ām)
"yes" — हाँ 🔊; तव (tava) "your" — तुम्हारा 🔊. Everything else, you can read — including
both ऋ's. One beat each, lips still.

## 17. Story

कथा — ऋत · The Order Behind the Seasons. A cultural story, register-labeled.

Why does Sanskrit's word for "season", ऋतु, begin with today's vowel? Because it is a small
window into a very large idea.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** ऋत (ṛta) and ऋतु (ṛtu) are kin: the dictionaries derive both from the
> root √ऋ (ṛ) "to go, move, fit". An ṛtu is literally a "fitting-time" — the appointed slot
> in the year's motion where each thing belongs. [REF:apte1890 s.v. ऋतु],
> [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. ऋत]

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** In the Ṛgveda's worldview, ṛta is the deep order of things —
> the pattern by which rivers keep to their beds, dawns return on time, ritual reaches its
> gods, and true speech stays true. The great gods are praised as its guardians. This is
> the tradition's picture of the world, offered here as exactly that.

> **[SYMBOL · प्रतीकम्]** For the Vedic poets, the turning seasons were ṛta made visible:
> winter yields to spring on schedule not by chance but because the cosmos keeps its word.
> A beautiful image — a statement of meaning, not of meteorology.

So the two smallest words in today's list carry the chapter's biggest freight: ऋत, the
order; ऋतु, the order's visible footsteps through the year. When Sanskrit names a season,
it quietly repeats the old thought that a season is *a thing that fits*. Read the pair
aloud once, slowly — ऋत, ऋतु — one clean beat on each ऋ, and notice that you are reading,
with your own eyes, a word family older than the alphabet you are reading it in.

## 20. Revision

पुनरावृत्तिः — one screen to remember.

> **The sound:** ऋ /r̩/ — a vowel made of r: tongue at the tap position, voice humming, one
> beat. Not "ri", not "ru", not a drum-roll — those are habits and slips, named with
> respect; the classical target is the hum. **The letters:** independent ऋ word-initially
> (ऋत, ऋतु); the mātrā ृ at the foot of the stem after a consonant — क + ऋ = कृ, "letter,
> then curl". Rare partners ॠ /r̩ː/ and ऌ /l̩/: recognize, don't drill. **The proof:** मत ≠
> मित ≠ मृत — three vowels, one job. **The prize:** अमृत = a- + mṛta "the deathless",
> cognate of *ambrosia*; मातृ, the dictionary's माता. **Six words:** ऋत, ऋतु, कृत, मृत,
> मातृ, अमृत.

Spaced review (distributed practice beats cramming [REF:cepeda2006]):

- **In 1 day:** re-take the dictation (exercise 4) cold; write कृ मृ तृ from memory; run
  the freeze test five times. Expect the "ri" habit to have partially regrown overnight —
  one brrr resets it. That reset *is* the practice.
- **In 1 week:** the triple discrimination again (exercise 5), plus the six words read at
  speed. Keep कुल/कूल and इति/ईति warm — lesson 7 tests every length pair in the chapter.
- **From lesson 4:** the mātrā map finally goes *above* the headline (े ै) — today's map
  row for "above" gets filled in.

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**This is the course's most sensitive sound lesson — set the tone before the phonetics.**
Many students (and many teachers) grew up with "ri" at home, at school, in temple
recitation. Never frame that as broken Sanskrit. Two statements are both true and the
lesson depends on keeping them both: the regional habits are real, respectable products of
real language history; and the classical target is /r̩/, which is what this class trains.
Students who feel their family tradition mocked will defend it instead of learning the
second sound — rightly. Offer /r̩/ as an *addition* to what they own, never a replacement
demanded of their grandmothers.

**The brrr moment produces laughter — spend it immediately.** A class that has just
shivered together on *brrr* is relaxed enough to try a weird new vowel without
self-consciousness. Run the ladder straight into the freeze test while the mood holds.

**If a student brings a chart printing "ri":** treat it as a live specimen, not a scandal —
"that chart teaches the habit; we're training the target." The first edition of this very
course made the same error; saying so out loud models the honesty the course is built on.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → hook: the shiver, drop the *b* (4) →
§5 idea: *button*/*bottle*, "you already make syllabic consonants" (5) → production: ladder,
freeze test, mirror checks (10) → letter + mātrā: board map, कृ मृ तृ, "letter, then curl"
(8) → the अमृत build + मातृ + the triple (8) → exercises 3–4 briefing, homework, incl. the
curl-hunt (5).

**Board work:** keep the mātrā geography map up all lesson — ा ी right · ि left · ु ू below
· ृ at the stem's foot — and add a dotted "े ै above: next lesson" teaser. For the payoff,
build अमृत on the board block by block (अ… मृ… त) and let a *student* read it aloud first.
Then tell them how long they have been waiting: since Chapter 1, lesson 3. Someone usually
gasps; that gasp is the lesson landing.

**Dictation logistics:** read at natural speed; do not over-buzz the ऋ as a hint. Collect
error types, not just scores: "ri"-driven spellings (रि for ृ), the ु/ृ swap, floating
curls, and कऋ each get different remediation — the first is a §9 problem, the rest are §15
problems.

**Differentiation.** *Hindi-literate learners:* strongest script advantage and strongest
habit; give them the explainer role — articulating habit-vs-target kindly is itself
objective 4, and judging a partner's freeze test sharpens their own. *Marathi-background
learners:* the drift is toward [ɾu]; assign the lip-mirror check specifically. *English
speakers:* usually fastest to /r̩/ via *butter*/*bird*, but watch the schwa onset (§12,
mistake 2) and the proud over-trill.

**What students find hard:** believing that a vowel can be an r (the *button*/*bottle* demo
converts the skeptics — let them feel their own tongue do it in English first); hearing
mṛ vs mri at speed (normalize the 1-day delay; scores jump after sleep); and keeping ऋ to
one smooth beat under exam nerves. The freeze test is the universal diagnostic — teach it
until students run it on themselves unprompted.
