## 1. Hook

A dot. A colon. That is today's entire cast — and yet these two are probably the most *seen*
marks in all of Sanskrit: open any verse, any prayer, any page, and within seconds a ं is
floating over one syllable and a ः is closing another. रामः earns its ः today; संस्कृतम् its
ं. Neither is a vowel. Neither is a consonant. The tradition gave them their own category and
their own name — "the ones that ride along" — and lesson 2 of this course promised you the
full story. Today the promise is paid: the smallest signs in the script, with the biggest
attendance record in the language, become yours.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — the two sounds that refused the boxes.

Every good classification system eventually meets items that will not fit its boxes. The
ancient grammarians sorted speech into स्वर (svara) — vowels, sounds that can stand alone —
and व्यञ्जन (vyañjana) — consonants, sounds that need a vowel to be pronounceable. Then two
leftovers remained on the table: a nasal after-hum and a breath after-puff. Each needs a vowel
*in front of it*: you cannot begin a word with either, cannot sing either on its own, cannot
even say either without first saying some vowel for it to lean on. A vowel they are not; but
they are not consonants of the ordinary kind either — no closure, no place of their own.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** The Sanskrit grammatical tradition treats anusvāra and visarga as a
> class apart from both vowels and consonants, and the standard Western grammars follow it:
> Whitney describes both as sounds that occur only after a vowel and never independently, and
> documents the tradition's long discussion of their exact status. [REF:whitney1889 §§67–73]

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The grammarians' own name for the pair says it all: अयोगवाह
> (ayogavāha) is traditionally glossed "carried along without connection" — sounds *carried*
> by the vowel they follow, holding no independent place of their own in the sound-lists.
> They ride; they do not stand.

Notice the honesty of the move. A lazier tradition would have stuffed the two into the vowel
list and moved on — and indeed the schoolroom count "14 vowels" does exactly that, as we saw
in Chapter 1. The grammarians refused. When something genuinely belongs to no existing
category, you *say so* and make a new one. That refusal, made over two thousand years ago, is
why this course could promise you in lesson 2 an honest count — 13 + 33 + 2 — and why today's
lesson exists as the proper home of the "+ 2". With it, Chapter 2's inventory work is
complete: every vowel owned, and now both of its riders.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — a chameleon nasal and a voiceless breath.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Anusvāra (ṃ) marks a nasal element after a vowel. Before a stop
> consonant it is pronounced as the nasal of that stop's varga — *homorganic*, "same place of
> articulation": अंक = [ɐŋkɐ], with the velar nasal [ŋ], because क is velar. Where no stop
> follows, it is a nasal hum — nasalization of the vowel, or a nasal continuant.
> [REF:whitney1889 §§70–71]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Visarga (ḥ) is a voiceless /h/ — a breath released after the vowel while
> the mouth still holds the vowel's shape. It is extremely frequent word-finally — the रामः
> pattern you have heard since lesson 1. [REF:whitney1889 §67]

**The chameleon is not exotic — your English does it too.** Say *ink* slowly: it is spelled
with n, but what you actually say is [ɪŋk] — the nasal slides back to the velar position
because k lives there. Latin did it so consistently that English spelling froze the results:
*in-* + *possible* = **im**possible, the nasal moving to the lips because p lives at the
lips. Nasal place assimilation is one of the most ordinary processes in the world's languages
[REF:ladefoged2015]. What Sanskrit adds is not the habit but the *bookkeeping*: the varṇamālā
grid from lesson 2 of Chapter 1 tells you exactly which nasal, every time. Find the following
stop's row; the dot is that row's fifth cell. अंक: the following stop क sits in the क-वर्ग,
the velar row; that row's nasal is ङ /ŋ/; therefore [ɐŋkɐ]. This is the first time the grid
*computes an answer for you* — it will not be the last.

**The breath sign is the mouth doing nothing, honestly.** For visarga, the vocal folds stop
buzzing and simply let air through — a whisper's texture, lasting a beat after the vowel.
Because the tongue and lips are still parked in the previous vowel's position, the breath
takes on that vowel's color — which explains a habit you will hear in recitation:

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** Traditional recitation practice often gives a word-final visarga
> a soft echo of the preceding vowel: रामः recited as [rɑːmɐhɐ], the echo copying whatever
> vowel came before. This echo is a recitation style, honored as such — in plain speech the
> visarga is the breath alone.

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतव्याख्या — the lesson's idea in the simplest possible Sanskrit.

अं इति अनुस्वारः।
(aṃ iti anusvāraḥ.) "*aṃ* is the anusvāra." — अं अनुस्वार है।

अः इति विसर्गः।
(aḥ iti visargaḥ.) "*aḥ* is the visarga." — अः विसर्ग है।

ततः किम्?
(tataḥ kim?) "Then what?" — फिर क्या?

That last line is a complete Sanskrit exchange in two words — the question every listener
asks every storyteller, and you can already read its first word. (किम् 🔊 stays an ear-word:
its final म् needs the virāma, Chapter 5.) The traditional names arriving today: अनुस्वार
(anusvāra) — literally "the after-sound", it sounds *after* the vowel; विसर्ग (visarga) — "the
release", a letting-go of breath [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. विसर्ग]. And a bonus hidden in
this lesson's title: अयोगवाहौ (ayogavāhau) ends in -au, the dual ending — "the *two*
ayogavāha" — written with the ौ you learned yesterday. Sanskrit has a whole grammatical
number for exactly two things; the full story is Volume 3's.

## 8. English Explanation

Two small signs, one shared category, and — for the first time — a rule the grid computes for
you. Take the signs one at a time, then put them to work on six new words.

### The two signs, mechanically

**Anusvāra ं** is a dot above the headline. It adds a nasal element *after* the akṣara's
vowel: क (ka) → कं (kaṃ), अ (a) → अं (aṃ). **Visarga ः** is a pair of small circles after the
akṣara, on the line. It adds a voiceless breath *after* the vowel: क (ka) → कः (kaḥ), अ (a) →
अः (aḥ). In IAST they are ṃ and ḥ — the same letters m and h wearing an underdot, exactly the
"dependent" idea in Roman dress. Both signs are written last, after the consonant and any
mātrā; both are read last, after the vowel (§15). And both belong to neither of the big
categories — if you once learned "14 vowels" at school, Chapter 1 already retired that count:
the honest inventory is 13 + 33 + 2, and these are the 2.

### The dot reads the grid

The anusvāra has no fixed sound of its own. Before a stop consonant, it becomes the nasal of
that stop's row — the grid's fifth column, delivered to your door:

| The next stop's row | The dot becomes | Where |
|---|---|---|
| क-वर्ग · velar | ङ (ṅa) — [ŋ] | back of tongue + soft palate |
| च-वर्ग (palatal) | ञ (ña) — [ɲ] | tongue body + hard palate |
| ट-वर्ग (retroflex) | ण (ṇa) — [ɳ] | tongue tip curled back |
| त-वर्ग (dental) | न (na) — [n̪] | tongue tip on the teeth |
| प-वर्ग (labial) | म (ma) — [m] | the two lips |
| *no stop follows* | a nasal hum — [◌̃] or [m] | air through the nose, vowel color kept |

Worked example, with the only stop you can read: **अंक** — अ, then the dot, then क. The क is
velar, so the dot is the velar nasal: [ɐŋkɐ]. Your tongue's back is already lifting toward
the क position while your nose is still humming — that overlap *is* the homorganic nasal. The
middle rows of the table are previews; their example words arrive with their consonants in
Chapters 3–4. The last row you already own by ear: in संस्कृतम् (saṃskṛtam) the dot stands
before स — not a stop — so it stays a pure nasal hum: [sɐ̃skr̩t̪ɐm], no lip closure, no
tongue contact.

### The breath sign — voiceless, never dropped

Visarga is /h/ — the *voiceless* breath. Two disciplines, one for each native-language habit:
it is never voiced into a full "ha" syllable (the vocal folds stay off — whisper-texture
only), and it is never silently dropped (the breath must actually arrive; a palm held before
the lips should feel it). The recitation echo — रामः as [rɑːmɐhɐ] — is a tradition-register
style for chanting (§5), not a license to add syllables in plain speech.

### The spelling twins: अंक and अङ्क

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Classical orthography often writes the homorganic nasal explicitly, as
> a conjunct with the varga's own nasal letter: अङ्क, with a visible ङ. The anusvāra spelling
> अंक is the common modern convention. Both spellings are read identically — [ɐŋkɐ].
> [REF:whitney1889 §73], [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

So when a printed text shows you अङ्क where this course writes अंक, nothing has changed but
ink: the dot was always *shorthand* for that row-nasal, and classical typesetting simply says
the quiet part out loud. In IAST the two spellings are distinguished — the explicit form is
written aṅka, the dot form aṃka — one sound, two written costumes. How consonants stack into
conjuncts like ङ्क is Chapter 5's machinery; today you only need to recognize the twins as
twins.

### Worked readings — the payoff

Block by block, exactly as you learned in Chapter 1:

- **ततः** = त + तः → ta-ta-ḥ — /t̪ɐt̪ɐh/ "then, thereafter". Your first sentence-connector.
- **अतः** = अ + तः → a-ta-ḥ — /ɐt̪ɐh/ "hence".
- **इतः** = इ + तः → i-ta-ḥ — /it̪ɐh/ "from here".
- **कुतः** = कु + तः → ku-ta-ḥ — /kut̪ɐh/ "from where?".
- **कः** = one block — /kɐh/ "who? which?" — the star of §16.
- **मतिः** = म + ति + ः → ma-ti-ḥ — /mɐt̪ih/ — lesson 1's मति wearing its dictionary ending (§13).

Six words, one shared skill: full vowel, then the rider — hum or breath — and stop.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — three drills, in order. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c02l06-signs-drill.mp3` (drills 1–2 + dictation track) and
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c02l06-word-list.mp3` (drill 3).

**Drill 1 — the nasal-hum ladder (anusvāra).** (1) Say English *ink* very slowly and freeze
just before the k: hold that nasal. That is [ŋ] — nose buzzing, back of tongue up, lips open.
(2) Now build the word: अ… slide into the hum… release into क: अं-क, अंक [ɐŋkɐ]. Five times,
slow to natural. (3) Control test: try it *wrong* once on purpose — close your lips into [m]
before the क — and feel the clumsy jump from lips back to soft palate. The grid's answer
removes the jump: the hum happens *where the next sound already is*. (4) Finish with the hum
case: संस्कृतम् — dot before स, so no contact at all, just the nasal color on the vowel.

**Drill 2 — the visarga breath (palm test).** Hold a palm two finger-widths from your lips,
the other hand on your throat. (1) Whisper English *ha* — that airy texture, voiceless, is
the target. (2) अः — full vowel, then the breath: puff on the palm, zero buzz on the throat.
(3) The word-final series, one breath each: कः — ततः — अतः — इतः — कुतः — मतिः. Every word
must pass both checks: air on the palm (not dropped), no buzz after the vowel (not voiced).

**Drill 3 — the question-word rhythm.** With the word-list audio, chant the family in call
and response: कः? कुतः? — इतः! ततः? — अतः! ("Who? From where? — From here! Then? — Hence!")
Steady beat, visarga breath landing cleanly on every beat-end. Then read the full §14 list
twice — once slowly, block by block; once at speaking speed. Last on the audio, clearly
labeled, comes the recitation style: रामः with its echo [rɑːmɐhɐ]. Imitate it once, enjoy it,
and put it back on its shelf — it belongs to chanting, not to plain speech.

## 10. IPA

Today's signs break a pattern the course has followed so far: they get no single symbol.

**Anusvāra — transcribe contextually** (the course rule, from the STYLE_GUIDE table): [ŋ]
before the क-row, [ɲ] [ɳ] [n̪] [m] before their rows, and a nasalized vowel [◌̃] (or [m])
where no stop follows. This is why today's transcriptions appear in **brackets** — [ɐŋkɐ] —
rather than slashes: brackets record what actually comes out of the mouth, and with the dot,
*context decides* what comes out. The slash-vs-bracket distinction from Chapter 1 is finally
earning its keep.

**Visarga = /h/** — a voiceless glottal fricative: open vocal folds, pure breath. One preview
worth flagging now: the letter ह, waiting in Chapter 4, is /ɦ/ — the *voiced* glottal
fricative. Same place, opposite switch: ः whispers, ह buzzes. Hold that contrast; it will
keep your visarga honest when ह arrives.

**Narrow note.** The recitation echo writes itself naturally in brackets: रामः plain is
[rɑːmɐh], recited [rɑːmɐhɐ] — the added [ɐ] is the tradition's ornament (§5), never part of
the word.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — walkthrough of the diagram (planned:
`assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c02l06-homorganic-map.svg` — the stop grid with the dot feeding
each row's nasal, plus a breath panel for visarga).

**Anusvāra has no address of its own — it borrows the next sound's.** What is constant is the
*velum*: the soft palate lowers, opening the door to the nose. Checks:

1. **Nose buzz:** rest a finger lightly on the side of your nose and hum मं — feel the
   vibration. Every anusvāra must buzz there.
2. **Borrowed address:** say अंक in slow motion and freeze mid-hum. Where is your tongue? The
   back should already be rising to the क position — nose buzzing *and* tongue in place. If
   your lips are touching, you have defaulted to [m]: reopen them and re-run §9 drill 1.

**Visarga is the open door.** Tongue and lips stay exactly where the vowel left them; the
vocal folds stop buzzing and simply release breath. Checks:

1. **Palm:** two finger-widths from the lips — every visarga lands a small puff there.
2. **Throat:** fingers on the larynx — buzzing through the vowel, *silence* during the
   breath. If the buzz continues, you are saying a voiced "ha" (§12, mistake 1).
3. **Mirror (optional, satisfying):** breathe कः onto a mirror held close — the visarga is
   the little circle of fog.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **Voicing the visarga into a full "ha" syllable.** रामः becomes "rāma-ha", ततः becomes
   "tata-ha" — the vocal folds keep buzzing and a whole extra voiced syllable appears. *Fix:*
   the throat-hand test (§11): buzz through the vowel, silence during the breath. Whisper the
   ending until the habit sets, then restore normal volume to the rest of the word.
2. **Dropping the visarga entirely (Hindi habit).** Hindi has no visarga, so the eye skips
   it: ततः read as तत, मतिः as मति. In Sanskrit that deletes real information — the ḥ is part
   of the word. *Fix:* the palm test on every word-final ः; dictation (§18, exercise 3) will
   catch what the palm misses.
3. **Reading every anusvāra as [m] (romanization habit).** IAST ṃ looks like m, so learners
   close their lips wherever they see it: अंक becomes [ɐmkɐ], with a clumsy lip-to-velum jump.
   *Fix:* ṃ is an instruction, not a letter: "nasal — place decided by what follows." Before
   क the lips must stay open; run §9 drill 1's deliberate-error step and feel the difference.
4. **Echoing everywhere.** After hearing recitation style, learners add the echo-vowel in
   plain speech: every ततः becomes [t̪ɐt̪ɐhɐ], every word grows a syllable. *Fix:* two modes,
   clearly labeled — plain speech = breath only; recitation = echo, on purpose, as tradition
   (§5). Practice both *separately* so they never blend.

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — three word-families, worked in all four layers.

### The connector family — your first story-glue

**ततः** (tataḥ) "then, thereafter" — तब, उसके बाद · /t̪ɐt̪ɐh/
**अतः** (ataḥ) "hence, therefore" — अतः, इसलिए · /ɐt̪ɐh/
**इतः** (itaḥ) "from here" — यहाँ से · /it̪ɐh/
[REF:apte1890 s.v. ततः, अतः, इतः]

Until today, every word you could read was a *thing* — a mother, a garland, a lotus. These
three are different: they connect **sentences**. ततः moves a story through time ("then…"),
अतः through logic ("hence…"), इतः through space ("from here…"). Look at their skeletons: all
three end in the same तः piece, hung on a different pointer — a family resemblance you can
simply see on the page. (The suffix story behind it is Volume 4's; the *use* is yours today —
§17 runs on it.) A bonus for Hindi readers: अतः survives, unchanged, in formal Hindi prose.

### The question pair

**कः** (kaḥ) "who? which?" — कौन · /kɐh/
**कुतः** (kutaḥ) "from where?" — कहाँ से · /kut̪ɐh/
[REF:apte1890 s.v. कः, कुतः]

कः is the question word you have been *hearing* since Chapter 1's classroom dialogue — एषः कः
वर्णः? "which letter is this?" — and today its key word finally moves from your ear to your
eye (§16). कुतः is its "from where?" sibling — and notice it answers rhyme-perfectly with
इतः: कुतः? इतः! "From where? From here!"

### मति puts on its dictionary coat

**मतिः** (matiḥ) "thought, mind" — मति, बुद्धि · /mɐt̪ih/ [REF:apte1890 s.v. मतिः]

In lesson 1 of this chapter you read मति (mati). Dictionaries and word lists, though, cite
the word as मतिः — the *citation form*, the ending a Sanskrit noun wears when it stands alone
and names itself. That same naming ः is the one you have heard closing रामः, वर्णः and
अभ्यासः since lesson 1 — it was never decoration. One honest line and no more: a noun's
ending changes with its job in the sentence, and the full system — declension — is Volume 3's
story. For now, when a word list shows ः, read it, breathe it, and know it marks the word in
its Sunday best.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule**, and today's is about the riders.

### Where the signs live

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Anusvāra and visarga attach to the **akṣara as a whole**, not to the
> bare consonant: they are written after the consonant and its mātrā are complete, and read
> after the akṣara's vowel. कं reads ka-ṃ; कः reads ka-ḥ; मतिः reads ma-ti-ḥ, the ः riding
> the finished ति. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

- **ं** — one dot, floating **above the headline**, over the right shoulder of its akṣara.
- **ः** — two small circles, stacked like a colon, **after** the akṣara on the line — close
  companions, never touching the letter.

Stroke order is the easiest of the course — a dot and two circles — but *placement* and
*sequence* are strict. Extend Chapter 1's writing chant: **"letter, length, then the
riders."** Consonant first, mātrā second, sign last: क → का → कां; त → तः. A dot written
before the mātrā, or circles squeezed under the headline, are the errors the tracing sheet
(§18, exercise 2) exists to catch.

### What the signs are not

They are **not vowels**, and they are not counted as such — the inventory remains 13 + 33 + 2
(§4, §8). They behave like mātrās in one way only: they are dependent marks that ride a
finished akṣara. But a mātrā *replaces* the vowel; the riders *follow* it. का swaps the अ for
आ — कं keeps the अ and adds a hum after it. Keep the categories clean and the script stays
mechanical: every sign in देवनागरी now has exactly one job you can name.

### One honest preview

You now know two signs that modify an akṣara from outside. Chapter 5 introduces the last
family of script machinery — the virāma and consonant conjuncts like the ङ्क inside अङ्क —
and with it, the full spelling system. Nothing after that is new mechanics; it is all new
letters in old slots.

## 17. Story

कथा — ततः किम्? · Then What? The garland story from Chapter 1 continues — and for the first
time, a story *moves*, because you can read its connectors. Bold = readable; glue words are
glossed 🔊 below. Each line: Devanagari, IAST, English, Hindi.

1. **कमला** अत्र अस्ति। **मम** **माला**!
   (kamalā atra asti. mama mālā!) "Kamalā is here. — My garland!" — कमला यहाँ है। — मेरी माला!
2. **माता** — **कुतः** **माला**, **कमला**?
   (mātā — kutaḥ mālā, kamalā?) "Mother: From where — the garland, Kamalā?" —
   माँ — माला कहाँ से, कमला?
3. **कमला** — **इतः**!
   (kamalā — itaḥ!) "Kamalā: From here!" — कमला — यहाँ से!
4. **माता** — **ततः**?
   (mātā — tataḥ?) "Mother: And then?" — माँ — फिर?
5. **कमला** — **ततः** **मम** **कमल**-**माला**!
   (kamalā — tataḥ mama kamala-mālā!) "Kamalā: Then — my lotus-garland!" —
   कमला — फिर मेरी कमल-माला!
6. **माता** — आम्, **अतः** तव **माला**।
   (mātā — ām, ataḥ tava mālā.) "Mother: Yes — hence, your garland." —
   माँ — हाँ, इसलिए तुम्हारी माला।

Glue words 🔊 (all established in Chapter 1): अत्र (atra) "here" — यहाँ; अस्ति (asti) "is" —
है; आम् (ām) "yes" — हाँ; तव (tava) "your" — तुम्हारा. Everything else you read yourself.
Notice what changed since Chapter 1: that version of the story could only *point* — this one
has time (ततः), space (इतः, कुतः) and even logic (अतः). Four little words turned a scene
into a sequence. From now on, every course story runs on this glue — and you own it. Read the
story aloud twice: once block by block, once at story speed, every visarga passing the palm
test.

## 19. Quiz

प्रश्नाः — answers in the key at the end; no peeking mid-quiz.

1. Categorically, अं and अः are: (a) the 14th and 15th vowels (b) consonants of the क-वर्ग
   (c) ayogavāha — dependent sounds, neither vowel nor consonant (d) mātrās
2. Why are they not counted among the vowels? Answer in one sentence.
3. "Homorganic" means: (a) produced at the same place of articulation as the neighboring
   sound (b) having the same length (c) voiced in the same way (d) written with the same sign
4. In अंक the anusvāra is pronounced [ŋ] because: (a) the dot is always [ŋ] (b) क is a velar,
   so the nasal takes the velar place — the क-row's own fifth cell (c) the dot copies the
   *previous* sound (d) [ŋ] is easier to say
5. Visarga is: (a) a voiced sound like a full "ha" syllable (b) a voiceless /h/ — a breath
   after the vowel (c) silent, a purely written mark (d) a nasal hum
6. कं is read in which order? (a) ṃ then k then a (b) k then ṃ then a (c) k then a then ṃ
   (d) k then a — the dot is not read
7. अङ्क and अंक are: (a) two different words (b) two spellings of the same word, read
   identically (c) singular and plural (d) Sanskrit vs Hindi spellings of different sounds
8. Reading check — write the English meaning of each: (a) ततः (b) कुतः (c) कः

1. (c) ayogavāha — dependent sounds (§4, §8).
2. Because they cannot stand alone — they only ever occur leaning on a vowel that precedes

7. (b) two spellings of the same word, read identically — [ɐŋkɐ] (§8).

## 20. Revision

पुनरावृत्तिः — one screen to remember.

> **The category:** अं and अः are ayogavāha — dependent sounds; the honest count stays
> 13 + 33 + 2. **The dot:** before a stop, read the grid — the dot is that row's nasal
> (अंक → [ŋ]); no stop, nasal hum. **The breath:** ः = voiceless /h/ — palm feels it, throat
> never buzzes on it, plain speech never echoes it. **The order:** letter, length, then the
> riders — कं = k-a-ṃ, मतिः = m-a-t-i-ḥ. **The twins:** अङ्क = अंक, one reading. **The
> prize:** कः, कुतः, ततः, अतः, इतः, मतिः, अंक — question words, your first sentence-
> connectors, and every dictionary's naming-ending, all in one day.

Spaced review (distributed practice beats cramming [REF:cepeda2006]):

- **In 1 day:** re-take the dictation (exercise 3) cold; write the कं/कः series from memory;
  palm-test every final ः as you read §14 once.
- **In 1 week:** lesson 7 — the chapter assessment — retests every vowel *and* both riders,

  evening before.
- **From lesson 7 onward:** ततः and अतः appear in every course story from now on; every

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**This lesson closes a promise, and the class should feel it.** Lesson 2 of Chapter 1 parked
अं and अः in a labeled box "full treatment in Chapter 2"; open today by pointing at that very
box. Students who fought the "not 14 vowels" correction back then tend to convert here — the
homorganic rule is the *reason* the category exists, and deriving it feels like winning an
argument with your own textbook. Let them win it.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → hook: a projected verse page, students
circle every ं and ः they can spot (4) → visarga work: palm + throat tests, the voiceless/
dropped/voiced three-way contrast (9) → anusvāra: *ink*-freeze demo, the grid computes अंक on
the board (10) → word list + connector family reading in pairs (7) → exercises 1 and 4 (7) →
homework briefing, incl. the 1-day re-dictation (3).

**Board work:** keep the 5-row grid skeleton from Chapter 1 on the side board with only the
nasal column filled in (ङ ञ ण न म). Park a large ं above it. Every time the class meets the
dot, one student walks it to the correct row-cell. अंक gets solved there publicly; the §18
exercise 4 bonus (प-row prediction) gets solved there tomorrow.

**Dictation logistics:** read plain style, no echoes, natural speed, each word twice with a
10-second gap. Collect error *types*, not just scores: dropped ः, voiced ः, dot on the wrong
side or below the line, and [m]-for-[ŋ] in अंक each want different remediation (palm test,
throat test, tracing sheet, grid walk respectively).

**Differentiation — Hindi-literate learners:** the anusvāra costs them nothing (say गंगा and
अंदर, name what their tongue already does); their entire workout is the visarga, plus *not*
importing the Hindi dot-for-candrabindu habit into Sanskrit reading. Give them the recorder
role in pair work — judging a partner's palm test sharpens their own breath. English-habit
learners run the opposite ledger: the breath-h is easy (English *h* is close, just keep it
voiceless), and the homorganic idea is secretly native (*ink*!), but the IAST ṃ = "always m"
trap (§12, mistake 3) is theirs — drill the deliberate-error step of §9 drill 1.

**What students find hard:** hearing [ŋ] vs [m] before क (production first — the *feel* of
open lips is easier than the ear, and the ear follows); keeping the recitation echo out of
plain speech once they have heard it (name the two modes every time, "plain" vs "chant");
and believing the two circles of ः are a sound at all — the palm test is the converter, use
it early and often.
