## 1. Hook

Make a long hiss — sssss. Now the hushing sound that quiets a room — shhhh. English stops
counting at two. Sanskrit has three, and the difference between the two "sh"-sounds is no
experts-only mystery: it is exactly the difference your tongue drilled for two lessons in
Chapter 3 — the च-place versus the ट-place. Two addresses you already own, one new
instruction: hiss instead of touch. And a prize waits at the end of the hour: भाषा — the
word "language" itself — becomes readable. Today you write, with your own hand, the name
of the thing you are learning.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — the class with steam in its name.

Chapter 3 told the स्पर्श story: twenty-five sounds made by *contact*. Lesson 1 added the
in-between sounds. The tradition's final consonant class gives up contact altogether, and
its name says so with a physical image — breath forced through a narrow gap, like steam
through a kettle's spout.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** ऊष्मन् (ūṣman) means "heat, steam, hot breath." The ūṣman class —
> श ष स ह — is the tradition's name for the breath sounds. Three of the four are
> sibilants, today's letters; the fourth, ह, is a breath sound of a different kind (a
> voiced glottal fricative, not a sibilant) and gets the next lesson to itself.
> [REF:whitney1889 §§59–65]

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** Sanskrit's three-way sibilant contrast did not survive
> everywhere it traveled. Most Middle Indo-Aryan languages reduced it — Pali collapsed
> all three sibilants into a single s — and in modern spoken Hindi श and ष have merged
> into one sound, while the letters keep their separate seats in the script.
> [REF:masica1991]

The letters survived everywhere; the three sounds did not. Today the sounds get their
seats back.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — one manner, three addresses.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** श, ष and स are voiceless fricatives — hiss sounds: where a stop
> makes full closure, a fricative leaves a narrow channel, and air forced through it
> becomes turbulent noise. Their places are three old addresses: स /s/ is dental, the
> hiss at the त-row's teeth; श /ɕ/ is palatal, the hiss at the च-row's arched dome;
> ष /ʂ/ is retroflex, the hiss at the ट-row's curled-back position.
> [REF:ladefoged2015], [REF:whitney1889 §§59–65]

This is why Chapter 3 was the hard part and today is the transfer. A fricative is a stop
that never quite closes — so every stop you own is a recipe: say च and freeze the tongue;
without moving anything, force breath through — out comes श. Say ट, freeze the curl,
hiss — ष. Say त, freeze at the teeth, hiss — स. Three sounds, zero new tongue positions.

Your ear gets a handle too: the farther back the constriction, the longer the resonating
pocket in front of it and the lower the noise. श is the *bright* hiss, ष the *dark,
hollow* one, स the plain sharp one — a pitch staircase you can hear yourself descend in
the triangle drill (§9).

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Everyday modern Hindi has merged श and ष completely in speech —
> both are pronounced alike, roughly as English "sh" — though the letters stay distinct
> in writing. Careful Sanskrit keeps all three sibilants apart, and IAST keeps them apart
> on paper: *śa* for श, *ṣa* for ष. Casual romanization that writes both as "sha" erases
> a real Sanskrit distinction — this course never does.
> [REF:masica1991], [REF:ohala1994]

## 8. English Explanation

Three letters, one manner, three addresses — then the words this chapter has been saving.

### The three cards — one hiss each

- **श (śa) /ɕ/ — the bright hiss, palatal.** Tongue body arched high toward the dome —
  the च-row's address, the ई doorway. Closest English anchor: the sh of *ship* — but
  English sh sits a step lower and flatter; श is higher and further forward, brighter,
  almost smiled.
- **ष (ṣa) /ʂ/ — the dark hiss, retroflex.** Tongue tip curled up and back past the
  ridge — exactly where ट lives. English has no clean anchor, and needs none: the recipe
  beats the anchor. Say ट, hold the curl, hiss. Hollower and darker than any English sh.
- **स (sa) /s/ — the plain hiss, dental.** The s of English *sun*, made a touch further
  forward, on the teeth where त lives. Today's one genuinely free card.

On paper the three stay just as distinct: IAST writes *śa*, *ṣa*, *sa* — the accents are
the tongue's address written down. If you have seen both श and ष romanized "sha" on
posters and menus, you have seen the merger in print; this course keeps them apart
everywhere, in both scripts.

### Worked readings

- **भाषा** = भा + षा → bhā-ṣā — /bʱɑːʂɑː/ "language". The course's own subject, readable
  at last.
- **ऋषि** = ऋ + षि → ṛ-ṣi — /r̩ʂi/ "seer". The vowel-letter ऋ standing as its own
  akṣara, then today's ष wearing the इ-mātrā.
- **पुरुष** = पु + रु + ष → pu-ru-ṣa — /puɾuʂɐ/ "person". Lesson 1's र and today's ष
  cooperating; every उ short and full.
- **सीता** = सी + ता → sī-tā — /siːt̪ɑː/ "Sītā". Two long vowels, two beats each.
- **सुख** = सु + ख → su-kha — /sukʰɐ/ "happiness". स at the teeth, ख with its puff.

### The honesty note — and a promise kept on schedule

Three famous words are *almost* yours: सत्य 🔊 (satya) "truth", शक्ति 🔊 (śakti) "power",
नमस्ते 🔊 (namaste). Each contains a conjunct — two consonants fused into one block (त्य,
क्ति, स्ते) — and conjuncts unlock in Chapter 5. Track the promise precisely: नमस्ते is
न + म + स्ते. The first two blocks have been yours for chapters; as of today you own
every *letter* in the third — स, त, and the ए-mātrā. What you still cannot write is the
join itself: स् leaning vowel-less against ते. Chapter 5 opens with exactly that tool.
You are one move away.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — four drills, in order. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c04l02-drill.mp3` (drills 1–3), 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c04l02-sh-pair-test.mp3` (drill 4 + exercise 2), 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c04l02-word-list.mp3` (§14 words + dictation track).

**Drill 1 — hold-and-hiss (the transfer).** Three rounds each: (1) say च, freeze the
tongue mid-contact, push breath through the frozen position — श emerges; (2) say ट,
freeze the curl, hiss — ष; (3) say त, freeze at the teeth, hiss — स. Then chain: च-श,
ट-ष, त-स, feeling the tongue never move within a pair. This drill *is* the lesson: every
sibilant is a Chapter 3 address with the contact loosened.

**Drill 2 — the s-triangle.** One slow breath: स → श → ष, walking the tongue from teeth
to dome to curl; then back down. Five rounds. Listen as much as you feel — the hiss
*falls in pitch* as you walk back: plain, bright, dark. If two corners sound the same,
the tongue did not move; return to drill 1.

**Drill 3 — the ladders.** With the drill audio, read each pair aloud, exaggerating the
contrast first, then relaxing: शा-षा · शि-षि · शु-षु (*śā-ṣā · śi-ṣi · śu-ṣu*). Then the
mixed row: सा (sā) — शा (śā) — षा (ṣā). Keep the vowel identical so only the hiss changes.

**Drill 4 — ear training.** Play the pair-test track 🔊: syllables and short words,
brighter or darker. Do not answer yet — listen twice with the transcript hidden, saying
"bright" or "dark" aloud. The scored version is exercise 2; the ear usually arrives a day
before the tongue, and that is normal.

## 10. IPA

The three new values, from the STYLE_GUIDE inventory:

- **श /ɕ/** — voiceless alveolo-palatal fricative: the fricative half of the च-row — the
  affricate /t͡ɕ/ *ends* in exactly this hiss.
- **ष /ʂ/** — voiceless retroflex fricative, /ʈ/'s hissing sibling: same curled-tip
  address, channel instead of contact.
- **स /s/** — voiceless dental sibilant, at the teeth with the त-row.

**Where English "sh" sits:** the sound of *ship* is [ʃ] — between the two Sanskrit
letters: flatter and lower than श [ɕ], not curled like ष [ʂ]. It appears in this course
only inside brackets, as a reference point; no Sanskrit phoneme corresponds to it.

**Narrow notes:** भाषा is [bʱɑːʂɑː] — the retroflex hiss between two long back vowels
makes the darkness easy to hear. ऋषि is [r̩ʂi] — syllabic r, then the curl held for ष.
All three sibilants are voiceless: the throat-hand stays silent through every hiss.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — three stations, one instrument panel. The tongue-contrast diagram
(`planned:assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c04l02-sh-tongue-contrast.svg`) shows all three
cutaways side by side.

1. **स station (teeth):** tongue tip up at the upper teeth, a hair short of touching; a
   thin jet of air over the tip — feel the cool stream on a fingertip at the lips.
2. **श station (dome):** say a silent long ई and hold — the tongue body is already at
   the palatal address. Keep it there and hiss. That is v1.c03.l02's ई doorway reopened:
   इ, ई, the च-row and श share one house.
3. **ष station (curl):** run v1.c03.l03's ridge walk — tip behind the teeth, back past
   the bump, curled toward the dome. Hold the curl a hair open and hiss around it.
4. **The panel:** throat-hand silent on all three (voiceless breath only); jaw almost
   still — only the tongue commutes; the ear as third gauge: plain at the teeth, bright
   at the dome, dark at the curl.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with a fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **श = ष — the merger (the Hindi habit, and this lesson's flagship).** Everyday Hindi
   says one sound for both letters, so भाषा and शत come out with the same hiss and a
   contrast Sanskrit depends on disappears. *Fix:* aim by tongue, not by ear — the
   ट-recipe (say ट, hold, hiss) recovers ष mechanically; drill the ladders (§9) until
   शा-षा stops feeling like one syllable twice.
2. **English "sh" for both (mostly English-habit learners).** [ʃ] sits *between* the two
   targets — near श but flatter, nowhere near ष's curl — so one English sh papers over
   both letters. *Fix:* split it in two directions: for श, smile and arch higher (start
   from silent ई); for ष, abandon [ʃ] entirely and build from ट.
3. **ष without the curl.** Under time pressure the tip drifts forward and ष decays into
   [ʃ] or स — विष slides toward विस. *Fix:* the freeze-check — stop mid-word on the ष
   and hold: is the tip actually past the ridge? पुरुष and दोष are the test words.
4. **ष written as य (the shape trap).** Both hang a rounded body on the same stem — a
   hurried ष loses its diagonal sash and drifts toward य (or प). *Fix:* the sash *is*
   the letter: प's bowl plus one clean diagonal = ष. In dictation, check every ष for its
   sash, every य for its empty bowl (§15).

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — four small stories the word list is hiding.

### शत and दश — the number receipts

**शत** (śata) "hundred" — सौ · /ɕɐt̪ɐ/ · **दश** (daśa) "ten" — दस · /d̪ɐɕɐ/

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit शत stands beside Latin *centum* and English *hundred*;
> दश beside Latin *decem* and English *ten*. These are parallel inheritances from a
> shared ancestor language — sister words descending side by side, not one language
> borrowing from, or giving birth to, another. [REF:mallory2006]

The cognate table of v1.c01.l01 paid its first installments in Latin letters; today two
of its rows come home to Devanagari, and you can *read* the kinship.

### शिव — the adjective before the name

**शिव** (śiva) "auspicious, kind" — मंगलकारी; शिव · /ɕiʋɐ/

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** As a dictionary word, शिव is an adjective: "auspicious, gracious,
> kind, friendly." [REF:apte1890 s.v. शिव]

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** In the Purāṇic and devotional traditions, Śiva is the name
> of the great god — the auspicious one par excellence. In this lesson the word is
> vocabulary: an adjective doing honest secular work, with the name resting on it.

### ऋषि — the poster word comes home

**ऋषि** (ṛṣi) "seer" — ऋषि · /r̩ʂi/

In v1.c02.l03 this word taught you the vowel ऋ — you could say it long before you could
spell it. Today its other half arrives: ऋ + षि, two akṣaras, fully readable. And that ष
is no accident — after sounds like ऋ, Sanskrit's स regularly turns retroflex by a tidy
sound law whose full story waits for Volume 3. For now, enjoy the loop closing: the /r̩/
poster word, spelled at last, wearing today's letter.

### भाषा — the word for what you are doing

**भाषा** (bhāṣā) "language, speech" — भाषा · /bʱɑːʂɑː/

Two akṣaras, two long vowels, one dark retroflex hiss in the middle. Hindi did not
inherit this word by slow descent — it *borrowed* it straight from Sanskrit, spelling
intact (a tatsama, "same-as-that" loan), which is why your eyes already know it. From
today, when this course says "the language," you can write which one.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule**: three new letters, one look-alike
trap, and the class ledger updated.

### Stroke order — श, ष, स

Course convention as ever — body first, stem next, headline last; the stroke card
(`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c04l02-stroke-order.svg`) is the authority, and
handwriting traditions vary in details.

- **श** — the small left curl first: a tight loop hanging just under headline height;
  then the body — the open bowl sweeping right and down into the stem-line, with the
  short tail flicking down-left from its base; then stem, headline. The closed curl is
  श's signature.
- **ष** — write प's bowl first; then the sash — one clean diagonal from the bowl's upper
  right down through to its lower left; then stem, headline. Think of the sash as the
  tongue-curl made visible: प with a slash is ष, and the slash is what your tongue does.
- **स** — from below the headline, curve in and tie the small loop at mid-left; then
  release the line down and rightward along the base into the stem; then stem, headline.
  **स vs म:** म ties its loop down at the lower-left corner; स hangs its loop at
  mid-height. Loop low = म, loop high = स.

### The look-alike watch: ष vs य

Lesson 1's य and today's ष hang similar rounded bodies on the same right-hand stem. The
whole difference is interior: य's bowl is *empty*, entered by its small left-side wave;
ष's bowl wears the diagonal sash. A ष that loses its sash reads as य (or drifts toward
प); a य that grows one becomes ष. Dictation (§18, exercise 3) checks exactly this.

> **The triangle:** स (teeth, plain) → श (dome, bright) → ष (curl, dark) — one manner,

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**One summit today: the contrast.** Everything else — स, the writing, the vocabulary —
is support. If students leave able to produce and hear श vs ष on demand, the lesson
succeeded; polish can follow.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → hook: sssss/shhhh, "Sanskrit has
three" (3) → transfer work: hold-and-hiss from च and ट, then the triangle (10) → ear
work: bright/dark listening, then the pair test as a class game on mini whiteboards
(6) → writing श ष स + the ष/य watch, board demo and air-writing (8) → word list in
pairs; the भाषा and ऋषि moment named aloud (6) → dialogue + story, choral then solo
(4) → homework briefing incl. the 1-day retest (3).

**Stage the flagship for Hindi speakers without condescension.** Say it plainly: "Your
script already knows these three letters — your everyday speech keeps two of the sounds.
That is normal language change, not an error. Sanskrit needs the third sound back, and
your tongue already knows where it lives — you learned ट's address two chapters ago."
Then run the ट-recipe and let the room *hear* ष reappear. The recipe converts; lecturing
does not.

**Differentiation:** Hindi-literate learners write all three glyphs fluently — load them
with the ear work and the recorder role; their pair-test scores are the honest metric.
English-habit learners usually hear the contrast quickly but produce [ʃ] for both — send
them through silent-ई for श and the ट-freeze for ष separately, never "fixing" both at
once. Heritage learners from reciting families may already produce a clean ष — make them
the model voices for drill 2, then hand them the स-cleanliness check (no "sh"-creep
before इ) as their own challenge.

**What students find hard:** believing the third sound exists (the triangle's audible
pitch staircase convinces); holding the tongue still during hold-and-hiss (have them
freeze silently for a full second first); ष's curl decaying mid-word (freeze-checks on
पुरुष and दोष); and the ष/य sash in fast dictation — keep the pair on the board.
