## 1. Hook

Two letters were certified in Chapter 3 and have been waiting ever since. ङ has never once
appeared inside a word you read — not in 214 words. ञ managed a single cameo, dissolved past
recognition inside the ligature ज्ञ, where you had to be *told* it was there. Every other
letter in the varṇamālā has worked for its keep; these two have sat on the bench for three
chapters. Today you find out why — the reason is a rule, not an accident — and then both go
to work at once: in the name of India's great river, in the number five, and in the longest
words you have ever read.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिकः सन्दर्भः — the two unemployed letters, and the names on today's list.

Chapter 3 built the varga table five cells at a time and promised that every cell earns
its seat. For ङ and ञ the promise came with an IOU: sounds, shapes, slots — and then
silence, because the places they work were still locked. Chapter 5 built the lock-pick
(the conjunct weld); today the doors open. The tradition's own terminology hints at where
they live:

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Traditional grammatical terminology calls the fifth member of each
> varga the पञ्चम (pañcama) — "the fifth". The nasals are, by name, row-members: each
> keeps house beside its own stops. [REF:apte1890 s.v. पञ्चम]

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** The script this course teaches was long known simply as नागरी
> (nāgarī), "the urban (script)", from नगर (nagara) "city". The देव- prefix that yields
> देवनागरी (devanāgarī) appears later, and why it was added — what the full name was first
> meant to say — is debated; scholars decline to certify a single story.
> [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

Register discipline in one line: we can teach you to *read* देवनागरी today, and still say
plainly that the biography of its name is uncertain.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिकं विवरणम् — one rule of the mouth, one rule of practice.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Within a word, the nasal written before a stop is that stop's own
> varga nasal: ङ before the k-row (गङ्गा), ञ before the c-row (पञ्च), ण before the ṭ-row,
> न before the t-row, म before the p-row. The nasal shares the stop's closure — the tongue
> is already in position, and the nose simply opens first. This is why ङ and ञ almost never
> stand alone: their workplace is the conjunct, pressed against their own row.
> [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

You already own this fact with your ears: v1.c02.l06 taught you that the anusvāra dot before
a stop is *read* as that stop's class nasal. Today runs the same rule through the letters
themselves — the dot was an abbreviation; ङ्ग and ञ्च are the full spelling.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Fluency is its own learning strand: timed re-reading of *known*
> material builds automatic recognition, and it is a different activity from learning new
> material. Recognition that costs no attention frees the mind for meaning — which is what
> reading is for. [REF:nation2013]

That second fact is the design brief for this whole chapter. Nothing in today's words is new
machinery; every drill below takes parts you own and runs them faster.

संस्कृतेन विवरणम् — three lines, every part certified:

> **गङ्गा वहति।**
> (gaṅgā vahati.) "The Gaṅgā flows." — गंगा बहती है।

> **विद्यालयः अत्र अस्ति।**
> (vidyālayaḥ atra asti.) "The school is here." — विद्यालय यहाँ है।

> **अहम् संस्कृतम् वदामि।**
> (aham saṃskṛtam vadāmi.) "I speak Sanskrit." — मैं संस्कृत बोलता/बोलती हूँ।

वहति is glossed whole-word, as verbs have been since Chapter 3. In the third line संस्कृतम्
is the thing spoken — for a neuter word the form is the same either way, so your eye meets
nothing new; the system behind such endings is Volume 3's subject. And notice what the line
*is*: the first Sanskrit sentence in this course in which you read the course say its own
name.

## 8. English Explanation

Nothing new arrives today — that sentence will open every lesson of this chapter, and it is
the point. The two idle letters, the rule that employs them, and then the long words.

### The rule that hires ङ and ञ

Say गङ्गा slowly: ga — ṅ — gā. Between the two g-sounds sits a nasal, and not just *any*
nasal — [ŋ], made at exactly ग's own back-of-tongue station. Now पञ्च: pa — ñ — ca; the
nasal before च is [ɲ], at च's own station. That is the whole rule (§5, formally §15):
**inside a word, the stop chooses its own nasal** — the one from its own varga row. The
tongue is already parked where the stop needs it; the nose opens a beat early, then the
stop fires.

And there is the answer to three chapters of unemployment: this rule is ङ and ञ's entire
job description. They occur pressed against their own row's stops — and "pressed against"
in Devanagari means *conjunct*. A letter whose only work is cluster work will almost never
stand alone on the line. The bench was not neglect; it was scheduling.

### The stacks — Chapter 5's empty shelf fills

v1.c05.l02 named three conjunct patterns and left one nearly empty: vertical stacking.
Today it fills. ङ has no vertical stem for the horizontal join to shave, so it stacks —
ङ् above, partner below: ङ्ग (ṅga), ङ्क (ṅka), ङ्घ (ṅgha). ञ gives up its lower body and
settles flat onto च: ञ्च (ñca). Fonts vary in the details; the encoding underneath is the
same old story — C₁ + ् + C₂, the virāma invisible inside the stack exactly as inside स्त.
Read the new blocks in words: **गङ्गा** (gaṅgā), **अङ्गम्** (aṅgam), **सङ्घः** (saṅghaḥ),
**पञ्च** (pañca). And one display-only stretch: **भगवद्गीता** (bhagavadgītā), whose द्ग is
the plain Law-6 weld of द् + ग — no nasal involved, shown only to prove the mechanism
scales to famous names. It stays off your vocabulary list.

### Two spellings, one reading

Modern print often abbreviates the class nasal to the anusvāra dot: you will meet गंगा on a
newspaper page where a classical text prints गङ्गा (gaṅgā). Both spell the same sounds; you
read them identically — the pair you have known since Chapter 3's अंक (aṃka) and अङ्क
(aṅka), now scaled to real words. This course writes the classical conjunct and asks you to
recognize both without blinking. One boundary keeps the idea honest: the convenience trade
concerns a nasal before a **stop**. In संस्कृतम् the dot stands before स — a sibilant — and
there the anusvāra *is* the classical spelling: not a shortcut, the word's correct dress
(§15).

### The long words

The rest of the list needs no new signs — only nerve. The trick is the course's oldest one,
now with a number attached: **count the blocks first, then read the blocks, then the
word.** म-हा-भा-र-त-म् is six blocks; रा-मा-य-ण-म् is five; so are उ-प-नि-ष-द् and
दे-व-ना-ग-री. Every block is Chapter-4 easy. And संस्कृतम् — written component by component
at Chapter 5's closing ceremony — is four blocks carrying four chapters: anusvāra
(Chapter 2), स्क weld (Chapter 5), ृ vowel-sign (Chapter 2), final virāma (Chapter 5). You
have written it slowly. Today you read it fast — that is the whole difference between a
ceremony and a skill.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — nothing new in the mouth; the work moves to the eye and the clock. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c06l01-drill.mp3` (drills 1–3),
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c06l01-word-list.mp3` (three speeds + dictation and

**Drill 1 — the nasal ladder.** Read across with the audio, nose opening before each stop:
ङ्ग ङ्क ङ्घ ञ्च (ṅga · ṅka · ṅgha · ñca). Then inside words, block by block: अ-ङ्ग-म् —
अङ्गम्; ग-ङ्गा — गङ्गा; स-ङ्घः — सङ्घः; प-ञ्च — पञ्च. No vowel inside any stack — the
Chapter 5 seam discipline unchanged.

**Drill 2 — the count race.** Before reading each word, say its block count aloud:
महाभारतम् — six; रामायणम् — five; देवनागरी — five; विद्यालयः — four. Where the count is
five, shout it in Sanskrit: पञ्च! Counting first forces the eye to see blocks, not
letter-soup — this chapter's saccade drill, as top-scanning was the repha's.

**Drill 3 — three speeds.** The §14 list three times with the audio: (1) slow, block by
block, every seam checked; (2) natural pace, whole words; (3) target pace, against the
clock. Log the third time; the bar is in exercise 5. Speed never buys back a clipped
vowel: *pañca* full, every time.

**Drill 4 — the grid.** Print the speed grid ✍ (§18 exercise 6): twenty-five known words
in a 5×5 field; the audio calls twelve in random order; your finger lands on each within
three seconds. Timed recognition on known material only — fluency-strand practice in its
purest form (§5).

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थितिः — the articulators already know today's material; these checks prove it to you.
The map asset (`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c06l01-nasal-map.svg`) shows the varga
table with the fifth column lit and arrows into today's words.

1. **The shared-closure check.** Say गङ्गा in slow motion and freeze between the blocks:
   the back of the tongue is touching for ङ — and it is *still touching* for ग. One
   closure, two sounds, nose open then closed. Now पञ्च: the middle of the tongue holds
   च's station through the ञ. If you feel the tongue re-park between nasal and stop, you
   inserted a vowel — re-run drill 1.
2. **The eye check — taller blocks.** Stacks grow downward: ङ्ग and ञ्च are taller than
   their neighbors. Scan the *whole* block height before reading, the vertical twin of the
   repha's top-first scan.
3. **The count check.** Before any word of four blocks or more, count first (drill 2). If
   your eye is crawling letter by letter, the count resets it to block-vision.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्याः भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix; these feed the quiz distractors.

1. **"panch" — the Hindi five imported.** पञ्च loses its final अ and its ñ turns into a
   nasalized vowel. *Fix:* Sanskrit पञ्च has a full consonant ञ (tongue on च's station) and
   a full final vowel: *pañca*. The schwa-deletion discipline of v1.c01.l03 never retires.
2. **A vowel leaked into the stack:** गङ्गा read "gaṅagā", सङ्घः read "saṅaghaḥ". *Fix:*
   a conjunct has no vowel inside — Chapter 5's seam rule applies to stacks exactly as to
   स्त. Drill 1 slowly, jaw still between the members.
3. **The wrong nasal written in dictation:** गन्गा or गम्गा for गङ्गा. *Fix:* the stop
   chooses its own nasal — before ग only ङ is possible within a word. You never have to
   guess; you have to *ask the stop* (§15).
4. **Treating गंगा and गङ्गा as different words or readings.** *Fix:* one word, one
   reading, two spellings — dot as convenience, conjunct as classical (§8). The course
   writes the conjunct; the world prints both; your eye accepts both.
5. **Letter-crawl on long words:** महाभारतम् attacked one letter at a time until the
   mātrās blur. *Fix:* count blocks first, then read blocks (§9 drill 2). Six easy blocks
   are not one hard word.

## 13. Examples

### संस्कृतम् — the course's name, at reading speed

**संस्कृतम्** (saṃskṛtam) — सं-स्कृ-त-म्: anusvāra, weld, vowel-sign, virāma — four
chapters in four blocks. You heard this word in v1.c01.l01; you wrote it, component by
component, at Chapter 5's closing ceremony. Today it joins your reading list at full speed.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** संस्कृत is a past participle: सम् (sam) "together" + कृत (kṛta)
> "made", with an inserted -s- — "put together, refined, polished". The language's own name
> describes a finished, deliberate thing. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. saṃskṛta]

### पञ्च and *five* — siblings, not loans

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit पञ्च, Greek *pénte*, and English *five* are parallel
> descendants of one Proto-Indo-European word, reconstructed \*pénkʷe. Cognates — siblings
> from a shared ancestor — not loans in any direction. [REF:mallory2006]

The lesson-1 tool, third outing: *karma* was handed to English whole (a loan); चक्र and
*wheel* are cousins; पञ्च and *five* are cousins too. The Sanskrit *names* of the numbers
beyond पञ्च are Volume 2 Chapter 3's subject — today पञ्च is a reading word that happens
to be the best count-race shout in the language.

### Built words — नमस्कारः and विद्यालयः

- **नमस्कारः** = नमः (namaḥ) "a bow" + कारः (kāraḥ) "a making" — "the making of a bow",
  नमस्ते's formal sibling [REF:apte1890 s.v. नमस्कार]. You met the -कार element in
  Chapter 5: the grammarians' ककार (kakāra), "the k-maker". Same worker, warmer job.
- **विद्यालयः** = विद्या (vidyā) "learning" + आलयः (ālayaḥ) "abode" — "abode of learning",
  the mold of Chapter 4's हिमालय, "abode of snow". As a word for "school" it is standard
  *modern* Sanskrit usage — the term on real school gates today — and we label it as that.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule** section: no new signs, but the rule that
finishes the nasal system.

### The homorganic-nasal rule, formally

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** (1) Within a word, the nasal before a stop is that stop's own varga
> nasal — ङ before k-row stops, ञ before c-row, ण before ṭ-row, न before t-row, म before
> p-row. (2) In writing, these pairs form conjuncts — for ङ and ञ, vertical stacks: ङ्ग,
> ङ्क, ङ्घ, ञ्च — which is why the two letters rarely stand alone. (3) Modern convenience
> spelling may abbreviate the class nasal to the anusvāra dot (गंगा for गङ्गा); both
> spellings are read identically, and this course writes the classical conjunct. (4) Before
> a sibilant — as in संस्कृतम् — the anusvāra is not an abbreviation but the classical
> spelling itself. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

The encoding, as always, keeps sound order: गङ्गा is typed ग + ङ + ् + ग + ा; the stack is
the renderer's costume, and the virāma sits invisibly inside — Law 6 with no amendments.

### What this rule does for your writing

In dictation you never guess a nasal-before-stop: **ask the stop.** Hear *gaṅgā*, see the ग
coming, and the nasal writes itself — ङ, no other. Hear *pañca*, and the च hands you ञ. One
decision in the language costs zero decisions in your notebook.

### Pause-form dress, unchanged

Today's list wears the sentence forms you will read all chapter: neuters in म् (अङ्गम्),
masculines in ः (सङ्घः, विद्यालयः), feminine words in their own long vowels (गङ्गा,
वर्णमाला, देवनागरी). Questions still rise on the voice, sentences still end in the daṇḍa,
and role-changing endings are glossed whole-word until Volume 3 teaches the system.

## 22. Teacher Notes

अध्यापकटिप्पणी

**Open with the bench, not the rule.** Put the varga table on the board and ask which
letters the class has never read inside a word. Someone will find ङ and ञ; let the room
feel the oddity first. Then the rule lands as a *solution* — the stop chooses its own
nasal, so these two only work in conjuncts — and the three-chapter wait turns from neglect
into good scheduling. That narrative beat is the lesson.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → the bench + the rule; ङ्ग ञ्च built
on the board, अंक/अङ्क recalled (8) → गंगा/गङ्गा honesty + the संस्कृतम् boundary case (4)
→ word lab in pairs: the twelve, count-first protocol, seam policing (10) → count races as
a whole-class game, पञ्च! shouts encouraged (5) → three-speed reading with the audio; grid
race if time allows (8) → exercises 1–2 launch, homework, Chapter 5 +1-week reminder (5).

**Run the count race as call-and-response.** Hold up a word card; the class shouts the
count, one student reads the word. The shout forces block-vision on everyone — and पञ्च
doing double duty as answer and vocabulary is the kind of joke that makes a word permanent.

**Differentiation:** Hindi-literate learners read गंगा, संघ, नमस्कार on sight — their work
is the classical layer: writing the conjuncts and *explaining* the dot-versus-stack trade
(deputize them for the teach-back). Watch their पञ्च: "panch" clips two things at once.
Script-newcomers have the opposite problem — long words trigger letter-crawl; drill the
count-first protocol until it stops. The dictation separates the groups honestly: with §15
nobody should miss a nasal choice — misses there are rule-gaps, not speed-gaps.

**What students find hard:** believing गंगा and गङ्गा are identical in the mouth (read
both aloud, ask which was which — they cannot tell, which is the point); keeping vowels
out of ङ्घ (breathy घ invites "saṅaghaḥ"); the boundary case (expect "so the dot is always
informal?" — correct with the rule's own wording); and pacing — the 45-second bar tempts
clipping, and the standing line holds: speed never buys back a dropped अ.
