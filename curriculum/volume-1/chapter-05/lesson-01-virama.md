## 1. Hook

Since the third lesson of this course you have been carrying a secret debt. Every consonant
you have ever written says अ whether you want it or not — क is *ka*, म is *ma*; you could
write कमल, but you could not write a bare *k* if your life depended on it. And the word for
your own self — अहम्, "I", an ear-word since Chapter 1 — has waited behind that one missing
move. Today the script pays its debt: one small stroke, the smallest mark you will ever
learn, and the inherent vowel dies on command. The first word you write with it is yourself.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — the stroke with two names.

The tradition names today's sign twice — once for what it does, once for what it leaves
behind. And this course's English nickname for it, the vowel-killer, is only a blunt
translation of the first name.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The stroke ् that cancels a consonant's inherent अ is called विराम
> (virāma) "stop, cessation, pause" — the sign stops the vowel. A word or letter it
> produces — one ending in a bare consonant — is called हलन्त (halanta) "ending in a
> consonant", and that name is commonly used for the sign itself as well.
> [REF:whitney1889 §9]

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The second name is a technical term of the Pāṇinian
> grammatical tradition: there हल् (hal) is the compressed cover-label meaning "any
> consonant", so हलन्त (halanta) says in one word "consonant-final". Look closely at the
> label हल् — it ends in the very stroke it helps to name.

One honest expectation before the drills. Sanskrit does not end its words in bare
consonants very often: open any page of this course and the word-endings run heavily to
vowels and to the two leaning signs ः and ं, which you have owned for chapters. The
virāma-words are a steady minority — but the minority includes everyday essentials like
अहम् "I", किम् "what?" and तत् "that", so you will meet the stroke daily. Where the virāma
secretly does most of its work is *inside* words, invisibly — that is its second job,
and tomorrow's whole lesson.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — the bargain completed.

Recall the design argument from v1.c01.l03 §5. The vowel अ /ɐ/ is the cheapest vowel to
produce — the mouth nearly at rest — and the most frequent vowel of the language; so the
script builds it into every consonant letter free of charge, and the common case,
consonant + अ, costs a single letter. Economical. But every engineering bargain has a
second page, and today you finally turn to it: **a default is only usable if you can also
switch it off.** A script that always says *ka* needs a way to say just *k* — otherwise
the word *aham* is unwritable forever.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Law 5 of the script: the virāma ् cancels the inherent vowel.
> क + ् = क् /k/ — the bare consonant, no vowel after it. On the page it is a short
> stroke slanting down to the right, attached at the foot of the letter it silences.
> Word-final bare consonants are written with it: अहम् (aham), तत् (tat), जगत् (jagat).
> [REF:masica1991 ch.6] [REF:whitney1889 §9]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** This completes the abugida's characteristic design: write the
> common case for free (consonant + अ), and mark only the departures — a different vowel
> with a mātrā, no vowel at all with the virāma. An alphabet like Latin spells every
> vowel every time; Devanagari spells only the exceptions to its default.
> [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

Notice how the vowel system of the script has now closed into three moves: **replace**
the default (the mātrās of Chapter 2), **lean on** the syllable (ं and ः), and — from
today — **delete** the default (्). Nothing about this is mystical; it is the same
economy you met in Chapter 1, now seen whole. The inherent-vowel logic has come full
circle: the free अ was never a gift, it was a loan — and ् is how the script settles it.

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतव्याख्या — the lesson's idea in the simplest possible Sanskrit — and the volume's
milestone lines. 🔊 recorded in `planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c05l01-sentences.mp3`.

अहम् वदामि।
(aham vadāmi.) "I speak." — मैं बोलता हूँ।

अहम् नमामि।
(aham namāmi.) "I bow." — मैं प्रणाम करता हूँ।

किम् तत्?
(kim tat?) "What is that?" — वह क्या है?

तत् जगत्।
(tat jagat.) "That is the world." — वह जगत् है।

Pause form as always: every word complete, a hair of silence between words. One honest
line for later: in connected speech Sanskrit habitually turns a final म् into ं before a
following consonant — अहम् वदामि flows as अहं वदामि (ahaṃ vadāmi). That joining habit is
sandhi, Volume 3's story: noted today, taught there. In this course's pause-form reading,
अहम् stays अहम् — final lips closed, full stop.

## 8. English Explanation

One stroke today — and then the sentence this volume has been walking toward.

### The stroke and the switch

क has never once, in four chapters, agreed to be just /k/. Add the stroke: क् (k). The
inherent vowel is gone — not softened, not shortened: gone. And here is the full-circle
moment: v1.c01.l03 §15 told you to read क as "consonant क् plus free अ" — which means you
have been *looking at* the virāma since your first writing lesson. Every time this course
printed a bare consonant — क्, स्, म् — the little stroke was already there, doing
exactly today's job. Today it is officially yours: क = क् + अ, and therefore क + ् = क्.

### Building a word: अ, ह, म — and the kill

Watch अहम् get built, piece by piece. Write अ — the independent vowel letter, because no
consonant stands before the vowel. Write ह — it carries its free अ, so the page now says
अह (aha). Write म — three akṣaras: अहम, reading *a-ha-ma*. Say it aloud: one vowel too
many. The word for "I" ends on closed lips — /ɐɦɐm/ — no final vowel. So kill it: one
small stroke under the final म, and अहम becomes **अहम् (aham) "I"**. That is the whole
mechanism. The stroke always sits under the letter whose vowel must die — here the last
one, and in every word of this lesson the last one.

### The word set — a question word, an answer word, a world

- **किम् (kim) "what?"** — the question family grows: कः (kaḥ) "who?" and कुतः (kutaḥ)
  "from where?" have carried your dialogues since Chapter 2; किम् now asks about things.
- **तत् (tat) "that"** — the answer word, किम्'s natural partner: किम् तत्? तत् जगत्।
- **सत् (sat) "being, real"** — small word, long future (§13).
- **जगत् (jagat) "world"** — your first final त् in a noun you will meet everywhere.
- **वाक् (vāk) "speech"** — a final क्, and a fitting word for the day you learn to say I.
- **भवान् (bhavān) "you (respectful)"** — a final न्. Learn it as a polite chunk said to
  a man; its full family (including the forms for and by women) is Volume 2 grammar.

### The verbs — and the first sentences

Three verbs join the roster, whole-word gloss as always: **वदामि (vadāmi) "I speak"**,
**नमामि (namāmi) "I bow"**, **वसामि (vasāmi) "I dwell"**. Notice all three end the same
way — that shape is no accident, and taking verbs apart is Volume 2's opening act; until
then, whole words, honestly labeled. One honest note now: a Sanskrit verb carries its
person inside it — वदामि alone is already a complete sentence, "I speak". अहम् makes the
"I" explicit and emphatic. Today we say both words anyway; ceremony demands it:

**अहम् वदामि। अहम् नमामि।** — the first sentences of this course in which the subject is
*you*. Every sentence until today was about रामः, गुरुः, बालकः — third persons, other
people. This is the milestone the chapter overview promised: the first-person unlock.
And a small bonus, promised word for word in v1.c04.l03 §16: आम् (ām) "yes" — the very
first word a गुरुः ever said to you in this course, back in v1.c01.l03's two-line
dialogue — needed only its final म् to become readable. The 🔊 marker comes off: आम् is
yours in print. The identity frame completes it: अहम् + your name — अहम् रामः! — the
verbless nominal sentence you have used since Chapter 3, now in the first person (§16).

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — the chapter's pivot: no new sounds anywhere in Chapter 5, so drill time moves
from articulation to **reading fluency and clean finals**. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c05l01-drill.mp3` (drills 1–3), 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c05l01-word-list.mp3` (word list + dictation track), 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c05l01-sentences.mp3` (drill 4 + §16 dialogue).

**Drill 1 — the cut.** Practice syllables, honestly labeled as drill units: अक् (ak) ·
अत् (at) · अम् (am). Say अ, close into the consonant — and stop there. The sound ends in
a closure, not in a release. Hand under the jaw: after the closure the jaw must not
bounce open again. If anything leaks out after the consonant, you released a vowel.

**Drill 2 — no echo.** The word set, each twice with the model: अहम् · किम् · तत् · सत् ·
जगत् · वाक् · भवान्. One discipline: you may remember that traditional recitation gives
the visarga an echo vowel (रामः chanted as *rāmaha* — a TRADITION-register practice,
v1.c02.l06). That echo belongs to ः **only**. Final म्, त्, क्, न् take no echo — ever.
*aham*, never "aham-uh".

**Drill 3 — spelling pairs.** Same letters, one stroke of difference — read each exactly
as written, bare column then killed column: अहम / अहम् · तत / तत् · किम / किम् · जगत /
जगत्. The left column reads with full final अ (*a-ha-ma*, *ta-ta*…); the right column
ends on the consonant. Only the right column contains real words today — the point is
that your eye must catch the stroke at speed. This drill seeds exercise 4's timed grid.

**Drill 4 — THE SENTENCES.** With the model audio, pause form: **अहम् वदामि। अहम्
नमामि। अहम् वसामि।** Then the identity line: अहम् — and your own name, aloud. Record the
final round; you will want this recording later in the course, the way you now treasure
your Chapter 1 six-letter writing sample.

## 10. IPA

No new sound values today — a new *absence*. The whole lesson in one bracket pair:
अहम [ɐɦɐmɐ] versus अहम् [ɐɦɐm] — the entire difference is one final [ɐ].

**Word transcriptions:** अहम् /ɐɦɐm/ · किम् /kim/ · तत् /t̪ɐt̪/ · सत् /sɐt̪/ · जगत्
/d͡ʑɐgɐt̪/ · वाक् /ʋɑːk/ · भवान् /bʱɐʋɑːn̪/ · वदामि /ʋɐd̪ɑːmi/ · नमामि /n̪ɐmɑːmi/ ·
वसामि /ʋɐsɑːmi/.

**Narrow notes:** a final [m] means the lips close and stay closed — no vowel rides the
release. Final [t̪] and [k] close cleanly and let go without any [ɐ]-colored puff after
them. भवान् ends in the dental nasal [n̪] — tongue on the teeth, air through the nose,
then silence. And the milestone sentence in pause form: अहम् वदामि /ɐɦɐm ʋɐd̪ɑːmi/ — a
closed [m], a hair of silence, then the verb.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — nothing new to place; everything to *hold*. The plate
(`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c05l01-virama-plate.svg`) shows the mechanism; your
mirror shows the discipline.

1. **Where each final ends.** म् — lips sealed, voice humming through the nose, then
   silence. त् and न् — tongue-tip on the teeth (the दन्त्य station of Chapter 3), one
   closed, one nasal. क् — tongue-back at the velum. The virāma does not change any
   articulation you own; it only removes what comes after.
2. **The freeze test.** Say अहम् and hold the final position, lips sealed, for two full
   seconds. Anything that escapes in those seconds is the echo vowel — the enemy of the
   week (§12). Repeat with तत् (tongue frozen on the teeth) and वाक् (tongue-back held).
3. **Mirror check.** Watch your jaw through अहम्, किम्, जगत्: it must not reopen after
   the final closure. A reopening jaw is a vowel being born — catch it in the glass.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — four real errors, each with a fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **The vowel echo: "aham-uh".** English habit releases final stops into a puff, and
   heritage recitation habit generalizes the visarga's echo to everything. Result: अहम्
   comes out *ahama* or "aham-uh" — the virāma undone by the mouth. *Fix:* the freeze
   test (§11) and drill 1's hand-under-jaw — the closure is the end of the word; the
   visarga echo belongs to ः alone.
2. **The stroke forgotten: अहम for अहम्.** The page then honestly reads *ahama* — the
   script never assumes a silent vowel. This is the exact mirror of the Hindi habit (§7):
   Hindi may drop an unwritten-off अ in speech; Sanskrit never does, so the writer must
   say it with ्. *Fix:* proofread every word's last letter — "did I hear a vowel there?
   No? Then where is the stroke?" Exercise 6 trains exactly this.
3. **The stroke under the wrong letter.** The virāma silences the letter it touches —
   put it under ह and you have written अह्‌म, which spells *ahma*, a different (and
   wrong) word. *Fix:* say the word slowly and find the silent consonant — it is the
   final one in every word of this lesson; the stroke lands there and nowhere else.
4. **The killer confused with the u-hook.** Both live below the letter, and beginners
   swap them: कु (ku) has the *curled* hook and adds a vowel; क् (k) has the short
   *straight* slash and deletes one. Opposite jobs. *Fix:* the contrast chip on the
   plate and stroke card — "curl adds u, slash kills a" — and quiz Q6.

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — three stories hiding in the word list.

### अहम् — the deepest cognate

**अहम्** (aham) "I" — मैं · /ɐɦɐm/

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit अहम्, Latin *ego*, and English *I* are cognates — all
> three descend from the Proto-Indo-European first-person pronoun \*eǵh₂, each shaped by
> its own line of regular sound changes. Cognates, not loans: nobody borrowed anybody's
> "I"; the three languages inherited the same word from their common ancestor.
> [REF:mallory2006]

Pronouns are among the words languages hold on to longest — and the first-person pronoun
sits at the very bottom of that vault. When you say *aham*, *ego* or *I*, you are
flexing one ancient word three ways. Fitting, for the day the word becomes writable.

### जगत् — the moving world

**जगत्** (jagat) "world" — जगत्, संसार · /d͡ʑɐgɐt̪/

One line, worth keeping: the dictionaries connect जगत् with the root गम् (gam) "go" —
the world named as "that which moves". [REF:apte1890 s.v. जगत्] A civilization's word
for the universe, and it means *the moving one*.

### तत् and सत् — two small words with large futures

**तत्** (tat) "that" — वह · /t̪ɐt̪/ · **सत्** (sat) "being, real" — सत् · /sɐt̪/

Today: a pronoun and a small noun-adjective, fully readable, doing honest daily work in
किम् तत्? and in your word list. Later: these two open and anchor some of the most
quoted sentences in Sanskrit literature — you will meet those sentences with their full
text, sources and proper registers in Volume 5. No preview quote here: this course cites
verses only when it can show you the whole verse and its locus. For now, enjoy owning
the words themselves.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule**: no new letter today, but the script's
fifth law and the smallest stroke you will ever write.

### Stroke order — the virāma

Course convention as ever — the stroke card
(`planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c05l01-stroke-order.svg`) is the authority.

- **्** — a single short stroke slanting down to the right, attached at the foot of the
  letter, roughly where the letter's body meets the baseline. Write it **last**: letter
  body, headline, then the kill. It never touches the headline and never grows long —
  half the height of a mātrā hook at most.
- **Three states of an akṣara, one foot rule.** क (inherent अ) · का (mātrā replaces the
  vowel) · क् (virāma deletes it). One akṣara carries exactly one of these states —
  inherent vowel, other vowel, or no vowel — never two at once. There is no such thing
  as *कु्*: a vowel sign and the vowel-killer contradict each other.
- **The look-alike warning, on paper.** कु curls; क् slashes. The u-hook bends back
  under the body like a little tail; the virāma is straight and brief. Slow writers
  never confuse them; hurried writers do (§12 mistake 4).

### The five laws — the script's constitution, complete for words

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** **Law 1 — inherent vowel:** an unmarked consonant reads with अ.
> **Law 2 — mātrā:** any other vowel after a consonant is written as a sign. **Law 3 —
> mātrā geography:** the sign's position is fixed by the script — right, left, below,
> above, composite — regardless of when the vowel is spoken. **Law 4 — the signs:**
> ं and ः attach to the whole akṣara and are neither vowels nor consonants. **Law 5 —
> virāma:** the stroke ् cancels the inherent vowel; a bare consonant, word-finally, is
> always written with it. [REF:masica1991 ch.6] [REF:whitney1889 §9]

Laws 1–4 are Chapter 2's constitution (v1.c02.l07 §15); today adds the fifth article.
For *single* consonants, the system is now complete: with, without, or with-another —
every vowel state of every consonant is writable by you.

### Tomorrow's door, honestly ajar

One case remains: two consonants with **no vowel between them inside a word** — the स्त
in नमस्ते. You could now write स् त — and you would be understood, but no printed page
does it that way: the script *fuses* such pairs into one sign. That fusion is the
conjunct system, tomorrow's whole lesson — and here is the secret worth carrying in:
the virāma you learned today is the invisible hinge inside every one of those fusions.

## 18. Exercises

अभ्यासाः — six exercises; 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are production tasks. Do them in order.

**अभ्यासः 1 · शब्द-निर्माणम् (word-building, production).** Build each word from its

**अभ्यासः 2 · श्रुतलेखनम् (dictation, production).** Play the dictation track 🔊 (in the
word-list audio): eight §14 words in random order, each read twice with a writing pause.
Write them in Devanagari. Self-check against §14 letter by letter, then run the §14
checking question on every final: *did I hear a vowel there? No? Where is my stroke?*
7/8 is a pass; re-take tomorrow either way (§20).

**अभ्यासः 3 · अनुलेखनम् (tracing & writing, production).** Print the tracing worksheet ✍
(`planned:assets/worksheets/volume-1/v1c05l01-tracing.pdf`): trace the virāma onto
क् म् त् न् five times each following the stroke arrows, then अहम्, किम्, तत्, जगत्,
भवान् unaided, then the sentence row — अहम् वदामि। अहम् नमामि। — daṇḍa included.

**अभ्यासः 4 · द्रुतपठनम् (the timed grid, reading).** The grid on the worksheet: twenty
cells mixing killed and unkilled spellings — अहम/अहम् · तत/तत् · किम/किम् · जगत/जगत् ·
कु/क् — in random order. Read the grid aloud exactly as written, left to right, in 60
seconds: bare column with full final अ, killed column ending on the consonant. Zero
misreadings is the bar; the drill from §9 (drill 3) was the warm-up for exactly this.

**अभ्यासः 5 · वाक्य-रचना (sentence assembly, record — production).** Word tiles: अहम् ·
वदामि · नमामि · वसामि · your own name with ः. (a) Assemble and write the three milestone
sentences: अहम् वदामि। अहम् नमामि। अहम् वसामि। (b) Write the identity line: अहम् + your
name. (c) Record yourself reading all four in pause form — final म् closed, a hair of
silence, no echo — and compare against the sentence audio 🔊.

**अभ्यासः 6 · शुद्धि-पत्रम् (proofreading, production).** The worksheet prints six words
as a careless scribe left them: अहम · किम् · तत · वाक् · भवान · सत्. Three are correct;
three have lost their virāma. Circle the wounded ones, write each correctly, and say
aloud what the uncorrected spelling would actually read as — अहम is honestly *ahama*,
and the script never lies (§12 mistake 2). This is the proofreader's reflex the rest of
the course will assume.

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**Stage the debt-payment.** The lesson's engine is four chapters of suppressed need:
learners have wanted to write अहम् since Chapter 1. Do not defuse it early. Open with §3,
then write अहम on the board and ask the room to read it honestly — someone will say
*a-ha-ma*, and the wrongness will be audible. Hold that discomfort for a beat; then add
one stroke and let them re-read. The law teaches itself in that moment; your commentary
afterwards only names it.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → the board moment above + Law 5
named (5) → drill 1–2: the cut and the no-echo discipline, hand under jaw (7) → writing:
stroke card, air-writing, exercise 1 word-building on slates or paper (8) → word list in
pairs, checking question aloud (6) → drill 3 pairs + exercise 4 timed grid, two runs (6)
→ THE SENTENCES: drill 4, then exercise 5 assembly and the identity line around the room
— every learner says अहम् + their own name aloud (6) → homework briefing incl. the
halanta hunt and the 1-day re-dictation (2).

**The echo is the week's real enemy.** English releases final stops; recitation habit
generalizes the visarga's echo. Both produce "aham-uh". The freeze test converts faster
than explanation: two seconds of enforced silence after the closure, hand under the jaw.
Insist that the echo belongs to ः alone — that framing (§9 drill 2) keeps the TRADITION
practice respected *and* contained.

**The अहं question is coming.** Any heritage learner will point out that everyone says
अहं वदामि. Answer exactly as §6 does: correct observation, real phenomenon, called
sandhi, taught in Volume 3; this course reads in pause form so that every word is heard
whole while the inventory is being built. Do not improvise beyond that — the honest
deferral is the course's standing policy.

**Differentiation:** Hindi-literate learners know the glyph passively (जगत्, भगवान्) —
promote them to proofreading referees for exercise 6, and load them with the no-echo
discipline instead. English-habit learners need the freeze test until it bores them.
Heritage chanters get the recorder role in exercise 5 and the pause-form bar: finals
closed, no echo, no sandhi yet.

**What students find hard:** believing the stroke is *load-bearing* (one missing slash
changes the word — exercise 6 exists to make that visceral); the कु/क् look-alike at
speed (slow the hand, not the eye); and reopening the jaw after final म् (the mirror
catches it; §11 check 3).
