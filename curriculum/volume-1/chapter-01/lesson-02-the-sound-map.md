## 1. Hook

Say the English alphabet: A, B, C, D… Why that order? No reason — history's accident, a chain
of borrowings from Phoenician traders onward, frozen by habit. Now watch the Sanskrit order:
क… च… ट… त… प. Say those five sounds slowly and pay attention to your tongue. It starts at
the back of your mouth and walks forward, step by step, until it reaches your lips. That is
not an accident. The varṇamālā is not a list — it is a **map of your mouth**. Today you learn
to read that map.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — where today's map comes from.

### The Śikṣā tradition: phonetics as an ancient science

Long before modern linguistics, Sanskrit scholars analyzed speech systematically. The Śikṣā
texts — versified phonetics manuals attached to the Vedic schools — describe where and how
each sound is produced, so that recitation could be transmitted without error.

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The Pāṇinīya Śikṣā organizes speech sounds by their point of
> articulation (sthāna); its verse 13 lists eight such places — chest, throat, head,
> tongue-root, teeth, nose, lips, and palate. [REF:paniniyashiksha 13]

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The Pāṇinīya Śikṣā counts the varṇas as "sixty-three or sixty-four"
> (verse 3), depending on which marginal sounds are included. This course uses the classical
> teaching inventory — 13 vowels + 33 consonants + 2 ayogavāha — and always says exactly what
> it is counting. [REF:paniniyashiksha 3]

The mouth-map you learn today is not a modern overlay — it *is* the tradition's own analysis,
confirmed by modern instruments (§5).

### A note on the script

One thing the varṇamālā is **not**: a script. The map is a map of *sounds*; Devanagari is just
one way of writing them down.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** Devanagari descends from the ancient Brāhmī script through its Gupta
> and Nāgarī stages. Over the centuries Sanskrit has been written in many regional scripts —
> Grantha in the Tamil country, Śāradā in Kashmir, Bengali script in the east, and more.
> Devanagari is today's dominant convention, not "the Sanskrit script". [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — the grid, as modern articulatory phonetics sees it.

A consonant is defined by (at least) two choices: **where** the airflow is obstructed (place
of articulation) and **how** (manner: voiceless or voiced, aspirated or not, oral or nasal).
The Sanskrit stops fill this two-dimensional grid completely — 5 places × 5 manners = 25.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The varṇamālā is ordered phonetically: vowels first, then stops by place
> from back to front — velar, palatal, retroflex, dental, labial — each row ordered voiceless,
> voiceless aspirated, voiced, voiced breathy, nasal; then semivowels; then sibilants and h.
> [REF:whitney1889 §19–75]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Modern articulatory phonetics confirms the traditional organization: the
> five places are real, measurable positions of the tongue and lips, and voicing, aspiration
> and nasality are exactly the parameters phoneticians use today. [REF:ladefoged2015]

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतव्याख्या — the lesson's idea in the simplest possible Sanskrit.

वर्णानां माला वर्णमाला।
(varṇānāṃ mālā varṇamālā.) "A garland of sounds — that is the varṇamālā." —
वर्णों की माला ही वर्णमाला है।

अ इति स्वरः। (a iti svaraḥ.) "*a* is a vowel." — अ स्वर है।

क इति व्यञ्जनम्। (ka iti vyañjanam.) "*ka* is a consonant." — क व्यञ्जन है।

The traditional names are arriving: स्वर (svara) "vowel", व्यञ्जन (vyañjana) "consonant", वर्ण
(varṇa) "speech sound" — the course's working vocabulary from today onward (§14).

## 8. English Explanation

The one idea of this lesson: **the varṇamālā is organized, not memorized.** Two questions
generate the whole consonant system — *where* is the sound made, and *how*?

**Where — the five places,** back of the mouth to the front: कण्ठ्य (kaṇṭhya) velar, तालव्य
(tālavya) palatal, मूर्धन्य (mūrdhanya) retroflex, दन्त्य (dantya) dental, ओष्ठ्य (oṣṭhya)
labial. The grid's rows are named after these — क-वर्ग "the k-row", च-वर्ग "the c-row", etc.

**How — the five manners.** Within each row, always the same walk: voiceless → voiceless
aspirated → voiced → voiced breathy → nasal. Learn the walk once and you know the internal
order of all five rows.

### The honest count: 13 + 33 + 2

**Vowels (स्वर): 13.**

| अ | आ | इ | ई | उ | ऊ | ऋ | ॠ | ऌ | ए | ऐ | ओ | औ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| /ɐ/ | /ɑː/ | /i/ | /iː/ | /u/ | /uː/ | /r̩/ | /r̩ː/ | /l̩/ | /eː/ | /ɐi̯/ | /oː/ | /ɐu̯/ |

(One curiosity: grammarians' lists also include a long ॡ as ऌ's theoretical partner, purely
for symmetry. It occurs in no attested word — mentioned here once, revisited briefly in v1.c02.)

**Consonants (व्यञ्जन): 33.** 25 stops (the five vargas) + 4 semivowels (य र ल व) + 4 in the
sibilant group (श ष स ह).

**Ayogavāha (अयोगवाह): 2.** The anusvāra अं (ṃ) and the visarga अः (ḥ).

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Anusvāra and visarga are ayogavāha — "dependent sounds". They are
> categorically neither vowels nor consonants: they cannot stand alone and only ever occur
> leaning on a vowel. [REF:whitney1889 §70–73]

You may have learned "14 vowels, including अं and अः" at school. That count is a classroom
convenience — the two are drilled alongside the vowels because they are written with
vowel-like signs — not a fact about the sounds. When anyone gives you a count, ask: *what
exactly are you counting?* This course's answer is always: 13 + 33 + 2.

**Why this matters for the next 138 lessons:** every sound lesson in Volume 1 drops its new
sound into this grid — once the map is in your head, each new letter is one new cell, not one
new mystery.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — today you pronounce the *map itself*: one scout sound from each place.

**The tongue walk** (🔊 planned: `assets/audio/volume-1/v1c01l02-five-places-demo.mp3`):

1. **क (ka) /k/ — velar.** Back of the tongue rises to the soft palate: closure far back, almost in the throat.
2. **च (ca) /t͡ɕ/ — palatal.** The tongue body presses the hard palate; the release is soft, with a slight hiss.
3. **ट (ṭa) /ʈ/ — retroflex.** Curl the tongue tip up and back; it taps the ridge well *behind* the teeth. Darker, hollower.
4. **त (ta) /t̪/ — dental.** Tip forward until it touches the *back of the upper teeth*. Lighter, crisper than ट.
5. **प (pa) /p/ — labial.** The tongue rests; the lips close and pop open.

Say क च ट त प slowly, five times. Each step, the closure moves forward; by the fifth round
you should *feel* the walk without thinking. This one drill installs the grid's whole
structure. Each sound is a preview, not a mastery target — every anchor is refined in Chapters 2–4.

## 10. IPA

Those symbols between slashes — /k/, /t̪/, /ʈ/ — are the International Phonetic Alphabet, the
modern standard for writing sounds unambiguously, one symbol per sound. This course gives
every Sanskrit sound its IPA value from a single fixed table (STYLE_GUIDE §2), so "how is this
pronounced?" always has one checkable answer. [REF:ladefoged2015]

**Slashes vs brackets.** /k/ names a *phoneme* — a sound category of the language, the thing
that distinguishes words. [kʰ] in brackets records *phonetic detail* — what actually comes out
of a mouth. English "pin" is phonemically /pɪn/ but phonetically [pʰɪn], with a puff of air
English speakers never notice — the same puff Sanskrit treats as a full letter (ख vs क).

**Why "as in English" always lies a little.** Anchors like "त as in *top*" are useful, never
exact: English *t* is alveolar — the tongue touches the gum ridge, *between* Sanskrit dental
त /t̪/ and retroflex ट /ʈ/. The anchor points at the neighborhood, not the house. This course's
rule: every anchor states its imprecision, and the IPA value plus your own mouth are the referee.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — walkthrough of the vocal-tract diagram (planned:
`assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c01l02-vocal-tract.svg`) — a head in cross-section, facing left,
with five numbered markers:

1. **कण्ठ्य velar zone:** back of the tongue at the soft palate. Whisper क and hold the closure: that pressure far back is marker 1.
2. **तालव्य palatal zone:** the dome of the hard palate. Say च; notice the broad contact of the tongue body.
3. **मूर्धन्य retroflex zone:** the ridge behind the teeth, reached by the tip of the curled-back tongue. Say ट; feel the curl.
4. **दन्त्य dental zone:** the back of the upper front teeth. Say त; the tip should actually touch tooth enamel.
5. **ओष्ठ्य labial zone:** the lips. Say प; the tongue does nothing at all.

**Mirror check:** open wide in front of a mirror and silently mime क → च → ट → त → प. The
velar closure is too far back to see — but you can see the jaw and tongue-root shift for क,
the curl for ट, the tip-on-teeth for त, and the lip closure for प. Point to each zone on your
own face as you go: throat, palate, ridge, teeth, lips.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — each with its fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **Hearing त and ट as "the same t"** (English speakers). English has one /t/, so the ear
   files both Sanskrit sounds under it. *Fix:* physical, not auditory — tongue tip on the
   teeth for त, curled back for ट; exaggerate for a week. The contrast distinguishes real
   words, so it cannot be skipped.
2. **Assuming Devanagari = Sanskrit.** Devanagari also writes Hindi, Marathi, Nepali; and
   Sanskrit has been written in many scripts (§4). *Fix:* say "Sanskrit **in** Devanagari".
   Devanagari on a sign does not mean you are looking at Sanskrit.
3. **Counting अं and अः as vowels** ("14 vowels"). They are ayogavāha — dependent sounds,
   neither vowel nor consonant (§8). *Fix:* recite the vowels to औ and stop; then name अं and
   अः separately, with their category.
4. **Reading IPA slashes as ordinary spelling.** /t͡ɕ/ is not "letters t-c" — one symbol, one
   sound, defined by phonetics, not English spelling habits. *Fix:* always read an IPA value
   together with its articulator description ("tongue body at the hard palate").

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — the model row, worked in full.

### The क-वर्ग as the pattern for everything

| Letter | IPA | Manner | What changes from the previous cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| क (ka) | /k/ | voiceless stop | — the baseline: back of tongue + soft palate |
| ख (kha) | /kʰ/ | voiceless aspirated | add the puff of air |
| ग (ga) | /g/ | voiced | drop the puff, switch the voice on |
| घ (gha) | /gʱ/ | voiced breathy | keep the voice, add breathy release |
| ङ (ṅa) | /ŋ/ | nasal | same closure, air rerouted through the nose |

Read the row aloud with a hand on your throat: क ख ग घ ङ. At ग the throat starts buzzing —
that buzz is voicing, the *only* difference between क /k/ and ग /g/. Same place, same closure,
one switch flipped. That is the same-place insight: the grid turns 25 stops into 5 places × 5
switches. The other four rows repeat this walk at their own places — learn this one row and
you already understand all of them.

**A word you already own.** नमस्ते (namaste) "greeting — bow to you", from lesson 1. Map its consonants: न /n̪/
dental nasal, म /m/ labial nasal, स /s/ dental sibilant, त /t̪/ dental stop. Almost the whole
word lives at the teeth and lips on your new map — you can now describe *where* a word happens.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1, "grammar" is often a **script rule**, and this is the big one,
previewed here in one paragraph.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Devanagari is an *abugida*: every consonant letter, unmarked, carries an
> inherent अ — क alone reads *ka*, not *k*. Other vowels attach to the consonant as signs
> (mātrās), and a vowel-killer stroke (virāma ्) silences the inherent vowel when a bare
> consonant is needed. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

That is the script's whole operating system: inherent vowel, vowel sign, silencer. This is a
preview only — the inherent-vowel rule is taught for real in lesson 3, your first mātrā in
lesson 4, the virāma's full mechanics in Chapter 5. For today, notice what it explains: why
the grid's cells read *ka kha ga* rather than *k kh g* — the letters come with अ built in.

## 16. Conversation

सम्भाषणम् — classroom flavor, two lines; 🔊 audio planned with the Chapter 1 drill pack (§27).
The teacher points at a letter on the board.

— एषः कः वर्णः?
(eṣaḥ kaḥ varṇaḥ?) "What letter is this?" — यह कौन-सा वर्ण है?

— कः इति वर्णः।
(kaḥ iti varṇaḥ.) "The letter *ka*." — यह वर्ण 'क' है।

Two useful pieces: वर्ण (varṇa) from today's vocabulary, and इति (iti) — the little
quotation-mark word Sanskrit uses to mention a sound or a name. This exchange returns in
every letter lesson; by Chapter 3 you will answer it without translating.

## 17. Story

कथा — the grammarian and the drum.

Around the 4th century BCE — estimates vary — a scholar named Pāṇini, from Śalātura in the
northwest of the subcontinent, composed the most compact complete description of any language
ever attempted:

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Eight Chapters") describes Sanskrit in roughly
> 4,000 short rules (sūtras) and effectively fixed the classical norm of the language. It
> remains a landmark in the history of linguistics — a full grammar, compressed with
> near-algebraic economy. [REF:cardona1997]

The Aṣṭādhyāyī opens with fourteen little sound-lists — the varṇa inventory arranged for
maximum rule-writing efficiency. The tradition calls them the Māheśvara sūtras, "the sūtras
of the great lord Śiva":

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** In the traditional telling, Śiva, at the close of his cosmic dance,
> sounded his two-headed drum (ḍamaru) fourteen times — and from those fourteen beats Pāṇini
> received the fourteen sound-lists that open his grammar. The tradition itself tells this as
> a story of inspiration, honoring the inventory's perfection.

Notice what we just did — and what this course will always do. The 4,000 sūtras, the date,
the historical Pāṇini: evidence-based claims, cited. The drum of Śiva: a story the tradition
tells, labeled as such, and worth telling *well* — a legend honestly presented loses none of
its beauty. What you may not do is put the drum in the history books, or let the history
books flatten the drum.

## 18. Exercises

अभ्यासाः — do these in order; 1, 2 and 6 are production tasks.

**अभ्यासः 1 · लेखनम् (writing/drawing).** Draw a simple side-view of a mouth (or trace the
vocal-tract diagram). Mark and label the five places, back to front, each with IAST + English

**अभ्यासः 2 · उच्चारणम् (speak & record).** Record yourself performing the tongue walk — क च ट
त प — three slow rounds, then two at speaking speed. Listen back against the five-places demo
audio. Checklist: does your ट sound darker than your त? Does your क happen at the very back?

**अभ्यासः 3 · श्रवणम् (listening, written answers).** Play the five-places demo audio (🔊), or
have a partner say single sounds from {क, च, ट, त, प} in random order. For each sound, write
the place — velar, palatal, retroflex, dental, or labial. Ten items; 8/10 is a pass.

**अभ्यासः 4 · वर्गीकरणम् (sorting, written answers).** Sort these nine letters into three
place-columns — velar, dental, labial — using the §5 grid (map-reading, not a memory test):
क, ग, ङ, त, द, न, प, ब, म.

**अभ्यासः 5 · मानचित्रपठनम् (grid reading, written answers).** Using the §5 grid, answer in one
phrase each: (a) क and ग share place and closure — what is the one difference? (b) Which
letter is the nasal of the ओष्ठ्य row? (c) ट and त differ in what — place, or voicing?

**अभ्यासः 6 · दर्पणाभ्यासः (mirror work, written answer).** In front of a mirror, say त then ट
five times each. Then write two sentences in your own words: where exactly did your tongue
touch for each sound, and what did you see move in the mirror?

पुनरावृत्तिः — one screen to remember.

> **The map:** five places, back → front: कण्ठ्य velar · तालव्य palatal · मूर्धन्य retroflex ·

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval (5) → hook, alphabet-order contrast (5) → tongue walk,
whole class (8) → grid built live on the board (10) → counts + ayogavāha (7) → exercises 3–5
in pairs (7) → homework, preview lesson 3 (3).

**Board work:** build the grid empty-first — draw the 5 × 5 skeleton, have the class place
क च ट त प in the place-column as they perform the walk (the grid should feel *derived*, not
copied), then add ख ग घ ङ across the top row as the §13 model.

**Mirror logistics:** small mirrors one per pair, or phone front-cameras (students have them).
Light in front of the face, not behind. Expect giggling at the retroflex curl — use it;
exaggerated articulation is the goal now, refinement comes in Chapters 2–4.

**Kinesthetic "tongue walk":** the class stands and takes one physical step forward per
sound — क (back of the room) to प (front) — saying the place name with each step. Silly,
memorable, cements the back-to-front ordering in one pass; reusable as a later-lesson energizer.

**Differentiation — heritage learners who already read Devanagari:** they know the *order*;
places, manners, IPA and honest counts are genuinely new to most. Have them articulate *why*
the order is what it is (they usually never learned this — watch the "oh!" moment), and give
them the "14 vowels" question: the correction lands best when they derive it themselves from
the ayogavāha definition. Pair them with true beginners for exercise 4, with the rule that
they may point at the grid but not say answers.

**What students find hard:** hearing त vs ट (normalize slow progress — production before
perception is fine this week); accepting that च is one sound /t͡ɕ/ despite the two-part
symbol; letting go of "14 vowels" — expect pushback, keep it warm and factual.

## 25. Voice-over Script

Narration for the lesson video (§24). Sanskrit terms carry IPA cues in [brackets]; the
narrator reads them as IPA, not as English spelling.

- **00:00** Say the English alphabet. A, B, C… ever wondered why that order? There is no reason. It is history's accident.
- **00:15** The Sanskrit sound list — the varṇamālā [ʋɐɾɳɐmɑːlɑː] — is different. Its order is a map. A map of your mouth.
- **00:40** Try it. ka [kɐ] — the back of your tongue, deep near the throat. The tradition calls this place kaṇṭhya [kɐɳʈʰjɐ] — velar.
- **01:00** ca [t͡ɕɐ] — the tongue body against the hard palate: tālavya [t̪ɑːlɐʋjɐ] — palatal.
- **01:15** ṭa [ʈɐ] — curl the tip up and back to the ridge: mūrdhanya [muːɾd̪ʱɐn̪jɐ] — retroflex.
- **01:30** ta [t̪ɐ] — tip forward, right onto the teeth: dantya [d̪ɐn̪t̪jɐ] — dental.
- **01:45** pa [pɐ] — the tongue rests; the lips do the work: oṣṭhya [oːʂʈʰjɐ] — labial. Back to front. Five places. The tongue walks forward. That is the map.
- **02:00** Each row repeats one rhythm. The first row: ka [kɐ], kha [kʰɐ], ga [gɐ], gha [gʱɐ], ṅa [ŋɐ]. No voice, then a puff of air, then the voice switches on, then voice with breath, then through the nose.
- **02:45** ka [kɐ] and ga [gɐ] — same place, same closure. One switch: voicing. Learn the walk once, and twenty-five consonants become five places times five settings.
- **03:30** Now, the honest numbers. Thirteen vowels — a [ɐ] to au [ɐu̯]. Thirty-three consonants. And two more sounds that are neither: aṃ [ɐ̃] and aḥ [ɐh].
- **04:00** The tradition calls them ayogavāha [ɐjoːgɐʋɑːɦɐ] — dependent sounds. They cannot stand alone; they lean on a vowel. If you once learned "fourteen vowels" — now you know what was really counted.
- **04:30** One more thing this map is not: a script. Devanagari grew from Brāhmī, through Gupta and Nāgarī — and Sanskrit has been written in many scripts across India. The map is made of sounds, not letter-shapes.
- **05:15** So here is your grid: five places, five manners, thirteen vowels, two dependents. Every lesson from now on drops one new sound into a cell you already understand.
- **05:45** Next time, we start where the tradition starts — with the first sound of the map: a [ɐ]. Namaste [n̪ɐmɐst̪eː].
