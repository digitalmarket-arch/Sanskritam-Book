## 1. Hook

Open any page of Sanskrit — a verse, a dictionary column, a newspaper line — and look for one
letter: अ. It is everywhere. Count the vowels in any passage and *a* (together with its long
partner *ā*) wins by a wide margin: it is the most frequent vowel of the language
[REF:whitney1889 §22]. Every unmarked consonant on the page is silently carrying it too.
Learn this one sound well, and a good part of everything you will ever say in Sanskrit is
already right. Today you also write your first three letters — by tonight, you will have
read Devanagari aloud for the first time in your life.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — the tradition did not merely use अ; it thought hard about it, and saved
its best line about it for last.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Eight Chapters"), composed around the 4th
> century BCE (estimates vary), describes Sanskrit in roughly 4,000 short rules and
> effectively fixed the classical norm of the language. [REF:cardona1997]

Inside that grammar hides a famous detail about today's sound. To make his rules elegant,
Pāṇini needed short *a* and long *ā* to count as the "same" vowel — so, within the grammar,
he treated short *a* as if it were pronounced open, the way *ā* is. A useful fiction, but a
fiction. And he knew it.

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The very last sūtra of the Aṣṭādhyāyī — 8.4.68, in its entirety:
> **अ अ** (a a) — undoes the fiction. In the traditional interpretation it says: the open
> (vivṛta) *a* used inside the grammar is now replaced by the real closed (saṃvṛta) *a* of
> actual speech. The grammar's final act, after thousands of rules, is to hand the sound अ
> back to the speakers — pronounced the way people really say it.
> [REF:ashtadhyayi 8.4.68] [REF:cardona1997]

Sit with that for a second: a grammarian some 2,400 years ago not only heard the fine
difference between an open and a closed *a* — he built his book so that its closing gesture
restores the true pronunciation. That closed, relaxed *a* is exactly the sound you learn
today.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — what your mouth actually does for /ɐ/.

Vowels have no closure anywhere — the air flows freely, and what shapes the sound is *where
the tongue sits*, *how open the jaw is*, and *what the lips do*. Phoneticians map every vowel
by these three settings: height (open ↔ close), backness (front ↔ back), and lip rounding.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** अ /ɐ/ is a short near-open central unrounded vowel: the jaw slightly
> open, the tongue resting in the middle of the mouth — neither raised nor pushed forward or
> back — and the lips neutral. It is close to a "rest position" of the vocal tract with the
> voice switched on. [REF:ladefoged2015] [REF:ohala1994]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** *a* (together with long *ā*) is by a wide margin the most frequent vowel
> in Sanskrit text. [REF:whitney1889 §22]

The two facts are connected, and they explain the script. Because /ɐ/ needs almost no
articulation — no tongue raising, no lip work — every consonant releases into it naturally:
close the lips for म, let go, and *ma* happens by itself. A maximally cheap, maximally
frequent vowel is exactly the vowel you would build *into* the consonant letters rather than
write again and again. That is precisely what Devanagari does (§15). The design is not
mystical; it is economical.

## 8. English Explanation

Three jobs today: the sound, the rule, the letters.

### The sound: /ɐ/, the low-effort vowel

Sanskrit अ is a short, full vowel made with the mouth almost at rest: jaw slightly open,
tongue central and relaxed, lips doing nothing. In IPA it is /ɐ/ — near-open, central,
unrounded.

English gets you close but not home, and honesty about anchors is course policy:

- **Closest anchor: the vowel of "cup", "but" — English /ʌ/.** Good neighborhood, but English
  /ʌ/ sits slightly more open and further back than अ. Start from "cup" and relax everything
  toward the center.
- **Second anchor: the first vowel of "about" — the schwa /ə/.** Central like अ, but a schwa
  is a *reduced*, weak, unstressed vowel — a mumble. अ is never a mumble: it is a full,
  steady vowel that can carry a syllable in any position, stressed or not.

If you speak Hindi, your anchor is better than any English word: the Hindi inherent vowel is
essentially the same sound — your one adjustment is pronouncing it in *every* written
position, including word-finally (§12, mistake 3).

### The rule: every consonant comes with अ inside

The single most important fact about reading Devanagari:

**An unmarked consonant letter is read as the consonant + अ.** क alone is *ka*, never *k*.
म alone is *ma*. This inherent vowel is invisible — nothing extra is written — and it is
always there unless something explicitly replaces or removes it.

This is why lesson 2's grid read *ka kha ga* rather than *k kh g*, and it is why you can
already read strings with no vowel letter in sight: कम = *ka* + *ma* = *kama*. मक = *maka*.
What if you need a bare consonant with no vowel? There is a silencing stroke (virāma ्) —
full mechanics in Chapter 5; you do not need it yet. And other vowels replacing अ? That is
the mātrā system — it starts tomorrow with आ.

### The letters: अ, क, म

- **अ** — the independent vowel letter. Used when the sound *a* stands on its own, with no
  consonant in front of it: at the start of अक (*aka*) or of अमृत (amṛta) "nectar". Inside
  कम no अ is written — the consonants carry it.
- **क** (*ka*) /k/ and **म** (*ma*) /m/ — your two helper consonants, both from lesson 2's
  map: क velar, म labial nasal. In Chapter 1 they are glyph + sound only, here so that you
  can build real syllables; their full varga treatment comes in Chapter 3.

One more preview: अ is a *short* vowel — one beat. Tomorrow's आ is the same mouth further
open, held for two beats, and the difference changes word meanings. Keep अ crisp today and
tomorrow's contrast will come free.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — three drills, in order. Audio: 🔊 `assets/audio/volume-1/v1c01l03-a-drill.mp3`
(isolation) and 🔊 `assets/audio/volume-1/v1c01l03-syllable-drill.mp3` (syllables).

**Drill 1 — isolation.** Play the model. Echo each token exactly — short, steady, no glide:
अ · अ · अ · अ · अ. Five rounds. Checkpoints: jaw one finger open, lips slack, the sound
identical from first to last. If your vowel drifts toward "cup" or stretches long, stop and
reset from a silent rest position.

**Drill 2 — the syllable ladder.** The inherent vowel in action, easiest to hardest:

| Step | Devanagari | IAST | IPA |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | क | ka | /kɐ/ |
| 2 | म | ma | /mɐ/ |
| 3 | कम | kama | /kɐmɐ/ |
| 4 | मक | maka | /mɐkɐ/ |
| 5 | अक | aka | /ɐkɐ/ |
| 6 | अम | ama | /ɐmɐ/ |
| 7 | कमक | kamaka | /kɐmɐkɐ/ |

Listen–repeat twice slowly, once at speaking speed. These are practice syllables, not
dictionary words (§13) — their job is pure: every vowel in the ladder is the same short /ɐ/.

**Drill 3 — the equality check.** English habit is to stress one syllable and shrink the
rest: *kama* becomes "KAH-muh". Sanskrit gives both vowels full value: क-म, two equal short
beats. Tap the table once per syllable as you say कम, मक, कमक — every tap identical. This
tap-habit will carry you all the way through the course.

**Length preview (ears only):** at the end of the isolation track the model says अ, then
holds the long आ — one beat, then two. Just listen; producing आ is tomorrow's lesson, where
the tradition's terms for short and long arrive properly.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — walkthrough of the diagram (planned:
`assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c01l03-mouth-position-a.svg`): a head in cross-section, tongue
lying flat, jaw slightly dropped, lips neutral.

Settings for /ɐ/:

1. **Jaw:** open about one finger's width — slip your index finger sideways between your
   front teeth; that is the whole opening.
2. **Tongue:** completely at rest, lying flat in the middle of the mouth, tip relaxed behind
   the lower front teeth. If you can feel your tongue *doing* something, it is doing too much.
3. **Lips:** neutral — not rounded, not spread into a smile. Slack.

**Mirror check:** say अ five times watching your lips. They should not move at all — no
rounding (that drifts you toward an o-like sound), no spreading. **Hand-under-jaw check:**
rest the back of your hand under your chin and alternate silence → अ → silence. You should
feel one small, constant drop — the same each time. **The म-gate:** hum a long म /m/, then
open the jaw one finger while the voice continues — the sound that falls out is अ. This is
the cleanest way to find the vowel, because it starts from genuine rest.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — four real errors, each with a fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **The "cat" substitution — /æ/** (English speakers). The vowel of *cat* is a front vowel;
   अ is central. The result sounds distinctly foreign and collides with no Sanskrit vowel —
   it is simply outside the system. *Fix:* the म-gate (§11): hum म, drop the jaw one finger,
   let the vowel fall out — the tongue never moves forward. Record and compare against the
   drill audio.
2. **Over-lengthening toward /ɑː/ — "father"** (English speakers). Long open [ɑː] is
   tomorrow's letter आ. Stretch your अ and you erase the length contrast before you have even
   learned it — and length changes meanings in Sanskrit. *Fix:* the tap drill (§9, drill 3):
   one tap, one short beat, done.
3. **Deleting the final a — "dharm", "kamal"** (Hindi speakers). Hindi phonology regularly
   drops this vowel; Sanskrit never does — *dharma*, *kamala*, every *a* spoken.
   [REF:ohala1994] *Fix:* finger-count the syllables as you speak — dhar-ma (2), ka-ma-la
   (3) — and exaggerate the final vowel for a week; it will settle to natural size by itself.
4. **Rounding the lips.** Even slight rounding pulls the vowel toward an o-like color.
   *Fix:* mirror (§11): say the ladder watching only your lips; touch a fingertip to each
   lip-corner — the corners must not move inward.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 "grammar" is often a **script rule**; today's is the biggest one in
the whole writing system.

### The inherent vowel

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Devanagari is an abugida: every consonant letter, unmarked, is read as
> consonant + अ. क = *ka*, म = *ma*, कम = *kama*. Other vowels attach to consonants as signs
> (mātrās — from lesson 4), and a silencing stroke (virāma ् — Chapter 5) removes the
> inherent vowel when a bare consonant is needed. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

When do you write the letter अ itself? Only when the vowel stands **without a consonant in
front of it** — typically at the start of a word or syllable: अक (*aka*), अमृत (*amṛta*).
After a consonant, *a* is never written — it is already there. So कम (*kama*) contains two
spoken *a*s and zero written ones. Read क as "consonant क् plus free अ" and every string in
this lesson decodes itself.

### Writing अ, क, म — stroke order

One common classroom convention follows, and an honest note with it: stroke-order traditions
for Devanagari vary between schools — there is no single canonical order. This course teaches
one consistent convention; the stroke-order diagram
(`assets/images/volume-1/v1c01l03-stroke-order-a-ka-ma.svg`) is the authority, and the rules
that generalize are: **body first, stem next, headline (शिरोरेखा) last; left before right,
top before bottom.**

**अ — 3 strokes.**
1. The left half: one continuous motion — start near the top, draw the upper curve down to
   the middle, flow into the lower loop, and finish with the short arm pointing right at
   mid-height. (This double curve is the hard part; see §22.)
2. The stem: vertical bar, top to bottom, meeting the mid-height arm.
3. The headline: one horizontal line across the top, left to right.

**क — 3 strokes.**
1. The bowl and knot: from the stem-line at mid-height, curve left, down and around, coming
   back to cross your starting point — the little knot closes the loop.
2. The stem: vertical bar, top to bottom, through the crossing.
3. The headline, last.

**म — 3 strokes.**
1. The body: start at the top, descend into the small loop at the lower left, then sweep
   right along the base and up to meet the stem-line.
2. The stem: vertical bar, top to bottom.
3. The headline, last.

Write big at first — half a page per letter. Precision scales down; it never scales up.

## 17. Story

कथा — why a tradition would bow to its easiest sound.

Sanskrit literature saves one of its most famous self-descriptions for अ. In the Bhagavad
Gītā, in the chapter where Kṛṣṇa names the foremost of every class of things — of mountains,
of rivers, of seasons — he comes to speech itself:

> **[SYMBOL · प्रतीकम्]** अक्षराणाम् अकारोऽस्मि (akṣarāṇām akāro 'smi) — "Of syllables I am
> the *a*." [REF:bhagavadgita 10.33] This is the tradition's poetry about अ — a statement of
> the sound's primacy and pervasiveness, honored here as literature. It is not a phonological
> claim, and it will never appear in a quiz.

Why *this* sound, of all sixty-odd? Notice what you learned today and the choice explains
itself. अ is the vowel your mouth makes from rest — no reaching, no rounding, no effort. It
is the most frequent vowel of the language. It hides inside every unmarked consonant letter,
so it is silently present across every line of text, the way — a poet might say — something
fundamental is silently present in everything. The tradition looked at its own sound system,
found the humblest and most pervasive member, and gave it the seat of honor. You do not need
the metaphysics to feel the aptness. And Pāṇini, working centuries earlier with a scientist's
temperament rather than a poet's, gave अ a different kind of honor: he ended his entire
grammar by restoring its true pronunciation (§4). Two very different minds, one conclusion:
this little sound matters.

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval (5) → hook + frequency fact (3) → sound work:
isolation drill, म-gate, mirror (8) → inherent-vowel rule built on the board (6) → writing
block: board demo, air-writing, tracing start (12) → syllable ladder + mini-dictation (6) →
homework + preview of आ (5).

**Pacing — this is the first writing lesson.** Resist the urge to move fast: production
quality on three glyphs beats six sloppy ones, and habits set today are nearly permanent.
Work in short bursts — 3–4 minutes of writing, then a sound drill, then back to writing.
Hands tire; ears do not.

**Board demonstration order:** draw each glyph huge, narrating strokes aloud ("body… stem…
headline") with numbered arrows chalked beside each stroke. Have the class air-write with
full-arm movements *before* pencils touch paper — big muscles learn the shape first, then
delegate to fingers. Then worksheet tracing while you walk the room watching stroke order,
not letter beauty. Wrong order with a pretty result still gets corrected now — it will not
survive contact with faster writing later.

**Left-handed writers:** never force the right hand. Have them tilt the paper clockwise
(20–30°) and keep the hand *below* the writing line so the headline stroke stays visible and
unsmudged; seat them on the left end of shared desks. If you are right-handed, demonstrate
strokes with the numbered diagram rather than insisting they mirror your hand.

**What students find hard:** (1) The double curve of अ — the loop collapses or reverses.
Isolate it: practice only the left half, margin-sized rows, big to small, before rejoining
the stem. (2) English speakers defaulting to "cup"/"cat" vowels — return to the म-gate; it
fixes the tongue position without talking about the tongue. (3) Hindi and heritage learners'
final-*a* deletion — expect it to resurface for weeks; keep the finger-counting fix light
and routine, never a reprimand. It is a language habit, not carelessness.

**Differentiation — learners who already write Devanagari:** their writing needs no drill,
but their stroke *narration* usually does — put them on explaining strokes aloud and on
coaching a beginner's stroke order (they may point, not grab pencils). Their genuine
challenge in this lesson is the full final vowel; give them अमृत/अर्थ echo work and the
dharma/dharm contrast as their stretch task.
