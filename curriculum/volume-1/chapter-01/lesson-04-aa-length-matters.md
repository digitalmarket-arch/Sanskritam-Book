## 1. Hook

Say *kala*. Now say it again, but hold the first vowel one beat longer: *kāla*. That single
beat of breath just carried you from कल (kala) "a sweet, indistinct sound" to काल (kāla)
"time" — the word the tradition also uses for death itself. One beat. In English, stretching
a vowel adds emphasis; in Sanskrit it swaps the word. Vowel length is not decoration here —
it is meaning. Today you learn to control that beat, to write it, and then — for the first
time in this course — to read real Sanskrit words. Twelve of them.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिक सन्दर्भः — why one beat of vowel was worth measuring.

For the Vedic schools, texts did not live on paper. They lived in trained memory, passed from
teacher to student in exact recitation — for centuries.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** The Ṛgveda was composed c. 1500–1200 BCE (scholarly consensus) and
> was composed and transmitted orally; its preservation depended on disciplined recitation
> lineages rather than writing. [REF:jamison2014 p.5]

Now put yourself inside that system. If the text exists only as sound, then *every property
of the sound is part of the text* — including how long each vowel lasts. Stretch one vowel
and you have recited a different word (कल is not काल). So the tradition did what careful
engineers do: it defined a unit and measured.

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The Śikṣā and prātiśākhya manuals — the Vedic schools' own
> phonetics textbooks — measure vowel duration in mātrās: a short (hrasva) vowel counts one
> mātrā, a long (dīrgha) vowel exactly two. Length, in this tradition, is not a nuance; it is
> arithmetic.

That is why this lesson exists as a *separate* lesson. In many alphabets, आ would be filed as
"same as अ, just longer" and forgotten. The tradition refused to file it that way, and modern
phonology agrees (§5): अ and आ are two different vowels that distinguish words.

One more gift from this history: the word मात्रा (mātrā) — the duration unit — is also the
everyday name for the *dependent vowel signs* of Devanagari, including the ा you will write
today. One word, two senses: the measure of a sound and the mark of a vowel. This lesson
teaches both, on purpose, together.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिक व्याख्या — what /ɑː/ is, and why "long vowels" are ordinary science.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** आ = /ɑː/ — an open back unrounded vowel, held long: jaw low, tongue low
> with the back slightly raised, lips neutral. Its duration is roughly twice that of hrasva
> अ /ɐ/. [REF:whitney1889 §§19–22]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Phonemic vowel length — duration alone distinguishing words — is common
> in the world's languages: Japanese, Finnish, Czech, Arabic and classical Latin all use it,
> as did the ancient Indo-European languages generally. [REF:ladefoged2015]

Two consequences for the learner:

**Length is normal — English is the odd one out.** English has no pure length contrast: its
"long" and "short" vowels (*bit*/*beat*) differ mainly in *quality*, and duration floats
freely with stress and emotion. So English-trained ears treat vowel duration as expressive
noise, not information. Finnish children, by contrast, grow up distinguishing *tuli* "fire"
from *tuuli* "wind"; Latin schoolboys knew mālum "apple" from malum "evil". Sanskrit sits
firmly in this majority club: duration carries meaning, so duration must be controlled.

**The ratio is measurable.** Record yourself saying काल (kāla) and कल (kala) and look at the
waveform in any audio editor: in careful speech the first vowel of काल should occupy roughly
twice the time of the first vowel of कल. The traditional "1 mātrā vs 2 mātrās" (§4) and the
modern stopwatch agree. That 2:1 target is your production goal — not "a bit longer",
*twice* as long. Learners who aim at "a bit longer" drift back to English habits within a
week; learners who count beats do not.

A note on quality: /ɐ/ and /ɑː/ differ in *position* as well as duration — /ɐ/ is central
with the tongue at rest, /ɑː/ is more open and further back (§10, §11). So the pair is
doubly safe for listeners: even if your length slips, the color differs. But do not rely on
that safety net; recitation tradition and dictation exercises both demand the length.

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतव्याख्या — the lesson's idea in the simplest possible Sanskrit.

अ इति ह्रस्वः स्वरः।
(a iti hrasvaḥ svaraḥ.) "*a* is a short vowel." — अ ह्रस्व स्वर है।

आ इति दीर्घः स्वरः।
(ā iti dīrghaḥ svaraḥ.) "*ā* is a long vowel." — आ दीर्घ स्वर है।

माता अत्र अस्ति।
(mātā atra asti.) "Mother is here." — माता यहाँ है।

Look at that last line again. माता — you can *read* that word now: मा (म + ा) + ता (त + ा).
By the end of this lesson, that sentence's first word is yours, not the book's. The
traditional terms arriving today: ह्रस्व (hrasva) "short", दीर्घ (dīrgha) "long", मात्रा
(mātrā) "measure; vowel sign", अक्षर (akṣara) "syllable block" (§15).

## 7. Hindi Explanation

हिन्दी व्याख्या

पहले एक अच्छी ख़बर — आज का स्वर आपके लिए नया नहीं है। आ हिन्दी में भी है, और मात्रा ा भी: आम,
काला, माला — आप यह रोज़ लिखते-पढ़ते हैं। क + आ = का — यह जोड़ आपने पहली कक्षा में सीखा था। इस
पाठ में आपका काम अंग्रेज़ी-भाषी साथियों से अलग है: उन्हें मात्रा-प्रणाली *सीखनी* है, आपको अपनी
जानी-पहचानी प्रणाली पर संस्कृत का अनुशासन *चढ़ाना* है।

अनुशासन नम्बर एक — लम्बाई की नाप। संस्कृत में स्वर की लम्बाई सजावट नहीं, अर्थ की रीढ़ है: कल
(kala) यानी मधुर, अस्पष्ट ध्वनि; काल (kāla) यानी समय। फ़र्क़ सिर्फ़ इतना कि पहला स्वर कितनी देर
टिकता है। हिन्दी में भी ह्रस्व-दीर्घ का भेद है, पर बोलचाल में हम प्रायः ढीले पड़ जाते हैं — सन्दर्भ
से काम चल जाता है। संस्कृत में, ख़ासकर पाठ और श्रुतलेख में, नहीं चलेगा। नियम पक्का कीजिए: दीर्घ

ध्वनि की माप और लिखाई का चिह्न।

अनुशासन नम्बर दो — शब्द के अन्त का स्वर। हिन्दी की आदत है अन्तिम अ को चुपचाप गिरा देना: हम
"कमल" लिखते हैं और *kamal* पढ़ते हैं। संस्कृत में तीनों अक्षर पूरे बोले जाते हैं — क-म-ल,

अन्तिम आ को पूरी दो मात्राएँ दीजिए — हिन्दी की जल्दबाज़ी वहाँ सबसे पहले पकड़ी जाती है।

## 8. English Explanation

Two things happen in this lesson, and they deserve separate attention: a new **sound** (the
long vowel /ɑː/) and a new **writing mechanism** (the mātrā). The second one is the key that
unlocks reading.

### Part one — the sound: अ /ɐ/ vs आ /ɑː/

|  | अ (a) | आ (ā) |
|---|---|---|
| IPA | /ɐ/ | /ɑː/ |
| Duration | 1 beat (hrasva) | 2 beats (dīrgha) — twice as long |
| Tongue | at rest, central | low, back of tongue slightly raised, further back |
| Jaw | slightly open | open — about two finger-widths (§11) |
| Lips | neutral, unrounded | neutral, unrounded |
| English anchor | none exact (lesson 3) | the *a* of *father* — but see the caution below |

The anchor caution, as always stated honestly: for many English speakers the *father* vowel
is close to /ɑː/ in quality, but English lets its duration collapse when the word is
unstressed, and some accents front or round it. Use *father* to find the *color* of आ; use
beat-counting (§9) to fix its *length*. The Hindi anchor is exact: the आ of आम "mango",
pronounced with care.

The contrast to internalize: अ is the mouth's rest position, short; आ is an open, back,
deliberate vowel, held for two beats. Not "the same sound, longer" — a different vowel that
is *also* longer.

### Part two — the mātrā system (the heart of this lesson)

Recall the inherent-vowel rule from lesson 3: क alone reads *ka*. Then how do you write *kā*?

Devanagari's answer: the vowel आ has **two written forms** —

1. **The independent letter आ** — used where the vowel stands on its own, at the start of a
   word: आ in अमल? No — look carefully: अमल begins with अ. A true example: आम (āma) "mango"
   🔊 begins with the independent आ.
2. **The dependent sign ा (the ā-mātrā)** — used after a consonant. It is a single vertical
   stroke standing to the **right** of the consonant, joined under the headline.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Devanagari is an abugida: consonant letters carry an inherent अ, and
> every other vowel attaches to a consonant as a dependent sign (mātrā). The ā-mātrā ा
> attaches to the right: क + आ = का. The independent letter आ appears word-initially; the
> mātrā appears after consonants. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

**The rule, in one line: to say a consonant with आ instead of अ, finish the consonant, then
write one vertical stroke to its right.** The mātrā *replaces* the inherent vowel — का is
*kā*, never *kaā*.

Apply it to everything you can write:

| Consonant | + mātrā ा | = | Reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| क (ka) | ा | का | kā /kɑː/ |
| म (ma) | ा | मा | mā /mɑː/ |
| त (ta) | ा | ता | tā /t̪ɑː/ |
| ल (la) | ा | ला | lā /lɑː/ |

Why does the script work this way? Because Devanagari's unit is not the letter but the
**akṣara** — the syllable block (§15). Each block holds one consonant (or vowel) plus its
vowel information, so writing marches syllable by syllable, exactly the way speech does. One
block, one beat-group. Alphabets spell sounds in a row; an abugida stacks each syllable into
a single visual unit. Once you know the inherent vowel and the mātrās, reading is almost
mechanical — there are no silent letters, no "ough" surprises.

### The payoff — reading your first real word

Take माता. Read it block by block:

- **मा** = म (ma) + ा → the mātrā replaces the inherent vowel → *mā*
- **ता** = त (ta) + ा → *tā*
- **माता** = mā-tā = **mātā** /mɑːt̪ɑː/ "mother" — माँ

That is the whole method. Two blocks, two long vowels, one word — and not a toy word: mātā
is among the oldest words in the family, cognate with English *mother* itself (§13). Twelve
words are now within your reach (§14). This is the moment the course has been building
toward since lesson 1: you are no longer learning *about* the script — you are reading it.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — three drills, in order. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c01l04-length-pairs.mp3` (drills 1–2) and
`planned:assets/audio/volume-1/v1c01l04-word-list.mp3` (drill 3).

**Drill 1 — the length ladder.** Set a slow, steady beat (metronome at 60, or tap the table).
On one breath, alternate: अ (one beat) — आ (two beats) — अ — आ — अ — आ. Do not let आ sag into
one-and-a-half beats; count silently "one-two" while it sounds. Then reverse the ladder
starting from आ. Three rounds. The goal is a *felt* 2:1 ratio, automatic by the end of the
week.

**Drill 2 — pair drill.** With the audio (or a partner reading), repeat each pair, then
answer the random-order round:

- कल — काल · कल — काल (kala — kāla)
- तल — ताल · तल — ताल (tala — tāla)
- Random round: the audio says one word of a pair; you say its partner.

Keep the *second* vowel identical in both words — the contrast lives only in the first
syllable. If your कल starts sounding like "kul" or your काल like English "call" (rounded),
return to §11 and reset the mouth.

**Drill 3 — the syllable grid.** Read across each row, then down each column, steady beat,
short syllables one beat, long syllables two:

| क /kɐ/ | का /kɑː/ |
|---|---|
| म /mɐ/ | मा /mɑː/ |
| त /t̪ɐ/ | ता /t̪ɑː/ |
| ल /lɐ/ | ला /lɑː/ |

Then combine blocks into real words from §14: माता, माला, कला, लता, काल, ताल. In recitation
style, exaggerate: give every dīrgha its full two counts. Precision first, speed never.

A recitation note: traditional chanting keeps the mātrā count audible — that discipline is
exactly what these drills are borrowing. When in doubt, slow down and count.

## 10. IPA

The new vowel: आ = **/ɑː/** — open back unrounded vowel + the IPA length mark.

**The length mark ː.** The triangular colon after a symbol means "held long": /ɑ/ long =
/ɑː/. It is the notation's way of writing the second mātrā. You will meet it again in every
dīrgha vowel of the course: ई /iː/, ऊ /uː/, ए /eː/, ओ /oː/.

**Narrow note — length is not the whole story.** Compare the course values: अ /ɐ/ is
*near-open central*; आ /ɑː/ is *open back*. Two differences, not one:

1. **Quantity:** /ɑː/ ≈ twice the duration of /ɐ/.
2. **Quality:** /ɑː/ is articulated lower and further back than /ɐ/.

So a merely stretched अ — [ɐː] — is still not a correct आ, and a clipped आ — [ɑ] — is still
not a correct अ. Aim for both dimensions; the drills in §9 train quantity, §11 trains
quality.

Phonemic slashes, as established in lesson 2: /ɑː/ names the sound category that
distinguishes काल from कल — exactly the kind of meaning-bearing unit the slashes are for.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थानम् — walkthrough of the diagram (planned:
`assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c01l04-mouth-position-aa.svg` — two side-by-side sagittal views:
अ at rest-position, आ open-back, with jaw-angle and tongue-height markers).

To find आ /ɑː/:

1. **Jaw:** drop it to about two finger-widths between the front teeth. This is noticeably
   more open than अ, whose jaw barely opens. (Test honestly: two stacked fingers should just
   fit.)
2. **Tongue:** let it lie low in the mouth, tip resting behind the lower teeth, with the
   *back* of the tongue slightly raised toward the throat. Compared with अ, everything moves
   down and back.
3. **Lips:** completely neutral. No rounding — this is the classic English-speaker slip,
   turning आ into the rounded vowel of *call*. If the mirror shows your lips pulling into a
   circle, reset.
4. **Hold it:** say आ and sustain it for a slow two-count without letting the jaw creep shut
   or the quality drift.

**Mirror + hand check.** Sit before a mirror (or phone front-camera). Say अ — आ — अ — आ. You
should *see* the jaw drop further for आ every time, and see no lip rounding. Now place the
back of your hand under your chin and repeat: the chin should press down twice as
noticeably, and twice as long, for आ. Ten alternations, watching and feeling.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्य-भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix. These feed the quiz distractors.

1. **Random length (English speakers).** English ties vowel duration to stress and feeling,
   so learners stretch vowels wherever the sentence-melody suggests — काल shrinks when
   unstressed, कल stretches for emphasis, and meaning scrambles. *Fix:* divorce length from
   emotion. Length belongs to the *word*, not the mood; count beats (§9 drill 1) until the
   2:1 ratio survives even in a bored voice.
2. **Reducing unstressed ā.** In long words English speakers keep the first ā and shrink the
   later ones: माता becomes "mātă", माला becomes "mālă". *Fix:* final and medial ā get the
   same two full beats as initial ā. Read माता as mā-tā, two equal long blocks — the drum
   pattern is DUM-DUM, not DUM-da.
3. **Collapsing final -ā vs -a in careful speech (Hindi speakers).** Hindi phonology deletes
   final short a and relaxes final length, so कला vs कल, and the full final -a of कमल
   (*kamala*, not "kamal"), blur in recitation and dictation. *Fix:* in Sanskrit every final
   vowel is pronounced at full value — final अ as a real /ɐ/, final आ as a full two-beat
   /ɑː/. Dictation exercises (§18) will catch this within minutes.
4. **Writing the mātrā before finishing the consonant (stroke-order error).** Learners hear
   *kā*, panic about the vowel, and write ा first or bolt it onto a half-formed क — producing
   deformed or reversed blocks (writing ाक, which is not a possible akṣara). *Fix:* the hand
   follows the mouth: consonant first, complete with its stem and headline, *then* one
   vertical stroke to the right. Chant while writing: "letter, then length."

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — the minimal pairs, worked in all four layers, then the payoff word.

### The pairs that prove the rule

**कल** (kala) "indistinct, sweet (of sound)" — मधुर, अस्पष्ट ध्वनि · /kɐlɐ/
**काल** (kāla) "time" — समय · /kɑːlɐ/
[REF:apte1890 s.v. कल, काल]

Same consonants, same final vowel — the *only* difference is the first vowel's length (and
its shifted quality, §10). One beat of breath separates a sweet murmur from time itself.

**तल** (tala) "surface, bottom" — तल, सतह · /t̪ɐlɐ/
**ताल** (tāla) "rhythm, musical time; palmyra tree" — ताल (लय); ताड़ · /t̪ɑːlɐ/
[REF:apte1890 s.v. तल, ताल]

A drummer's pair: *tala* the surface you strike, *tāla* the rhythm you keep. If your ear
still merges them, return to §9 drill 2 — these two pairs are the whole final exam of this
lesson's sound work.

**A final-vowel pair for the careful:** कल (kala) vs **कला** (kalā) "art" — कला · /kɐlɑː/.
Here the length contrast sits at the *end* of the word — precisely where Hindi habits (§12,
mistake 3) and English reduction (§12, mistake 2) both attack. Say them as a trio: kala —
kalā — kāla. Three words, one skeleton, three meanings.

### The payoff: your first word is four thousand years of family history

**माता** (mātā) "mother" — माता, माँ · /mɑːt̪ɑː/

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit मातृ/माता (mātṛ/mātā), English *mother*, Latin *māter*, Greek
> *mētēr* are cognates — parallel descendants of Proto-Indo-European \*méh₂tēr. None of them
> borrowed the word from another; they are siblings from one ancestral family. Note the long
> ā: English even kept a trace of that ancient length in its spelling history. [REF:mallory2006]

Lesson 1 promised that Sanskrit and English are relatives, not stranger and source. Here is
the receipt, in the first word you can read: the mātā you just spelled out block by block is
the same inherited word your own language has been saying as *mother* all along. Cognate,
not loan — as always, the distinction matters.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in Volume 1 this is a **script rule**, and today's is the biggest one in the
whole writing system.

### The mātrā rule, formally

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** In Devanagari, a vowel is written with its independent letter (आ) when
> it begins an akṣara on its own — in practice, word-initially or after another vowel — and
> with its dependent sign, the mātrā (ा), when it follows a consonant, where it replaces the
> inherent अ. Thus क + आ = का (kā), never क‌आ. [REF:masica1991 ch.6]

Distribution, as a decision procedure: *Is the ā-sound at the start of the word?* → write आ.
*Does it follow a consonant?* → write ा on that consonant. The two spellings never compete
for the same position. (Diagram: `planned:assets/diagrams/volume-1/v1c01l04-matra-mechanics.svg` —
क and आ merging into का, with the inherent अ visibly ejected.)

### The akṣara — the script's true unit

The blocks you have been reading — क, का, मा, ता — each one is an **अक्षर** (akṣara)
"syllable block" — अक्षर: one consonant (or a lone vowel) together with its vowel value. The
word माता is four sounds but two akṣaras: मा + ता. Devanagari text is a chain of akṣaras,
which is why it reads so predictably: each block tells you exactly one syllable's worth of
sounds. From today the course counts letters *and* akṣaras — get used to both units.

### Stroke order — आ, त, ल, ा

One common convention, described here; teaching traditions vary in details, and your
teacher's consistent habit is fine too. (Card: `planned:assets/images/volume-1/v1c01l04-stroke-order-aa-ta-la.svg`;
tracing: `planned:assets/worksheets/volume-1/v1c01l04-tracing-matra.pdf`.)

- **आ** — write a complete अ (the two left curves, then the vertical stem, as in lesson 3),
  then the ā-stroke: one more vertical line to the right, headline joining it last. आ is
  literally अ wearing its own mātrā — let the shape teach you the phonology.
- **त** — the left loop/curve first, then the vertical stem down the right side, headline
  last. Helper consonant: glyph + sound /t̪/ only today; the full dental family arrives in
  Chapter 3 (spiral).
- **ल** — the left hook and bowl in one movement, up into the vertical stem, headline last.
  Helper consonant: glyph + sound /l/ only today; full treatment with the semivowels later
  in Volume 1.
- **ा** — after the consonant is *complete*: one vertical stroke top-to-bottom at the
  consonant's right shoulder, connected by the headline. Always last, always right.

The headline habit from lesson 3 continues: letters hang from the line like clothes from a
clothesline; write the body, then the line (or the line continuously, per your teacher's
convention — consistency beats dogma).

## 17. Story

कथा — माला · The Garland. Micro-fiction in six lines, built almost entirely from §14. Words
you can read are **bold**; the few glue words are glossed 🔊 below. Each line: Devanagari,
IAST, English, Hindi.

1. **कमला** अत्र अस्ति।
   (kamalā atra asti.) "Kamalā is here." — कमला यहाँ है।
2. **माता** अत्र अस्ति।
   (mātā atra asti.) "Mother is here." — माता यहाँ है।
3. **माता** — पश्य, **कमला**! **माला**!
   (mātā — paśya, kamalā! mālā!) "Mother: Look, Kamalā! A garland!" — माता — देखो, कमला! माला!
4. **कमला** — **मम** **माला**?
   (kamalā — mama mālā?) "Kamalā: My garland?" — कमला — मेरी माला?
5. **माता** — आम्, तव **माला**।
   (mātā — ām, tava mālā.) "Mother: Yes, your garland." — माता — हाँ, तुम्हारी माला।
6. **कमला** — **मम** **माला**! **मम** **कमल**-**माला**!
   (kamalā — mama mālā! mama kamala-mālā!) "Kamalā: My garland! My lotus-garland!" —
   कमला — मेरी माला! मेरी कमल-माला!

Glue words 🔊: अत्र (atra) "here" — यहाँ; अस्ति (asti) "is" — है; पश्य (paśya) "look!" — देखो;

Read the story aloud twice: once slowly, block by block, once at story speed. A named
character, a mother, a gift — sixty percent of these words were unreadable to you yesterday.

## 20. Revision

पुनरावृत्तिः — one screen to remember.

> **The sound:** आ /ɑː/ — open, back, unrounded, and **two beats long**; dīrgha = 2 × hrasva,
> in quality *and* quantity distinct from अ /ɐ/. **The rule:** क + आ = का — finish the
> consonant, then one vertical stroke on the right; आ word-initially, ा after consonants; the
> mātrā replaces the inherent vowel. **The unit:** अक्षर — one block, one syllable; माता =
> मा + ता. **The proof:** कल ≠ काल, तल ≠ ताल — length is meaning. **The prize:** 12 readable
> words — माता, माला, कला, लता, कमल, कमला, काल, ताल, तल, मत, मम, अमल.

Spaced review (the schedule is the method — distributed practice beats cramming
[REF:cepeda2006]):

- **In 1 day:** re-take the dictation (exercise 3) cold, and write का मा ता ला from memory.
  Compare with your first attempt — count corrected mātrās.
- **In 1 week:** read the full §14 list aloud once at speed with full length discipline, and
  take the dictation a third time; re-answer quiz Q1–2 and Q6 without looking.
- **From lesson 3:** keep अ /ɐ/ honest — the two English traps (/æ/, /ɑ/) return in lesson
  5's assessment alongside today's pairs.

## 22. Teacher Notes

शिक्षक-टिप्पणी

**This is the lesson where reading clicks.** For three lessons students have been practicing
letters; today, mid-lesson, they read माता and realize it is a *word*. Do not rush that
moment. Plan the mātrā-mechanics segment so that the first full reading of माता happens with
full class attention — build it on the board block by block (म… मा… मा-ता… माता), let a
student say the word before you do, and then tell them what they just did: read Sanskrit.
Expect visible delight; spend it immediately on the word list, while motivation is at its
peak.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → hook: कल/काल spoken pair, class votes
on whether they heard a difference (4) → आ sound work: mouth position, mirror, length ladder
(8) → the mātrā rule on the board + माता moment (10) → syllable grid + word list reading in
pairs (8) → exercises 1 and 4 (7) → homework briefing, incl. the 1-day re-dictation (3).

**Board work:** keep a permanent two-column strip on the side board all lesson: क म त ल on
the left, का मा ता ला on the right, headline drawn as one continuous "clothesline". Every
new word gets decomposed under it. Write the akṣara boundaries with small vertical ticks
(मा|ता) for the first few words, then drop the ticks.

**The classic classroom error:** students hear *kā* and write the mātrā **on the wrong side**
(ाक) or start with the stroke and squeeze the consonant in after. Catch it at the tracing
stage, not the dictation stage: circulate during exercise 2 and watch pens, not just output —
the error is in the *sequence*, which finished worksheets hide. The chant "letter, then
length" (§12) works; so does having students say the consonant aloud as they finish it and
the vowel as they add the stroke.

**Dictation logistics:** read each word twice at natural speed with a 10-second writing gap —
resist the urge to over-lengthen the dīrghas as a hint; the point is recognizing *normal*
careful pronunciation. Collect error types, not just scores: missing mātrā, wrong-side
mātrā, and extra mātrā (hypercorrection on मत/मम/तल) each get different remediation.

**Homework spacing — say it out loud:** tell students *why* the re-dictation is tomorrow and
again next week, not tonight: spaced repetitions produce far more durable learning than
massed ones [REF:cepeda2006]. Students who know the design follow it; students who think the
gap is administrative skip it. The 1-day and 1-week re-dictations of this lesson are the
course's first explicit spacing loop — establish the ritual now, it runs for 139 more lessons.

**Differentiation — Hindi-literate learners:** the mātrā mechanics are automatic for them;
their genuine work is §12 mistake 3 (full final vowels, disciplined 2:1 length). Give them
the *recorder* role in pair reading — judging a partner's length against the model sharpens
their own — and hold them to recitation-grade length in exercise 5, where their fluency
otherwise lets them coast. Pair them with script-beginners for exercises 1–2 with the rule
"guide the hand with words, never take the pen."

**What students find hard:** hearing the pair contrast at natural speed (normalize a 1-day
delay — discrimination scores jump after sleep); believing that length is *lexical* rather
than expressive (the कल/काल vote in the hook usually converts the skeptics); and, for
English speakers, the final full -a of कमल — model it slowly, hand-beat the three syllables.

- **00:00** Listen to two words. kala [kɐlɐ]. kāla [kɑːlɐ]. Hear it? One beat of vowel.
- **00:12** kala [kɐlɐ] — a sweet, indistinct sound. kāla [kɑːlɐ] — time itself. In Sanskrit,

- **00:28** Today: the long vowel ā [ɑː], the writing trick that unlocks real words — and by

- **00:40** First, the sound. Drop your jaw — two fingers wide. Tongue low, pulled slightly
  back. Lips relaxed, never rounded. ā [ɑː].
- **01:00** Now the discipline. a [ɐ] gets one beat. ā [ɑː] gets exactly two. Count with me:
  a [ɐ] — one. ā [ɑː] — one, two. Again. a [ɐ]. ā [ɑːː]. Twice as long. Every time. Even
  when you're bored. *Especially* when you're bored.
- **01:35** And notice — it's not just longer. The mouth opens wider, the tongue sits further
  back. A stretched a [ɐ] is still not an ā [ɑː].
- **01:50** Now the writing system's best trick. You know that every consonant letter carries
  a free vowel: क alone says ka [kɐ]. So how do you write kā [kɑː]?
- **02:10** You do NOT write the big letter आ after it. Instead, आ shrinks — into a single
  vertical stroke on the consonant's right: ा. The mātrā [mɑːt̪ɾɑː].
- **02:30** Watch: क… plus the stroke… का. ka [kɐ] becomes kā [kɑː]. The free vowel is gone —
  the mātrā replaced it. One block, one syllable. The tradition calls each block an akṣara
  [ɐkʂɐɾɐ].
- **02:50** The rule in five words: letter first, then the length. आ at the start of a word;
  the stroke ा everywhere after a consonant. That's the whole system.
- **03:10** Now do it with every letter you own: mā [mɑː]. tā [t̪ɑː]. lā [lɑː].
- **03:20** And now — read with me. मा… mā [mɑː]. ता… tā [t̪ɑː]. Together: mātā [mɑːt̪ɑː].
  Mother. You just read your first real Sanskrit word.
- **03:45** And it's not any word. Sanskrit mātā [mɑːt̪ɑː], English mother, Latin māter, Greek

  Sanskrit word was your word all along.
- **04:20** Twelve words are now yours. Listen and repeat — two beats on every long vowel.

- **04:50** Now the dangerous pairs. tala [t̪ɐlɐ], surface — tāla [t̪ɑːlɐ], rhythm. kala

- **05:10** And the quiet ones: mama [mɐmɐ], my. mata [mɐt̪ɐ], thought. amala [ɐmɐlɐ],
