## 1. Hook

Tomorrow morning a word will leave your mouth that has woken gods. In the great temples of the
South, the day's first sound is a hymn genre called *suprabhātam* — "the beautiful dawn" — sung
before sunrise to rouse the deity; the word carrying that genre's name is the same word you will
use tomorrow as a simple "good morning!". The word is old; the greeting habit is younger than it
looks; and this course can hand you both facts in one breath, because that is what its labels are
for. Today: the four words that tell the time of day, one word for the day itself, and the
greetings that ride the clock.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिकः सन्दर्भः — old words, a young habit, and one variant handled honestly.

The four day-words on today's list are plain classical vocabulary — dawn, midday, evening and
night have never needed replacing. The interesting history sits on the greetings built from them:

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** सुप्रभात is attested in classical Sanskrit: सु (su) "good, beautiful" +
> प्रभात (prabhāta) "dawn" — "a beautiful daybreak". Its career as an everyday spoken greeting —
> "good morning!" — is modern spoken-Sanskrit convention. Both statements are true of the same
> word, and this course says both. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. suprabhāta]

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** In temple practice a whole devotional genre bears this word's name:
> the suprabhātam hymns — the Veṅkaṭeśa-suprabhātam of Tirumala is the most famous — recited
> before dawn to waken the deity for the day's worship. A beloved living practice, noted as
> practice.

One piece of course history, one line: the 1.x curriculum (PDF p.27) taught the variant
शुभप्रभातम् (śubhaprabhātam). Both forms circulate in spoken Sanskrit; this course teaches
सुप्रभातम् and recognizes the variant when you meet it — the गंगा/गङ्गा policy, applied to a
greeting.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिकं विवरणम् — two facts of learning, one fact about words and their jobs.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Greetings are formulaic chunks: learned whole, retrieved whole, keyed to
> their situations. Binding each chunk to its trigger — सुप्रभातम् to the morning, शुभरात्रिः to
> the night — is how such routines are best learned, and timed retrieval on known material builds
> the automaticity a real exchange demands. [REF:nation2013]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** A word's *formation* and a word's *use* are separate facts with separate
> histories. A form can be centuries old while one of its jobs is recent — English "goodbye"
> contracts a much older blessing; Sanskrit सुप्रभातम् is a classical word whose greeting-job is
> modern. Register labeling — saying which fact is which — is a core skill of using a language
> with a long history, not a footnote to it. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. suprabhāta]

That second fact is this chapter's honesty axis in miniature, and today's lesson carries its
showcase word. Nothing needs defending: the modern greeting is real and standard among Sanskrit
speakers; the classical attestation is real and older. The only error available is collapsing the
two facts into one — and §15 will make sure you never have to.

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतेन विवरणम् — the day, told in sentences you fully own:

> **प्रभातम् अस्ति। सूर्यः तत्र अस्ति।**
> (prabhātam asti. sūryaḥ tatra asti.) "It is dawn. The sun is there." — प्रभात है। सूर्य वहाँ है।

> **सायम् अस्ति। चन्द्रः तत्र अस्ति।**
> (sāyam asti. candraḥ tatra asti.) "It is evening. The moon is there." — शाम है। चन्द्रमा वहाँ है।

> **रात्रिः अस्ति। शुभरात्रिः!**
> (rātriḥ asti. śubharātriḥ!) "It is night. Good night!" — रात है। शुभ रात्रि!

Notice what a greeting *is*, grammatically: a complete utterance. सुप्रभातम्! stands alone exactly
as नमस्ते! always has — no verb, nothing missing. The day-words slot into the oldest frame you own
(… अस्ति), and the greetings need no frame at all.

## 7. Hindi Explanation

हिन्दी में समझिए

आज की आधी शब्दावली आपके घर की है। दिन, रात्रि, प्रभात, मध्याह्न, सायं — पाँचों हिन्दी में जीवित हैं;
पढ़ने में कोई नया अक्षर आपको नहीं रोकेगा। मेहनत तीन जगह है, और तीनों जानी-पहचानी।

पहली — वही पुरानी कसौटी, अन्तिम स्वर। हिन्दी का दिन संस्कृत में पूरा *dinam* है — "दिन्" नहीं; रात
यहाँ रात्रिः है — अन्त में विसर्ग की हल्की साँस समेत। और सायम् को हिन्दी की आदत से "सायं" मत बनाइए:
संस्कृत में अन्त का म् होंठ बन्द करके बोला जाता है, स्वर नाक में नहीं चढ़ता। शीशे के सामने बोलिए —
होंठ मिलें तो शब्द सधा।

दूसरी — आज का असली पाठ, लेबल का पाठ। सुप्रभातम् शब्द शास्त्रीय है — "सुन्दर प्रभात"; मन्दिरों में भोर
से पहले गाई जाने वाली सुप्रभातम् स्तुतियों तक इसका नाम पहुँचा है। पर "गुड मॉर्निंग" की तरह अभिवादन में
इसका चलन आधुनिक बोलचाल की संस्कृत की देन है। दोनों बातें एक साथ सच हैं — यही आज सीखने की कला है।
शुभरात्रिः "गुड नाइट" का संस्कृत अनुवाद है — टुकड़े पुराने, बनावट आधुनिक; हम इसे गर्व से और लेबल के
साथ सिखाते हैं। और अकेला शुभम् — "शुभ हो, कल्याण हो" — शास्त्रीय आशीर्वाद का शब्द है, जिसे आप चौथे
अध्याय से पढ़ना जानते हैं।

तीसरी — आँख का काम। रात्रिः के भीतर वही त्र बैठा है जो मित्र और तत्र में आपका पुराना साथी है — आज वह
ि और ः पहनकर आया है: रा-त्रिः। और मध्याह्नः में एक नया जोड़ दिखेगा — ह्न। घबराइए मत: यह ह् + न है,
उसी विधि से बना जो हर संयुक्ताक्षर बनाती है, और ह-परिवार में ह्य-ह्म को आप पाँचवें अध्याय से पहचानते
हैं। घटक बोलिए, जोड़ पढ़िए — विधि वही, शब्द नया।

## 8. English Explanation

Nothing new arrives today either — no letter, no sign. The load is eight words, one honest label
with two parts, and a clock.

### The clock circle

Four words tell the time of day, and one word names the whole span: **प्रभातम्** (prabhātam)
"dawn, morning" — **मध्याह्नः** (madhyāhnaḥ) "midday" — **सायम्** (sāyam) "evening" —
**रात्रिः** (rātriḥ) "night" — and **दिनम्** (dinam) "day", the circle itself, dawn to dawn. Run
them as a circle, always in order, around and around: an order your mouth memorizes so your eye
can stop working (§9). मध्याह्नः even carries its meaning in its build: मध्य (madhya) "middle" +
a form of the old day-word अहन् (ahan) — "mid-day", literally
[REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. madhyāhna].

### Two conjuncts do the reading work

**रात्रिः** is three blocks: रा-त्रिः. The middle block is त्र — the fused ligature certified in
v1.c05.l03 (मित्र, तत्र, अत्र) — today wearing the ि mātrā and followed by the visarga: त् + र +
इ, then ः. Old friend, new dress.

**मध्याह्नः** is the day's longest word and your smallest news: म-ध्या-ह्नः. The ध्या block is an
ordinary stem-loss weld (ध् + या); the ह्नः block is the one join you have never met — **ह्न =
ह् + न**, the third member of the ह-family whose first two, ह्य and ह्म, you learned to recognize
by components in v1.c05.l03. Same mechanism as every conjunct since Chapter 5: the silent one
shrinks, the speaker stands, no vowel inside. Components, then block, then word.

### The showcase label — both things are true

Now the word this chapter's honesty axis was built to handle. **सुप्रभातम्** (suprabhātam) is two
facts wearing one form. One: **the word is classical** — सु + प्रभात, "a beautiful dawn",
attested in the dictionaries of the classical language, dignified enough that a whole devotional
genre answers to its name (§4). Two: **the greeting is modern** — using it as the morning hello,
the way English uses "good morning", is a convention of modern spoken Sanskrit, not classical
idiom. Not a contradiction; a biography. Say both and you are simply right twice.

**शुभरात्रिः** (śubharātriḥ) "good night" is simpler: a modern calque — "good night" translated
part for part into perfectly readable Sanskrit (शुभ + रात्रिः). Real, standard, said nightly by
real speakers; labeled modern. Freestanding **शुभम्** (śubham) "may it be well" is the classical
wish-word — शुभ has been readable since v1.c04.l02; today it stands alone and blesses. And the
1.x variant in one line: शुभप्रभातम् (śubhaprabhātam) circulates too; this course teaches
सुप्रभातम्, and when the variant meets you on a poster, read it and lose no sleep — गंगा/गङ्गा
taught you how to hold two circulating forms of one thing.

### Which greeting when

नमस्ते fits every hour, every person — that has not changed and never will. The labeled pair are
clock-bound: सुप्रभातम् belongs to the morning, शुभरात्रिः to the night's parting. Midday and
evening take नमस्ते or नमस्कारः, and a farewell can always wear classical स्वस्ति or भद्रम् ते
(v2.c01.l01). That is the entire decision tree — small enough to run at speed, which is exactly
what §9 makes you do.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — no new sounds; the work is order, speed, and honest final vowels. Audio: 🔊
`planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c01l03-drill.mp3` (drills 1–3),
`planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c01l03-word-list.mp3` (three speeds + dictation and call tracks).

**Drill 1 — conjunct warm-up.** Components first, then the block, then the word: त् + र → त्र →
रा-त्रिः → रात्रिः; ह् + न → ह्न → म-ध्या-ह्नः → मध्याह्नः. No vowel inside either join — if you
hear "hana", a vowel leaked (§12). Then the breathy pair प्रभातम्, शुभम्: the भ is breathy-voiced
/bʱ/, a soft push of air through voice, never a plain b.

**Drill 2 — the clock circle.** With the audio, in order, round and round: प्रभातम् — मध्याह्नः —
सायम् — रात्रिः. Three passes: slow, natural, against the clock. Then the caller flips: the audio
names a time out of order, you answer with the *next* one in the circle before the beat runs out.

**Drill 3 — greeting-by-hour.** The audio names a scene word (प्रभातम्! … रात्रिः!); you answer
with a fitting greeting inside two seconds. Rules of the game: नमस्ते is always legal; सुप्रभातम्
only after प्रभातम्; शुभरात्रिः only after रात्रिः. Timed recognition of known chunks — the
fluency strand doing precisely its job [REF:nation2013].

**Drill 4 — three speeds.** The §14 list three times: block by block, natural pace, target pace.
Log the third time (§18 exercise 5). The standing law holds at every speed: *dinam* full,
*rātriḥ* breathed to the end — speed never buys back a clipped vowel.

**Narrow note — the ह्न join:** in मध्याह्नः the voiced breathy /ɦ/ flows directly into the
dental nasal /n̪/ — one gesture, no vowel between; the nose opens while the breath is still
moving. If a vowel appears ("madhyāhanaḥ"), re-run §9 drill 1.

**Narrow note — रात्रिः:** the cluster reads across the seam, [ɾɑː.t̪ɾih] — tap, then dental stop
and tap inside one block. The final visarga is the same voiceless /h/ as in every ः-word you own.

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** Recitation practice echoes the vowel after a visarga — रात्रिः recited
> as [ɾɑːt̪ɾihi], as रामः is [rɑːmɐhɐ]. A tradition-register habit of chanted text, noted as such;
> plain speech keeps the light /h/.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थितिः — three checks; the articulators already know everything here. The word-anatomy strip
on the day-clock card (`planned:assets/images/volume-2/v2c01l03-day-clock.svg`) marks both joins.

1. **The breathy check.** Palm on your chest for प्रभातम् and शुभम्: the भ should hum *and* push
   air — voice plus breath together /bʱ/. A plain "b" means the breath is missing; "ph" means the
   voice is.
2. **The ह्न seam.** Say मध्याह्नः in slow motion and freeze on the last block: the breath of ह
   is still flowing when the tongue reaches the teeth for न and the nose opens. Tongue re-parking
   mid-block means a vowel leaked — component drill, slower.
3. **The lips-or-nose check.** End सायम् with your lips visibly closed — [m]. If the mirror shows
   open lips and a nasal vowel, that is Hindi सायं intruding (§12). The same mirror clears दिनम्
   and सुप्रभातम्: every final म् is a closure you can see.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्याः भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix; these feed the quiz distractors.

1. **"din" and "raat" — the Hindi habit imported.** दिनम् clipped to "din", रात्रिः flattened to
   "raat". *Fix:* full final vowels and the visarga's light breath — *dinam*, *rātriḥ*. The
   schwa-deletion discipline of v1.c01.l03 never retires.
2. **सायं for सायम्.** Hindi nasalizes the vowel; Sanskrit closes the lips on a full म्. *Fix:*
   the §11 mirror check — see the lips meet.
3. **A vowel leaked into ह्न:** मध्याह्नः read "madhyāhanaḥ". *Fix:* a conjunct has no vowel
   inside — Chapter 5's seam rule applies to the ह-family exactly as to स्त. Build it from
   components: ह् + न, one block.
4. **Collapsing the label:** calling सुप्रभातम् "the classical Sanskrit good morning" — or,
   opposite error, "not real Sanskrit". *Fix:* two facts, both true — classical word, modern
   greeting-use (§8). One fact per claim, and you are never wrong.
5. **Treating शुभप्रभातम् as a mistake to correct in others.** *Fix:* it is a circulating
   variant, not an error; the course teaches सुप्रभातम् and recognizes both (§8) — exactly as
   your eye accepts गंगा and गङ्गा.
6. **The lookalike cognate:** "English *day* obviously comes from दिन." *Fix:* it does not — the
   resemblance is coincidence, the famous trap of §13. दिन's certified siblings live in Lithuania
   and Old Church Slavonic, not in England.

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — three stories inside today's list.

### सुप्रभातम् — one word, two facts

**सुप्रभातम्** (suprabhātam) — सु + प्रभातम्, "beautiful dawn". The word: classical, attested,
dignified enough to name the dawn hymns of temple tradition (§4). The greeting-use: modern spoken
convention, the everyday morning hello of living Sanskrit. Say it tomorrow with both facts in
your pocket.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** सुप्रभात appears in classical usage as "a beautiful daybreak"; its function
> as a morning greeting is modern spoken Sanskrit. The word predates its job — a normal biography
> for words in long-lived languages. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. suprabhāta]

### दिनम् and its distant siblings

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit दिन, Lithuanian *diena* and Old Church Slavonic *dĭnĭ* "day" are
> siblings — parallel descendants of a shared Proto-Indo-European ancestor, cognates and not
> loans in any direction. English *day*, despite the lookalike form and identical meaning, is
> **not** part of this family: it descends from a different root entirely — the standard textbook
> example of a false cognate. [REF:mallory2006]

The lesson-1 tool, another outing: *karma* was a loan, चक्र and *wheel* are cousins, पञ्च and
*five* are cousins — and दिन/*day* is the pair that *looks* related and is not. Resemblance is
evidence of nothing; descent is established by systematic correspondence, which Lithuanian shows
and English does not.

### The शुभ family — a wish and a calque

- **शुभम्** (śubham) "may it be well" — the neuter of शुभ "auspicious, good" (v1.c04.l02)
  standing alone as a blessing. Classical benedictions close on this word — शुभम् अस्तु "may
  there be welfare" (display only; the verb form waits for the verb chapters)
  [REF:apte1890 s.v. शुभ].
- **शुभरात्रिः** (śubharātriḥ) "good night" — शुभ + रात्रिः, both parts yours, joined on the
  model of "good night". A calque: a phrase translated part-for-part into another language's
  material. Labeled modern, used nightly.
- Display twin: **शुभदिनम्** (śubhadinam) "good day" also circulates in modern spoken use —
  transparent now that both halves are on your list. Display only, off the ledger.

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in this lesson the grammatical point *is* the label system, applied for the first
time to a whole word-family.

### The label rule, formally

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** (1) A word's formation and its uses are separate facts: each part of the
> courtesy kit carries **two** labels — one for the word, one for the job. (2) The day-cycle
> words — प्रभातम्, दिनम्, मध्याह्नः, सायम्, रात्रिः — are classical vocabulary in both form and
> use. (3) सुप्रभातम् is a classical word whose greeting-use is modern spoken convention;
> शुभरात्रिः is a modern calque built of classical parts; शुभम् is classical in word and wish
> alike. (4) नमस्ते is hour-neutral; the labeled pair are clock-bound — morning and night
> respectively. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. suprabhāta]

### What the labels do for your speech

Nothing stops you saying any of it — that is the point. The labels are not a permission system;
they are *knowledge about* the words you use freely. You greet the morning with सुप्रभातम् like
every modern speaker, and if a classicist raises an eyebrow you can name the convention, its
parts, and its dictionary entry. The register skill this chapter trains is exactly that move:
fluent use on the outside, honest history on the inside. Next lesson runs the same structure on
its hardest case (धन्यवादः — v2.c01.l04), and the chapter exam's register cards (v2.c01.l05) are
built from today's both-things-true pattern.

### Pause-form dress — and one honest shape

Today's list wears the usual sentence dress: neuters in म् (प्रभातम्, दिनम्, सायम्, शुभम्), the
masculine मध्याह्नः in ः — and **रात्रिः**, which wears ः *and is feminine*. The shape rule of
v1.c06.l02 ("ः-words answer to कः") was a rule of thumb about the words you had then; रात्रिः is
your first exception in the ledger, noted now, explained when Volume 2's gender chapters build
the system. Nouns stay whole-word glossed until Volume 3's case machinery; greetings, as §6 said,
are complete utterances and decline nothing.

## 16. Conversation

सम्भाषणम् — one day, four mini-scenes. Audio records into the Chapter 1 drill pack (§27). Every
word is self-read; the l01–l02 kit works every scene.

### Scene 1 — morning, on the school path

— सुप्रभातम्, भोः!
(suprabhātam, bhoḥ!) "Good morning, hey there!" — सुप्रभात, अरे सुनो!

— सुप्रभातम्! अपि कुशलम्?
(suprabhātam! api kuśalam?) "Good morning! All well?" — सुप्रभात! सब कुशल?

— सर्वम् कुशलम्!
(sarvam kuśalam!) "All is well!" — सब कुशल है!

### Scene 2 — midday, at the school

— नमस्ते! मध्याह्नः अस्ति।
(namaste! madhyāhnaḥ asti.) "Greetings! It is midday." — नमस्ते! दोपहर है।

— नमस्ते! आम् — सूर्यः तत्र!
(namaste! ām — sūryaḥ tatra!) "Greetings! Yes — the sun is right there!" — नमस्ते! हाँ — सूर्य वहाँ!

### Scene 3 — evening, taking leave of the teacher

— सायम् अस्ति। नमस्कारः!
(sāyam asti. namaskāraḥ!) "It is evening. Salutations!" — शाम है। नमस्कार!

— नमस्कारः! स्वस्ति!
(namaskāraḥ! svasti!) "Salutations — farewell!" — नमस्कार! स्वस्ति — शुभ हो!

— भद्रम् ते!
(bhadram te!) "Blessings on you!" — तुम्हारा भला हो!

### Scene 4 — night, at home

— रात्रिः अस्ति। चन्द्रः तत्र अस्ति।
(rātriḥ asti. candraḥ tatra asti.) "It is night. The moon is there." — रात है। चाँद वहाँ है।

— शुभरात्रिः!
(śubharātriḥ!) "Good night!" — शुभ रात्रि!

— शुभरात्रिः! शुभम्!
(śubharātriḥ! śubham!) "Good night — and be well!" — शुभ रात्रि! शुभ हो!

Housekeeping: the clock decides the greeting — only the morning scene opens with सुप्रभातम्, only
the night scene closes with शुभरात्रिः, and नमस्ते/नमस्कारः carry the middle of the day. Scene
3's farewells wear the classical label (v2.c01.l01); scene 1's wellness exchange is v2.c01.l02
working its shift. Pause form between turns, one full beat, as always.

## 17. Story

## 18. Exercises

अभ्यासाः — six exercises; 2, 3, 5 and 6 are production tasks. Do them in order.

**अभ्यासः 1 · अन्वेषणम् (conjunct-spotting — timed).** Sixty seconds: scan the §14 list and mark
every conjunct at work. Check: ्र in प्रभातम् and सुप्रभातम्; त्र in रात्रिः and शुभरात्रिः; ध्य

what are its components? (ह्न = ह् + न.)

**अभ्यासः 2 · दिनचक्रम् (the clock circle — production, spoken).** Recite the circle from memory,
in order, three full turns: प्रभातम् — मध्याह्नः — सायम् — रात्रिः. Then answer the audio's
out-of-order calls with the *next* member of the circle. Time the three turns; log it; repeat
tomorrow — the drop is the fluency.

**अभ्यासः 3 · अभिवादनक्रीडा (greeting-by-hour — production, spoken/recorded).** The worksheet's
clock faces ✍ (or the audio's scene calls): for each hour shown, say a fitting greeting within
two seconds. Score with the honest rules: नमस्ते always counts; सुप्रभातम् only in the morning
window; शुभरात्रिः only at night; स्वस्ति and भद्रम् ते at any parting. Record one full round.

**अभ्यासः 4 · अङ्कनम् (the label sort).** Sort the cards into *classical* / *modern* /
*both-true*: दिनम् · शुभरात्रिः as a formation · शुभरात्रिः as an everyday habit · सुप्रभातम् the
word · सुप्रभातम् the greeting-use · शुभम् · स्वस्ति · the variant शुभप्रभातम्. Check: exactly
one card family needs the two-part answer — that is the whole point of the lesson (§15).

**अभ्यासः 5 · श्रुतलेखनम् (dictation — production, written).** Play the dictation track 🔊: all
eight words in random order, each read twice with a writing pause. Write them in Devanagari.
Self-check against §14: the त्र fused, the ह्न assembled from components, every visarga written,
no vowel inside any join. 7/8 is a pass; re-take tomorrow (§20).

**अभ्यासः 6 · अनुवादः (translate the day — production, written).** Write the Sanskrit: (a) "It is
dawn. Good morning!" (b) "It is midday. The sun is there." (c) "It is evening. Farewell!"
(d) "It is night. Good night — may it be well!" Read your four answers aloud as one tiny story,
then note which of your greetings would deserve a modern label if a reviewer asked.

## 22. Teacher Notes

अध्यापकटिप्पणी

**Open with the temple, then the clock.** Play or describe thirty seconds of a suprabhātam hymn
and ask what the word means; let someone find सु + प्रभात on the board. Then the pivot: "and
tomorrow you will say this word to a sleepy friend — so is it ancient or modern?" The answer —
*both, in different senses* — is the lesson, and it lands better as a riddle resolved than as a
rule announced.

**Timing plan (45 min):** §3 retrieval, written (5) → clock circle on the board, four corners of
the room assigned to the four times; whole-class circle drill (8) → the label chips: two colors,
one chip per *word*, one per *use*; the सुप्रभातम् card gets one of each — build it live (7) →
conjunct lab: रा-त्रिः and म-ध्या-ह्नः from components; ह्य/ह्म recalled, ह्न added to the family
(6) → greeting-by-hour corners game: teacher calls a corner, class answers with a legal greeting;
illegal answers sit down (8) → word list at three speeds; dictation launch (6) → exercises,
homework, the real-world greeting assignment (5).

**Run the corners game strictly.** नमस्ते is always legal — that is not a loophole, it is the
fact being taught; the game's fun is that सुप्रभातम् at the रात्रिः corner *loses*. One wrong
buzz teaches the windows faster than any chart.

**Differentiation:** Hindi speakers own these words already — their work is the finals (*dinam*
not "din", lips closed on सायम्, the visarga breathed) and the labels, where their instincts
carry no information; deputize them for the teach-back. Script-side learners find मध्याह्नः long
— the count-first protocol (म-ध्या-ह्नः, three blocks) shrinks it.

**What students find hard:** believing the two-part label is precision rather than hedging (run
the "goodbye" parallel from §5); keeping vowels out of ह्न (slow-motion component drill); the
feminine रात्रिः wearing ः (one honest sentence — "rule of thumb, first exception, system next
chapter" — and move on; do not open the gender system early); and the day/*day* trap, which
someone will raise — let §13's answer be read aloud from the page.
