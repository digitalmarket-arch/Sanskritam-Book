## 1. Hook

Your phone keeps a Sanskrit word in its emoji tray. The folded-palms sign — sent a million
times a day as hello, thanks, please — is the picture of something with a precise classical
name, and every letter of that name is already yours: it even hides the second working
conjunct of the rarest nasal you own. Volume 2 opens with the easiest promise a course can
make — **no new letters, ever again** — and with the thing every conversation in every
language opens with: the greeting. Five classical words complete your courtesy kit today.
But first, exactly one week after your exam, right on schedule, three papers come out of
your bag.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिकः सन्दर्भः — the courtesy of a classical language, honestly labeled.

This chapter's name is **अभिवादनम्** (abhivādanam) "greeting, respectful salutation" — an
old friend in new dress:

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** अभिवादनम् is built on वद् "speak" — the root of वदति and संवादः —
> wearing अभि "toward": a greeting is speech turned toward someone.
> [REF:apte1890 s.v. अभिवादन]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** नमस्ते = नमः + ते "homage to you"; नमस्कारः = नमः + कारः "the making
> of a bow". Both are fully classical in formation; नमस्ते as the everyday all-purpose
> greeting is the living convention of spoken Sanskrit. [REF:apte1890 s.v. नमस्]

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** स्वस्ति "well-being" is attested from the Ṛgveda onward — Vedic
> hymns open and close blessings with it — and classical literature keeps it as a formula
> of well-wishing and leave-taking. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. svasti]

Here Volume 2's new discipline begins. Volume 1 separated fact, history, tradition and
symbol; this volume adds one more honest axis, **applied word by word**: is a courtesy
formula *classical idiom*, or a *modern spoken convention*? Today every kit word wears the
classical label; later this chapter brings words whose label is different — attested word,
modern everyday use — said so in one line, without embarrassment in either direction. The
Volume 1 precedent: विद्यालयः, the word on real school gates, labeled standard modern usage.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिकं विवरणम् — three old levers, one new label.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Greeting formulas are among the highest-frequency, most fixed chunks
> a language owns. Learning them as unanalyzed wholes — usable at once, unpacked later —
> is the standard, well-supported route into speaking. [REF:nation2013]

That is why today is a vocabulary lesson, not a grammar lesson: भद्रम् ते works perfectly
before you can parse it, exactly as मम नाम … did. The machinery is Volume 3's; the
courtesy is yours this morning.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Practice spaced over time produces far more durable learning than
> the same practice massed together — which is why the Volume Exam scheduled today's
> Station 0 a week in advance, and why its times are logged, not just performed.
> [REF:cepeda2006]

The stations themselves are retrieval practice — answers from memory before checking, the
strongest rehearsal there is [REF:roediger2006]. And the label system is science of a
quieter kind: a dictionary records that a word exists; honest teaching also records *who
says it, when*. Volume 2 keeps both books open.

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतेन विवरणम् — the kit at work, every part certified:

> **भोः! नमस्ते! स्वागतम्!**
> (bhoḥ! namaste! svāgatam!) "Hey — greetings! Welcome!" — अरे! नमस्ते! स्वागत है!

> **भद्रम् ते!**
> (bhadram te!) "Blessings on you!" — तुम्हारा भला हो!

> **स्वस्ति!**
> (svasti!) "Well-being — farewell!" — कल्याण हो!

Three lines, not one verb — greetings are complete utterances, the verbless sentence you
have trusted since धर्मः सत्यम्. The संज्ञा of the chapter: **अभिवादनम्** (abhivādanam)
"greeting" — glossed in §4, readable to the last sign: अ-भि-वा-द-न-म्.

## 8. English Explanation

Nothing new in the script — that promise now covers the rest of the course. What arrives
is a toolkit: the words a Sanskrit speaker reaches for in the first five seconds of any
meeting, each with an honest label on the handle.

### The kit, laid out

| Word | Job | Usage label |
|---|---|---|
| भोः | getting attention — "O!, hey" | classical address particle |
| नमस्ते | the all-purpose greeting | classical formation; the living everyday convention |
| नमस्कारः | its formal sibling | classical formation, same honesty |
| स्वागतम् | welcoming someone in | classical idiom |
| भद्रम् ते | a blessing on meeting or parting | classical courtesy formula, epic-common |
| स्वस्ति | well-being; the blessing-farewell | classical idiom, Vedic onward |
| नमः | the bow itself, standalone | classical; readable since Chapter 3 |
| अञ्जलिः | the *name* of the joined-palms gesture | classical word (the gesture: cultural practice) |

The trio in rows two to four is review — drilled in exchanges today, not re-taught. The
other five are this lesson's headwords:

- **भोः** (bhoḥ) "O!, hey" — the classical particle of address that opens a thousand
  classical dialogues, standing *before* the one called: भोः राम! Two signs and a breath —
  and the visarga is absolutely load-bearing (§12).
- **अञ्जलिः** (añjaliḥ) "the joined-palms gesture; cupped hands" — the dictionary word for
  the palms-together salute, and the word that gives ञ its second job (§13).
- **भद्रम्** (bhadram) "good; blessings" — Chapter 5's भद्र, sentence-dressed in म् and
  promoted to courtesy duty in the chunk **भद्रम् ते** "blessings on you".
- **स्वस्ति** (svasti) "well-being; farewell, blessings" — the kit's classical goodbye and
  the oldest word on today's list (§4).
- **नमः** (namaḥ) "homage, a bow" — read since v1.c03.l04 as नमस्ते's first half; today it
  stands alone as a full greeting.

### The label rule, formally

From this lesson on, every courtesy word carries one honest line: *is the word attested,
and is this use classical idiom or modern spoken convention?* The halves are independent —
a word can be perfectly classical while its everyday use is a modern habit; the honest
label says both. Today the whole kit wears the classical label, so the rule costs nothing;
it starts paying later this chapter, when the everyday thank-you arrives. §15 states it as
this volume's standing law.

### ञ्ज — the bench letter's second job

Chapter 6 hired ञ for exactly one word: पञ्च, the ञ्च stack. The homorganic rule that
hired it makes a prediction you can now check: before ज — a c-row stop — the only possible
nasal is ञ. अञ्जलिः confirms it: अ-ञ्ज-लिः. Hindi print will show you अंजलि with the
convenience dot; the classical spelling writes the conjunct, and you read both identically
— the गङ्गा/गंगा account, word for word (v1.c06.l01 §8).

### What today does not teach

Two previews are shown, deliberately untaught: the joined form भद्रं ते — the anusvāra
stepping across a word seam, sandhi, Volume 3 (§13) — and the pattern in गुरवे नमः
"homage to the teacher", whose word-shape the case system will explain (§15). Read them,
recognize them, build nothing from them yet.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — no articulation labs this volume; the work is warmth, carry, and timing.
Audio: 🔊 `planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c01l01-dialogue.mp3` (the §16 round at three
speeds plus a gap track), `planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c01l01-word-list.mp3` (kit
words twice each, then the dictation track).

**Drill 1 — the warmth contour.** Say नमस्ते twice: once flat, as if reading a list; once
to a friend you have not seen in a week — pitch starting a little high, landing soft. That
arc is the greeting's tune. Run the kit through it: नमस्ते · नमस्कारः · स्वागतम् ·
भद्रम् ते · स्वस्ति. Questions keep their rising tune; greetings get the warm fall.

**Drill 2 — the call.** भोः is said *across a room*: let the long ओ carry, and keep the
visarga breathing at the end — palm before the mouth, a puff on the ः, every time.
भोः! — pause — नमस्ते!

**Drill 3 — turn timing.** The dialogue habit from v1.c06.l02, now standing law: pause
form between turns — finish your line, one full beat of silence, then the reply. Run the
§16 round with the slow track, holding the beat.

**Drill 4 — three speeds, no casualties.** The round at slow, natural, then target pace.
Speed never buys back a final vowel or a visarga: *bhadram te* — both words complete;
*bhoḥ* — breath included, at any tempo.

## 10. IPA

No new values — the Volume 1 promise, kept into Volume 2.

**The kit:** भोः /bʱoːh/ · अञ्जलिः /ɐɲd͡ʑɐlih/ · भद्रम् /bʱɐd̪ɾɐm/ · स्वस्ति /sʋɐst̪i/ ·
नमः /n̪ɐmɐh/ — the review trio: नमस्ते /n̪ɐmɐst̪eː/ · नमस्कारः /n̪ɐmɐskɑːɾɐh/ · स्वागतम्
/sʋɑːgɐt̪ɐm/. The chunk: भद्रम् ते /bʱɐd̪ɾɐm t̪eː/. Display: गुरवे नमः /guɾɐʋeː n̪ɐmɐh/.

**Narrow note — भोः.** Breathy-voiced [bʱ] releases into the long pure [oː] — no English
"go"-glide — and closes on the visarga's voiceless [h]. Three events, one syllable.

**Narrow note — where the nasal lands.** अञ्जलिः divides [ɐɲ.d͡ʑɐ.lih] — the ñ closes the
*first* syllable, exactly as [pɐɲ.t͡ɕɐ] did in पञ्च; ञ्ज is that stack's voiced twin.

**Narrow note — what IPA does not record.** The warmth contour is pitch movement across
the phrase; the segments are identical whether you greet warmly or read a shopping list.
The symbols carry the sounds; the welcome is yours to add.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थितिः — nothing new to place; three checks to run before the round.

1. **The breathy onset.** भोः and भद्रम् open on भ — voiced *and* breathy: the folds hum
   through the extra breath. Hand on chest: voice from the first instant.
2. **The shared-closure check, voiced edition.** Say अञ्जलिः in slow motion and freeze
   mid-word: the middle of the tongue holds ज's palatal station through the ञ — one
   closure, nose open then closed, the ञ्च discipline of v1.c06.l01 with voicing added.
   If the tongue re-parks between ञ and ज, a vowel leaked in; re-run slowly.
3. **The twin-स seams.** स्वस्ति carries two welds — स्व, then स्ति — jaw still through
   both: *sva-sti*, never "sa-va-sa-ti". Close the circuit with the palm test on भोः, नमः.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्याः भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix; these feed the quiz distractors.

1. **भोः clipped to "bho".** The visarga dies first in fast speech, and without it the
   word loses its passport. *Fix:* palm test on every भोः — the breath is load-bearing at
   any speed (§9 drill 2).
2. **अंजलि written for अञ्जलिः in dictation.** The Hindi print habit reaches for the dot.
   *Fix:* the stop chooses its own nasal — ज hands you ञ, welded: ञ्ज. Recognize the dot
   form; write the classical conjunct — the गङ्गा/गंगा account exactly (v1.c06.l01).
3. **भद्रम् collapsed to "bhadrm".** Hindi's भद्र invites mid-word schwa-deletion. *Fix:*
   three full beats, भ-द्र-म् — the द्र weld one onset, the final म् a closed-lip landing.
4. **Reading the labels as rankings.** "Classical = correct, modern = wrong" — or the
   reverse. *Fix:* the labels are honesty, not hierarchy; both registers are real speech.
   The rule only insists you know which is which (§15).
5. **Treating the joined palms as part of the word's grammar.** *Fix:* the gesture is
   cultural practice — TRADITION register, welcome, never required, never assessed. The
   *word* अञ्जलिः is dictionary vocabulary — FACT register, fully assessable (§13).
6. **Generalizing गुरवे नमः** — building "X-वे नमः" for every noun in the ledger. *Fix:*
   display only; the shape behind गुरवे is Volume 3's case machinery. Recognize, don't
   build (§15).

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — three words with something inside them.

### अञ्जलिः — the word your phone has been sending

**अञ्जलिः** (añjaliḥ) — अ-ञ्ज-लिः · /ɐɲd͡ʑɐlih/

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** अञ्जलि names the two open hands placed side by side and slightly
> hollowed — the cupped-palms gesture of salutation and offering; the dictionaries record
> it as the standard word for the palms-joined salute.
> [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. añjali]

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** The añjali gesture — palms joined at the chest — accompanies
> नमस्ते across South Asia as everyday courtesy and as reverence, in homes, temples and
> classrooms alike. It is cultural practice carried beside the language: welcome whenever
> it feels right, required nowhere, and never part of any word's grammar.

The script story: ञ worked one day in all of Volume 1 — the ञ्च of पञ्च. Today the
homorganic rule hands it the voiced twin: ञ्ज. Two conjuncts, one letter, zero exceptions.

### भद्रम् ते — a blessing with a seam you can already see

**भद्रम् ते** (bhadram te) "blessings on you" — /bʱɐd̪ɾɐm t̪eː/

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** भद्र "good, blessed" yields the courtesy formula भद्रं ते "good
> betide you, blessings on you" — a standard well-wishing expression, common in the epics.
> [REF:apte1890 s.v. भद्र]

The dictionaries print it joined — **भद्रं ते** (bhadraṃ te) — because in connected speech
the final म् before a following consonant is written as the anusvāra dot. You know that
dot's reading rule since Chapter 2; new is only the *location*: the abbreviation stepping
across a word boundary. That step is sandhi, Volume 3's subject. Today read the pause form
भद्रम् ते, and recognize the joined dress when a text wears it — exactly the सत्यम् एव /
सत्यमेव arrangement from exam day.

### स्वस्ति — the farewell that marks doorways

**स्वस्ति** (svasti) — स्व-स्ति · /sʋɐst̪i/

The oldest word in today's kit (§4's HISTORY note), and still the cleanest way to wish
someone well on parting: स्वस्ति! One relative you did not expect:

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The auspicious doorway mark स्वस्तिक (svastika) carries स्वस्ति in
> its name — "the sign of well-being". The word is the blessing; the mark is named after
> it. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. svastika]

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — in a vocabulary lesson this section carries the **usage rules**: how the kit
deploys, and the label law that now governs this volume.

### भोः — the address particle

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** भोः is a classical particle of address — "O!, sir!, hey" — placed
> before the person or title called: भोः राम! The dictionaries record it across classical
> dialogue and drama. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.v. bhos]

Two usage notes, chunk-honest. First, the called name may change shape: रामः is called as
राम — you met this with the exam dialogue's नमस्कारः, आचार्य! Such calling shapes are used
whole today, like every name-chunk before them; the system that produces them is the case
machinery, Volume 3. Second, भोः needs no name at all — alone, it is a complete "hey
there!" and the natural first word of the §16 round.

### The label law — this volume's standing rule

Every courtesy word this volume teaches carries one honest usage line, word by word:
**attested word + its register — classical idiom or modern spoken convention.** Today's
whole kit is classical: भोः, अञ्जलिः, भद्रम् ते, स्वस्ति, नमः, and the trio — classical
formations whose everyday all-purpose use is the living convention, said so in one line.
The labels are facts about usage, never grades; both registers are real Sanskrit in real
mouths. The first *modern spoken convention* labels arrive later this chapter, and the
rule will hold them to the same single honest line.

### गुरवे नमः — a preview curiosity, display only

On school walls and prayer books you will meet **गुरवे नमः** (gurave namaḥ) "homage to the
teacher". नमः you know; the other word is गुरुः wearing a shape that means *to the
teacher* — नमः reaches its object through that shape. The system behind it is the case
machinery of Volume 3. Today: recognize the famous chunk, translate it on sight, and build
no new ones — the pattern is on display, not on duty.

### Pause-form dress, unchanged

The kit keeps the sentence dress you know: भद्रम् and स्वागतम् wear म्, नमस्कारः wears ः,
and turns still end in daṇḍa with a full beat between speakers. Questions still rise;
greetings now fall warmly (§9). Nothing else moves.

honesty, not hierarchy — and today it costs nothing because the entire kit is classical.

dictation. The palm test separates speed problems from rule problems honestly.

**What students find hard:** the visarga on भोः dying at speed; the pause-form beat
collapsing once the round gets fun (call the beat aloud for the first two rounds); the
labels heard as rankings; and writing ंज for ञ्ज in dictation — send them back to the
stop-chooses-its-nasal rule, not to memory.
