## 1. Hook

Your calendar has been doing arithmetic behind your back. December carries a *ten*,
November a *nine*, October an *eight* — and the 1.x edition of this very course printed
that Sanskrit sent them there. It did not, and the true story is grander. Today you take
the four words that finish your counting hand — six, seven, eight, nine — put the little
question that asks for all of them, an old Volume 1 friend, to full work, and you earn
the right to tell the month-name story the honest way: one ancestor counted first, and two calendars still carry its numbers.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिकः सन्दर्भः — the steadiest words in any language, and a correction delivered as
a gift.

Numbers live in the same deep drawer as the kinship words of Chapter 2: learned in
childhood, used daily, almost never replaced. That stability is why the numerals are
textbook evidence for the Indo-European family — and why this chapter can hand you a
cognate shelf with total confidence.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The Proto-Indo-European numerals are among the most stable items of
> inherited vocabulary: Sanskrit, Latin, Greek and English carry the same words for two
> through ten ("one" is the famous exception — lesson 1's honestly empty slot),
> each worn down by its own regular sound changes — parallel inheritance, not borrowing.
> [REF:mallory2006] [REF:burrow2001]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The same inheritance runs *downward* inside Indo-Aryan: Hindi छह,
> सात, आठ, नौ, दस are the direct descendants of षट्, सप्त, अष्ट, नव, दश — the regular
> गृह → घर story, five more times. [REF:masica1991]

One line of course history, told kindly: the 1.x curriculum's numbers unit (PDF pp.30–31)
taught the correct stems — the audit kept every word — and then printed a derivation
claim about the English month names that does not hold. This lesson is that salvage: the
words unchanged, the story finally honest, exactly as the course corrected the और gloss
with च in v2.c02.l04. §13 is the operating theatre.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिकं विवरणम् — why numbers must be automatic, why the drills are timed, and what
your tongue is really practicing.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Number words are the classic case for fluency work: a digit's name
> must arrive without translation, or counts and dates stay unusable in real speech.
> Timed retrieval of small chunks — the digit-name ladder, the bare कति exchange — is the
> fluency strand doing its job. [REF:nation2013]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Spaced retrieval beats massed re-study; today's Station 0 is
> l01's +1-day interval landing on schedule, and this lesson books its own +1-day and
> +1-week re-runs before it ends (§20). [REF:cepeda2006] [REF:roediger2006]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Sanskrit contrasts dental and retroflex consonants as different
> speech sounds — tongue on the teeth versus tongue-tip curled back — and retroflexion is
> a defining areal feature of the South Asian languages (not unique to them: Swedish and
> Norwegian have retroflexes too). Today's list is a minimal workout for exactly that
> contrast: षट् and अष्ट curled, सप्त and दश on the teeth. [REF:masica1991]

Hold the third fact in your mouth, not your head: the ladder in §9 is where it lives.

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतेन विवरणम् — the counting, told in lines you fully own:

> **एकम् द्वे त्रीणि चत्वारि पञ्च षट् सप्त अष्ट नव दश।**
> (ekam dve trīṇi catvāri pañca ṣaṭ sapta aṣṭa nava daśa.) "One, two, three, four, five,
> six, seven, eight, nine, ten." — एक, दो, तीन, चार, पाँच, छह, सात, आठ, नौ, दस।

> **कति? — षट्!**
> (kati? — ṣaṭ!) "How many? — Six!" — कितने? — छह!

> **तत्र कति? — दश!**
> (tatra kati? — daśa!) "How many there? — Ten!" — वहाँ कितने? — दस!

Two machines, both honest: the chant is lesson 1's counting dress, now grown to ten; the
कति exchange is the question kit's oldest member (v1.c02.l01) back at work, and its answer stands *bare* — no verb, no endings, the number alone. That bare
answer is not a shortcut we apologize for; it is the taught, standard pattern, and §15 says exactly why.

## 8. English Explanation

No new letter, no new sign — the Volume 2 luxury holds. The load is four new headwords,
two old friends back on duty — दश and the question word कति — and the chapter's flagship
correction.

### The set — the counting hand completes

**षट्** (ṣaṭ) "six" and **अष्ट** (aṣṭa) "eight" are the retroflex showcase: ष and ट both
want the tongue-tip curled back, and अष्ट welds them into one gesture, ष्ट. **सप्त**
(sapta) "seven" answers from the other rail — प्त welded, त on the teeth. **नव** (nava)
"nine" looks like an old friend and is honestly a new one: §13 performs the surgery.
**दश** (daśa) "ten" is genuinely an old friend — readable since v1.c04, its Latin sibling
*decem* already on record — back today as a review row on counting duty, not a new ledger
entry. And **कति** (kati) "how many?" is the tool that makes the other ten words usable:
the classical interrogative of quantity, answered bare.

With lesson 1's एक द्वि त्रि चतुर् पञ्च, the citation names now run 1–10 — the
shapes this course lists and answers with (the dictionaries head five to ten under their
stems पञ्चन्, षष्, सप्तन्, अष्टन्, नवन्, दशन् — which is why §13 meets नवन्).

### The chant grows to ten

एकम् द्वे त्रीणि चत्वारि पञ्च षट् सप्त अष्ट नव दश — the same honest label as lesson 1:
these are real, inflected counting forms (neuter where gender shows, one to four),
Sanskrit's counting dress, learned whole as practice while the system behind them waits
for its proper chapter. Notice something pleasant: from six onward the dress and the
citation name look identical — nothing new to wear.
That is an observation about this chant, not a law to extract (§15).

### कति back on question duty

The kit built since Volume 1 — कः · का · किम् · कुतः · कथम् · कुत्र — puts its oldest
member to counting work: **कति** "how many?" — no stranger: a §14 headword since
v1.c02.l01, answered with एक since v1.c02.l04, and now with the whole count. Every asker still starts with that same ancient k. The
grammar of कति today is one line long: it asks, and the answer may stand bare — कति? —
सप्त! No verb, no endings, no agreement. "How many *books*" — the number dressed to match
a noun — is real Sanskrit and really deferred; the bare answer carries everything this
chapter needs (§15).

### Two fences and a company dress

The title षडादिदशान्ताः "the ones from six to ten" shows षट् wearing a company dress,
षड्, before a voiced neighbor — joining rules are Volume 3's business; read it, build
nothing, pause form between words as always. And two things are deliberately not here:
numeral agreement ("eight books" waits for its machinery, §15), and the time words —
कालः, अद्य and the clock arrive next lesson with their own showcase.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — no new sounds; the work is one contrast, drilled until the tongue owns it.
Audio: 🔊 `planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c03l02-drill.mp3` (drills 1–3),
`planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c03l02-word-list.mp3` (three speeds + dictation track).

**Drill 1 — the curl ladder.** Slow motion, feeling the tongue-tip travel: स on the
teeth, ष curled back; त on the teeth, ट curled back. Now the words: षट् — curled at
*both* ends, the final ट् complete and alone, no helper vowel. Then अष्ट — curl for ष,
*hold the curl* through ट: ष्ट is one gesture, not two visits. Five reps each.

**Drill 2 — the two-rail roll-call.** With the audio, fixed order, three passes (slow ·
natural · timed): the curled pair षट् — अष्ट, then the teeth pair सप्त — दश, then नव
between them (न on the teeth, व lip-to-teeth), then all five in counting order: षट् सप्त
अष्ट नव दश. The alternation is the drill: curl, teeth, curl, teeth.

**Drill 3 — the chant to ten.** एकम् द्वे त्रीणि चत्वारि पञ्च षट् सप्त अष्ट नव दश — even
beat, long vowels given their two beats (त्रीणि, चत्वारि), and watch the hidden retroflex:
त्रीणि carries ण. Then the digit-name ladder: shuffled cards १–१०, name each within a
beat.

**Drill 4 — the कति volley.** The audio claps a count or deals a card; you answer inside
the beat: कति? — नव! Second round, you ask: point at anything in the room — कति? — and
answer yourself, bare. Timed retrieval of chunks: the fluency strand at work
[REF:nation2013].

## 10. IPA

No new values — every symbol has been on your chart since Volume 1.

**Word transcriptions:** षट् /ʂɐʈ/ · सप्त /sɐpt̪ɐ/ · अष्ट /ɐʂʈɐ/ · नव /n̪ɐʋɐ/ · कति
/kɐt̪i/ · दश /d̪ɐɕɐ/. Chant forms (display): एकम् /eːkɐm/ · द्वे /d̪ʋeː/ · त्रीणि
/t̪ɾiːɳi/ · चत्वारि /t͡ɕɐt̪ʋɑːɾi/ · पञ्च /pɐɲt͡ɕɐ/.

**Narrow note — the ष्ट weld:** in अष्ट the fricative and the stop share one tongue
posture — /ʂʈ/ with the tip curled throughout. A seam vowel ("aśata") or a palatal slide
("aśṭa" with ś) means the curl broke; re-run §9 drill 1.

**Narrow note — दश is not all dental:** द sits on the teeth /d̪/, but श is the *palatal*
sibilant /ɕ/ — the श of दर्शन, not the ष of षट्. The dental-retroflex contrast lives in
the stops and in स/ष; श is a third place, already yours since v1.c04.l02.

**Narrow note — नव:** /n̪ɐʋɐ/ — dental न, labiodental ʋ, both vowels full. It is
pronounced exactly like नव "new" — that identity of sound with difference of word is
§13's whole point.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थितिः — four checks; the articulators know all of this already. The tongue-map strip
on the cognate shelf card (`planned:assets/images/volume-2/v2c03l02-cognate-shelf.svg`)
marks the two rails.

1. **The curl check.** Say षट् in slow motion and freeze on ष: the tongue-tip is up and
   back, not touching the teeth. Keep it there for ट्. If the tip drifts forward you have
   said *सट् — the flattened error.
2. **The weld check.** अष्ट with a finger on your chin: one jaw gesture for ष्ट, no
   bounce between the two letters. A bounce means a vowel leaked into the cluster.
3. **The teeth check.** सप्त and दश: the त and द touch the *backs of the teeth* — further
   forward than English t/d. English speakers: aim past the ridge; Hindi speakers: you
   own this — keep it under speed.
4. **The final check.** षट् ends on ट् — released cleanly, lips open, no echo vowel. The
   mirror shows a still mouth after the ट्, exactly like the final म् discipline of
   गृहम्, transposed.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्याः भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix; these feed the quiz distractors.

1. ***सट् — the flattened six.** Dental स takes over for retroflex ष. *Fix:* §11 check 1
   — curl first, then speak; the ladder in §9 drill 1 rebuilds the habit.
2. ***अश्ट — the broken weld.** Palatal श slides in for ष, or the cluster splits into two
   visits ("aśata"). *Fix:* one curled gesture, held — §10's narrow note, §11 check 2.
3. **The contrast flipped on the other rail.** Over-correcting until सप्त and दश curl
   too, or letting English alveolar t/d split the difference. *Fix:* §11 check 3 — the
   two rails are a *contrast*; drill them as alternation (§9 drill 2).
4. **The direction flip:** "November comes from Sanskrit नव." The 1.x book printed it;
   posters still do. *Fix:* §13 — Latin is counting its own calendar; अष्ट and octō are
   siblings, and nobody borrowed anybody's numbers. The chapter exam's task D card is
   built from this exact line (v2.c03.l05).
5. **The merger:** treating नव "nine" and नव "new" as one word with two meanings. *Fix:*
   two distinct words that fell together in sound (§13); two separate English cousins —
   *nine* and *new* — prove the split.
6. **Invented grammar under pressure:** gluing endings onto a कति answer, or reaching for
   a verb (षट् सन्ति — untaught plural machinery; next chapter's verb bank stays
   singular; plural verbs wait for Volumes 3–4). *Fix:* the bare
   answer is the taught, standard form (§15); simplest correct beats ambitious wrong.
   Related: digit misreads at the ladder — ९ taken for ७, ८ for ५ — slow the ladder
   before speeding it (v1.c05.l05 digit cards).

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — three stories inside today's list, and one of them is the chapter's flagship.

### The October fix — the month names, told right

The 1.x curriculum (PDF pp.30–31) taught these very numbers and then captioned them:
"Many English words come from these! Tri-angle, Oct-agon, Nov-ember, Dec-ember" — and
under the ten, "दश (10) → December, Decimal". Said warmly: the words were right and the arrow
was wrong. Here is the story the evidence tells.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** October, November and December do not come from Sanskrit. They are
> Latin counting its own calendar: *octō*, *novem*, *decem* — "eight, nine, ten" — naming
> the 8th, 9th and 10th months of the old Roman year, which began in March. Latin *octō*
> and Sanskrit अष्ट are cognates — siblings descended from one Proto-Indo-European
> ancestor; neither derives from the other, and the same holds down the whole row of
> numerals. [REF:mallory2006] [REF:burrow2001]

And the honest version is the *better* story: not one language lending a word to a
calendar, but one ancestor's numbers surviving in two calendars — Latin counting its
year, Sanskrit counting its own, the same inheritance held twice, each line of descent
thousands of years deep. The course made exactly this move once before: the 1.x gloss
of और was corrected with च, kindly, in v2.c02.l04. Same pattern, bigger stage.

### The shelf — five siblings, no arrows

| Sanskrit | Latin | English |
|---|---|---|
| षट् | sex | six |
| सप्त | septem | seven |
| अष्ट | octō | eight |
| नव | novem | nine |
| दश | decem | ten |

Parallel descendants of the PIE numerals — among the most stable words in the family;
there is no arrow anywhere on this shelf [REF:mallory2006] [REF:burrow2001]. Lesson 1's
rows (चतुर् beside *quattuor*, एक honestly alone) sit above these; and the asker gets a
shelf row of its own — कति beside Latin *quot* "how many", the same question inherited
twice [REF:burrow2001].

### The नव split — one sound, two words, two cousins

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** नव "nine" and नव "new" are two distinct Sanskrit words — the
> dictionaries file them separately, with distinct stems (नवन् for the numeral, नव for
> the adjective) — that happen to fall together as नव — the numeral's counting form and
> the adjective's stem. Both have English
> cognates, and they are *different* ones: *nine* beside the numeral, *new* beside the
> adjective. [REF:monierwilliams1899 s.vv.]

Your ledger met नव in v1.c04.l01 wearing the double gloss "new; nine" — one row holding
two words until the course could afford the distinction. Today it can. The row splits
with full honors: नव "new" keeps its v1.c04 seat, and नव "nine" enters the ledger as
today's headword — not an erratum but a promise of exactness, kept the moment it became
teachable. And it hands you the cleanest proof that sound alone does not decide wordhood:
English kept the cousins apart, *nine* and *new*, all along.

3. **(5–10 min, teach-back)** Tell someone the November story told right: novem is
   Latin's own nine, counting the ninth month of a year that began in March; Sanskrit नव
   is its sibling, not its source — one ancestor counted first, and two calendars still
   carry its numbers. If they ask "so who borrowed from whom?", you own the best answer
   in the chapter: nobody — they inherited.

## 22. Teacher Notes

अध्यापकटिप्पणी

**Open with the calendar riddle, not the list.** Write OCTOBER · NOVEMBER · DECEMBER on
the board and under them 8 · 9 · 10. Ask: these are the 10th, 11th and 12th months — why
are they numbered 8, 9, 10, and in which language? Collect votes; let someone offer "from
Sanskrit" if the room carries it. Then deliver the real answer — Latin counting its own
old year, which began in March — and promise the Sanskrit connection is *better* than
derivation: siblinghood. The lesson's correction lands best as a solved riddle.

**Timing plan (45 min):** Station 0 + prompts, scores logged (7) → the calendar riddle
(5) → set walk-through on the two rails; the curl and weld drills (8) → कति volley in
pairs; bare answers enforced warmly (6) → chant to ten + digit-name ladder (6) → the
October fix on the month-name story card; the नव split (8) → word list at three speeds;
dictation launch (3) → exercises, homework, teach-back brief (2).

**Board work:** two horizontal rails — a curled-tongue icon over षट् · अष्ट, a teeth icon
over सप्त · दश, नव and कति between them. Beside it, the month ribbon from the story card:
March first, slots 8-9-10 highlighted. Keep one corner for the ledger tick: 279 → 283.

**Run the October fix as a gift, not a scolding.** Someone in the room learned "November
comes from नव" from a poster, a relative, or the 1.x edition of this very course — nobody
gets to feel foolish. The course's standing move (the और → च correction) applies
verbatim: name the old claim kindly, show what the evidence supports, and let the honest
version be the prouder one — one ancestor's numbers in two calendars.

**Differentiation:** Hindi speakers own the retroflex contrast natively — their work is
the finals (षट् ends on a bare ट्, no helper vowel), the ष्ट weld, and the direction
honesty, where "these are just our numbers" is precisely what exercise 4 examines.
English-side learners drill the curl ladder (§9 drill 1) before any speed work, and take
the two-rail roll-call slowly first.

**What students find hard:** holding the curl through ष्ट at speed (slow it down; one
gesture); believing the bare कति answer is *correct* rather than babyish — it is the
taught classical pattern, and inventing agreement is the error; the नव split feeling like
a trick — frame it as the course keeping a promise of exactness, with *nine*/*new* as the
proof; and, every chapter, accepting that "reject" can be the loving verdict on a
patriotic card — the teach-back in §21 is where that lands.
