## 1. Hook

Say "I like milk" in English and *you* are the one doing something: you like, the milk just
sits there. Sanskrit tells the same moment from the other end. क्षीरम् मह्यम् रोचते —
"milk pleases me". The milk does the pleasing; you receive it. If you speak Hindi, you
already think this way: मुझे दूध पसंद है puts "me" in a to-form, not in the doer's seat.
Today your Sanskrit learns the two things every traveller, guest and child needs first —
how to say what you *want* and what you *like* — and finds out why the two are built from
opposite ends.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिकः सन्दर्भः — two everyday needs, two ordinary classical words, and a gloss the
course now owes you.

Wanting and liking are among the first things any speaker needs, and classical Sanskrit
has plain, frequent words for both. Nothing about today's vocabulary is rare, poetic or
modern-coined; it is the language of ordinary requests and ordinary preferences.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** All four of today's words are ordinary classical Sanskrit:
> इच्छति "wants, wishes"; रोचते "pleases", used with the person pleased in a to/for
> form; मह्यम् "to me, for me" and तुभ्यम् "to you, for you", the to/for forms of अहम्
> and त्वम्. [REF:apte1890 s.vv. इष्, रुच्]; [REF:whitney1889]

One line of course history, stated as the fix it is. The 1.x course printed मह्यम् रोचते
with a plain English *I like* beside it and no note on how the sentence works. The words
were right; the silence was the problem. A learner who trusts that gloss builds "I like
the fruit" the English way — with the liker as the doer — and breaks the sentence on the
first try. In 2.0 the gloss stays, and the honest line arrives with it: literally, "it
pleases me" (§15).

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिकं विवरणम् — the experiencer, and why today's drills are built the way they are.

Every language must say who feels a liking and what is liked. Linguists call the person
who feels it the *experiencer*, and languages differ in where they seat it.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** English makes the experiencer the subject: *I like milk*. Hindi's
> everyday liking sentences seat it in a को-form and let the liked thing run the verb:
> मुझे दूध पसंद है। मुझे किताब अच्छी लगती है। — the verb follows किताब, not "me". Hindi
> builds needing the same way: मुझे पानी चाहिए। [REF:masica1991]

Sanskrit sides with Hindi for liking — क्षीरम् मह्यम् रोचते — and with English for
wanting — अहम् क्षीरम् इच्छामि, the wanter as subject, exactly as in the Chapter 4
doing-dialogue. One lesson, two builds facing opposite ways: that is the whole difficulty
of today, and the drills attack it head-on by keeping the two frames side by side.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Conversation-sized chunks are learned fastest when taught whole and
> analysed later; a chunk you can say the hour you meet it becomes the evidence the later
> analysis is built on. मह्यम् रोचते and किम् इच्छसि? are taught exactly so.
> [REF:nation2013]

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Spaced retrieval beats massed re-study; today's Station 0 is lesson
> 2's +1-day interval landing on schedule, and this lesson books its own +1-day re-run
> before it ends (§20). [REF:cepeda2006] [REF:roediger2006]

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतेन विवरणम् — the two frames, in sentences you fully own:

> **अहम् जलम् इच्छामि।**
> (aham jalam icchāmi.) "I want water." — मैं जल चाहता/चाहती हूँ।

> **फलम् मह्यम् रोचते।**
> (phalam mahyam rocate.) "I like the fruit." Literally: "The fruit pleases me." — मुझे
> फल अच्छा लगता है।

> **किम् इच्छसि? — किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?**
> (kim icchasi? — kim tubhyam rocate?) "What do you want? — What do you like?" — तुम क्या
> चाहते हो? — तुम्हें क्या अच्छा लगता है?

Read the first two lines as a pair. In the first, अहम् stands first and does the
wanting — the Chapter 4 shape. In the second, फलम् stands first and does the *pleasing*;
the person who likes it waits in मह्यम्. Pause form throughout, every word whole.

## 7. Hindi Explanation

हिन्दी में समझिए

आज दो बहुत काम की बातें सीखनी हैं — चाहना और पसंद करना। दोनों हर भाषा में रोज़ की
ज़रूरत हैं, और मज़ेदार बात यह है कि संस्कृत इन दोनों को उलटे सिरों से बनाती है।

पहली बात — **चाहना**। इच्छति का अर्थ है "चाहता है"। यह अध्याय 4 के नियमित साँचे पर
चलता है: इच्छामि "मैं चाहता/चाहती हूँ", इच्छसि "तुम चाहते/चाहती हो", इच्छति "वह चाहता/
चाहती है"। यहाँ कर्ता — यानी वाक्य का "करने वाला" — वही है जो चाहता है, ठीक हिन्दी
के "मैं पानी चाहता हूँ" की तरह: अहम् जलम् इच्छामि। पूछना हो तो: किम् इच्छसि? — "तुम
क्या चाहते हो?" आपकी हिन्दी में इच्छा शब्द पहले से है — वह संस्कृत का ही शब्द है,
और इच्छति उसी परिवार का है (§13)।

एक चेतावनी: हिन्दी में हम अक्सर "मुझे पानी चाहिए" भी कहते हैं — यह "मुझे" वाला ढाँचा
है। इच्छामि के साथ यह ढाँचा नहीं चलता। "मह्यम् जलम् इच्छामि" ग़लत है; चाहने वाला
वाक्य हमेशा अहम् से — अहम् जलम् इच्छामि।

दूसरी बात — **पसंद**। यहाँ हिन्दी बोलने वालों को सीधा लाभ है। हम रोज़ कहते हैं "मुझे
फल पसंद है", "मुझे फल अच्छा लगता है"। इन वाक्यों में पसंद करने वाला "मुझे" बनकर बैठता
है, और क्रिया उस चीज़ के साथ चलती है जो पसंद आ रही है: "मुझे किताब अच्छी लगती है" —
"अच्छी लगती", क्योंकि किताब स्त्रीलिंग है, "मैं" नहीं। हाँ, हिन्दी में "मैं फल पसंद
करता हूँ" भी चलता है — पर वह अंग्रेज़ी वाला ढाँचा है। संस्कृत का रोचते ठीक "मुझे अच्छा
लगता है" वाला काम करता है:

फलम् मह्यम् रोचते। — फल मुझे अच्छा लगता है।

फलम् — जो चीज़ पसंद आ रही है — इस वाक्य का कर्ता है। मह्यम् का अर्थ है "मुझे, मेरे
लिए"; तुभ्यम् का "तुम्हें, तुम्हारे लिए"; रोचते का "अच्छा लगता है, भाता है"। अंग्रेज़ी
का I like उलटा चलता है — वहाँ पसंद करने वाला ही कर्ता है। तीन भाषाओं में हिन्दी और
संस्कृत एक तरफ़, अंग्रेज़ी अकेली दूसरी तरफ़।

इसका एक बड़ा नतीजा: **रोचते बदलता नहीं**। "मुझे पसंद है" हो या "तुम्हें पसंद है",
कर्ता तो फल ही है — एक "वह"। इसलिए: फलम् मह्यम् रोचते। फलम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? बदलते हैं
केवल मह्यम् और तुभ्यम्। "अहम् फलम् रोचामि" में दो ग़लतियाँ एक साथ हैं — पसंद करने वाले
को कर्ता बना दिया, और रोचते पर अध्याय 4 का -मि चिपका दिया।

एक ईमानदार पंक्ति: रोचते के अन्त में -ते है, -ति नहीं। संस्कृत क्रियाओं के अन्त-चिह्नों
का एक दूसरा परिवार भी है — परम्परा इसे आत्मनेपद कहती है — और रोचते उसी परिवार का
शब्द है। आज इसे पूरा शब्द मानकर सीखिए। यह व्यवस्था, और मह्यम्-तुभ्यम् जैसे रूप कैसे
बनते हैं — यानी विभक्ति, शब्द के रूप-परिवर्तन की पूरी कहानी — खण्ड 3–4 में आएगी।

उच्चारण की चार चौकियाँ। पहली — इच्छति में वही च्छ की गाँठ है जो गच्छति में थी: छोटी
इ, फिर जीभ की पकड़, फिर एक ही बार फूँक के साथ खुलना — "इछति" नहीं। दूसरी — मह्यम्:
"महियम्" नहीं; ह और य के बीच कोई स्वर नहीं, ह की साँस सीधे य में बहती है। तीसरी —
तुभ्यम्: भ की भारी साँस भी बिना स्वर के य तक पहुँचे, और तु का त दाँतों वाला। चौथी —
रोचते: ओ और ए दोनों लम्बे और शुद्ध; अन्त का ते पूरा रहे — दस-क्रिया कोश की आदत से उसे
"रोचति" मत बनाइए।

## 8. English Explanation

No new letter, no new sign — four new words, two new frames, and one honest line that
changes how you hear the 1.x gloss forever.

### The four, one by one

**इच्छति** (icchati) "wants, wishes" — a regular verb, one more engine for the Chapter 4
pattern. Front इच्छ-, tails -मि/-सि/-ति, the joint stretched in the I-row:
**इच्छामि** (icchāmi) "I want", **इच्छसि** (icchasi) "you want", **इच्छति** "he/she
wants". Its च्छ is गच्छति's knot exactly, now after a short इ. The wanter is the subject,
the wanted thing wears its म्-dress glossed whole — the doing-dialogue chassis of
v2.c04.l03 with a new engine: **किम् इच्छसि? — अहम् जलम् इच्छामि।** And the polite
round runs as it always has, भवान् with the he/she form: **भवान् किम् इच्छति?**

**मह्यम्** (mahyam) "to me, for me" and **तुभ्यम्** (tubhyam) "to you, for you" — two
whole words, and the first thing to say about them is what they are *not*: they are not
"I" and "you". अहम् and त्वम् are still your doers. मह्यम् and तुभ्यम् are the same two
people standing in a different place in the sentence — the place where something comes
*to* them. You learn them today as two chunks; how Sanskrit builds such forms is
Volume 3's subject. तुभ्यम् is the familiar partner, matching त्वम्; with a teacher or
a stranger you will ask about wanting with भवान् for now (§15).

**रोचते** (rocate) "pleases" — a he/she/it form, like every bank verb's citation form,
but look at its tail: -ते, not -ति. That is not a misprint. Sanskrit verbs come in two
families of endings, and रोचते wears the other one — the family linguists call the
*middle voice*, which the tradition calls **आत्मनेपद** (ātmanepada). One line is the
whole of it today: रोचते is learned whole; the second ending family is Volume 3–4
business, exactly as v2.c04.l02 promised when it said Sanskrit has "a second voice with
a second set of endings".

### The honest line — "it pleases me"

Here is the line the 1.x gloss left out. **मह्यम् रोचते does not literally say "I like".
It says "it pleases me".** Three facts sit inside that sentence, and you need all three:

1. **The liked thing is the subject.** In फलम् मह्यम् रोचते, the fruit is the doer — it
   does the pleasing.
2. **The liker stands in a to/for form.** मह्यम् "to me"; for a question to a friend,
   तुभ्यम् "to you".
3. **रोचते wears the other ending family** — the middle voice, taught whole.

So the translation runs through a turn: *I like the fruit* → *the fruit pleases me* →
**फलम् मह्यम् रोचते।** The pleasure travels from the thing to the person — and that is
exactly the arrow on today's diagram (`planned:assets/diagrams/volume-2/v2c05l03-pleases-me-arrow.svg`).

### The Hindi bridge — and the odd one out

Hindi's everyday **मुझे फल पसंद है** (§5) puts the liker in a को-form and lets the liked
thing run the verb. Sanskrit's रोचते and Hindi's अच्छा लगता है face the same way. (Hindi
also has मैं … पसंद करता हूँ, built the English way; रोचते is never built like that.)
**English *I like* is the odd one out** of the three.

### One verb that never moves

Because the liked thing is the subject, and today's liked things are all single
he/she/it things, **रोचते never changes in today's frames** — फलम् मह्यम् रोचते।, फलम्
तुभ्यम् रोचते? — only the to-form moves. In the wanting frame the verb moves with its
doer, because there the doer *is* the person. Two frames, opposite ends:

| | Wanting — the I-frame | Liking — the it-frame |
|---|---|---|
| Who is the subject? | the wanter: अहम्, त्वम् | the liked thing: फलम्, क्षीरम् |
| Where is the person? | in the subject slot | in the to-form: मह्यम्, तुभ्यम् |
| Does the verb change? | yes — इच्छामि · इच्छसि · इच्छति | not today — always रोचते |
| Ask | किम् इच्छसि? | किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? |
| Answer | अहम् जलम् इच्छामि। | क्षीरम् मह्यम् रोचते। |
| Say no | अहम् जलम् न इच्छामि। | क्षीरम् मह्यम् न रोचते। |

The things wanted and liked all come from the pantry you own (§14); a whole market of new
things arrives tomorrow (v2.c05.l04).

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — no new sounds; one old knot, two new welds, and a final vowel that is not ई
and not "ay". Audio: 🔊 `planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c05l03-drill.mp3` (drills 1–5),
`planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c05l03-word-list.mp3` (word list, three speeds +
dictation track).

**Drill 1 — the knot again.** Climb with the audio: इ — इच् — इच्छ — इच्छति, then
इच्छामि, इच्छसि. The च्छ is गच्छति's gesture exactly: seal, a held beat, ONE release with
the puff riding it. Say गच्छति — इच्छति as a pair until the two knots feel identical.

**Drill 2 — the ह्य weld.** म — मह् — मह्यम्. The ह is voiced breath; let it run
straight into the य glide with no vowel between. Two syllables, mah-yam — never three
("ma-hi-yam"). Then the pair मह्यम् — अहम् to feel the same ह in two places.

**Drill 3 — the भ्य weld.** तु — तुभ् — तुभ्यम्. Lips close for भ, open with a breathy
release, and the tongue is already in the य position as they part. Dental त at the start,
short उ, no vowel sneaking in after भ.

**Drill 4 — the two long vowels of रोचते.** रो — रोच — रोचते. Both ओ and ए are long,
pure, steady — no glide in either. Then the contrast that matters most today: पठति —
रोचते, पिबति — रोचते. The bank's verbs end in a short -ति; रोचते ends in a long -ते.
Hearing and keeping that difference *is* hearing the second ending family.

**Drill 5 — the frame roll-call.** On an even beat, three passes (slow · natural ·
timed): किम् इच्छसि? — अहम् जलम् इच्छामि। — किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? — फलम् मह्यम् रोचते। —
अहम् जलम् न इच्छामि। — क्षीरम् मह्यम् न रोचते। The किम् questions keep the level tune
(the word asks); a yes/no question with no question word rises — फलम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थितिः — four checks; the articulators know all of this already. The weld strip on the
picture cards (`planned:assets/images/volume-2/v2c05l03-want-like-cards.svg`) marks the
च्छ, ह्य and भ्य joins.

1. **The knot check (इच्छति).** Freeze inside च्छ: tongue-body sealed against the palate,
   a held beat, then ONE release with a puff you can feel on your palm. Two releases mean
   the knot slipped.
2. **The voiced-breath check (मह्यम्).** Hand on your throat: the buzz of voicing runs
   through ह and on into य without a break. If the buzz stops, you have made English *h*;
   if a vowel appears between them, you have made "mahiyam".
3. **The lip check (तुभ्यम्).** Lips close for भ and part with a breathy release while
   the tongue already sits high and forward for य. One movement, no extra vowel.
4. **The mirror check (रोचते).** Lips round and hold for ओ; for the final ए the lips
   spread and the jaw stays still to the last beat. A jaw that rises at the end has
   glided into "ay"; a clipped last beat has turned ते into ति.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्याः भ्रान्तयः — real errors, each with its fix; these feed the quiz distractors.

1. **"I like" built the English way: \*अहम् फलम् रोचते। or \*अहम् फलम् रोचामि।** THE
   flagged error of the chapter. Both make the liker the subject; the second also forces
   the Chapter 4 tail -मि onto रोचते. *Fix:* start from the thing — *the fruit pleases
   me* — फलम् मह्यम् रोचते। (§8, §15).
2. **The verb bent to the liker: \*क्षीरम् तुभ्यम् रोचसि?** "You" like it, so the verb
   gets a you-tail. But the subject is the milk, a single "it". *Fix:* रोचते never moves
   in today's frames; only मह्यम्/तुभ्यम् change — क्षीरम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?
3. **The Hindi चाहिए transfer: \*मह्यम् जलम् इच्छामि।** Hindi's मुझे पानी चाहिए is a
   "to me" build, and it leaks into the wanting frame. *Fix:* wanting is the I-frame —
   अहम् जलम् इच्छामि। The to-form belongs to रोचते, not to इच्छति.
4. **मह्यम् read as "I".** Learners who meet मह्यम् रोचते as a two-word "I like" start
   using मह्यम् as a doer anywhere. *Fix:* मह्यम् means "to me, for me"; your doer is
   still अहम्. Say the literal line aloud each time: "it pleases me".
5. **रोचते regularized: "rocati".** The bank's -ति habit swallows the -ते. *Fix:* §9
   drill 4 — पठति · रोचते until the long final ए is automatic.
6. **The welds re-voweled: "mahiyam", "tubhiyam".** A helper vowel splits ह्य or भ्य.
   *Fix:* §9 drills 2–3; two syllables each, mah-yam, tubh-yam.
7. **Two liked things in one sentence: \*फलम् क्षीरम् च मह्यम् रोचते।** Today's liked
   things are subjects, and joined subjects change the verb's shape — v2.c02.l04's rule.
   *Fix:* two sentences and अपि — फलम् मह्यम् रोचते। क्षीरम् अपि मह्यम् रोचते। (Joined
   *wanted* things are fine: अहम् जलम् फलम् च इच्छामि। — there the verb follows अहम्.)
8. **Reporting someone else's liking: \*अर्जुनः फलम् रोचते।** It sounds like "Arjuna
   likes the fruit" but says nothing coherent — the liker would need a to-form of अर्जुन,
   which is Volume 3's. *Fix:* today you say what pleases *you* and ask what pleases the
   person in front of you; about others, report what they *want* — अर्जुनः फलम् इच्छति।

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — both frames at work across the pantry, the three-language turn, and Hindi's
open door.

### Wanting — the I-frame

- **अहम् जलम् इच्छामि।** (aham jalam icchāmi.) "I want water." — मैं पानी चाहता हूँ।
- **अहम् फलम् इच्छामि।** (aham phalam icchāmi.) "I want a fruit." — मैं फल चाहती हूँ।
- **त्वम् किम् इच्छसि?** (tvam kim icchasi?) "What do you want?" — तुम क्या चाहते हो?
- **अहम् अन्नम् न इच्छामि।** (aham annam na icchāmi.) "I don't want food." — मैं खाना
  नहीं चाहता।
- **माया क्षीरम् इच्छति।** (māyā kṣīram icchati.) "Māyā wants milk." — माया दूध चाहती है।

The last line matters: wanting can be *reported* about anyone, because the wanter is
simply the subject — any named doer takes -ति, as since Chapter 4.

### Liking — the it-frame

- **फलम् मह्यम् रोचते।** (phalam mahyam rocate.) "I like the fruit." — मुझे फल अच्छा
  लगता है।
- **क्षीरम् मह्यम् रोचते।** (kṣīram mahyam rocate.) "I like milk." — मुझे दूध अच्छा लगता
  है।
- **पुस्तकम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?** (pustakam tubhyam rocate?) "Do you like the book?" —
  तुम्हें किताब अच्छी लगती है?
- **किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?** (kim tubhyam rocate?) "What do you like?" — तुम्हें क्या अच्छा
  लगता है?
- **अन्नम् मह्यम् न रोचते।** (annam mahyam na rocate.) "I don't like the food." — मुझे
  यह खाना अच्छा नहीं लगता।

Look at the fourth line once more. In किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? the question word stands in the
*subject* slot — "what pleases you?" — so the answer simply drops the thing into that
slot and swaps the to-form: क्षीरम् मह्यम् रोचते।

### The arrow — three languages, one moment

| English | Turned | Sanskrit | Hindi |
|---|---|---|---|
| I like the fruit. | The fruit pleases me. | फलम् मह्यम् रोचते। | मुझे फल अच्छा लगता है। |
| Do you like milk? | Does milk please you? | क्षीरम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? | तुम्हें दूध अच्छा लगता है? |
| I like Sanskrit. | Sanskrit pleases me. | संस्कृतम् मह्यम् रोचते। | मुझे संस्कृत अच्छी लगती है। |
| I don't like the book. | The book doesn't please me. | पुस्तकम् मह्यम् न रोचते। | मुझे किताब अच्छी नहीं लगती। |

The "Turned" column is the tool: from English, go through it every time until the
Sanskrit comes out on its own. From Hindi, no turn is needed — the Hindi already faces the
right way. The diagram draws one arrow per row, always from the thing to the person.

### Hindi's open door — इच्छा and रुचि

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Hindi इच्छा "wish, desire" and रुचि "interest, liking" are Sanskrit
> words that Hindi uses unchanged. Inside Sanskrit, इच्छा belongs to the same word family
> as इच्छति, and रुचि to the same family as रोचते. [REF:apte1890 s.vv. इच्छा, रुचि]

So a Hindi speaker already holds a door-handle for each new verb: इच्छा → इच्छति "wants";
रुचि → रोचते "pleases". The family is a memory hook, not a lesson in word-building — how
these families are built stays closed until Volume 4.

| फलम् | मह्यम् | रोचते |
| क्षीरम् | तुभ्यम् | रोचते |
| किम् — "what?" | तुभ्यम् | रोचते |

Order in today's sentences: thing — person — verb, the shape you will drill. Sanskrit
allows other orders (मह्यम् फलम् रोचते is also correct), but one order is drilled and
nothing built on it will need un-building.

### Step 3 — why रोचते never moves today

The verb follows its subject, and today's subjects — फलम्, क्षीरम्, किम् — are single
he/she/it things. There is no *I*-form or *you*-form of रोचते in this lesson, because
neither you nor I is the thing doing the pleasing.

### Step 4 — the other ending family, in one line

रोचते ends in -ते where the bank ends in -ति: Sanskrit's second family of verb endings,
the middle voice — आत्मनेपद (ātmanepada) in the tradition's name. Learn रोचते whole; the
family is Volume 3–4 business, as v2.c04.l02 §15's forward line promised.

### Step 5 — asking and answering: the swap

| You hear | You say | The swap |
|---|---|---|
| किम् इच्छसि? | अहम् जलम् इच्छामि। | -सि in → -मि out (Chapter 4's engine) |
| किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? | क्षीरम् मह्यम् रोचते। | तुभ्यम् in → मह्यम् out; किम् → the thing |
| भवान् किम् इच्छति? | अहम् फलम् इच्छामि। | polite question in — truthful I-form out |

### Step 6 — saying no

न stands just before the verb, in both frames:

- अहम् जलम् न इच्छामि। "I don't want water." — मैं पानी नहीं चाहता।
- फलम् मह्यम् न रोचते। "I don't like the fruit." — मुझे फल अच्छा नहीं लगता।

### Step 7 — yes/no questions: the rising tune

With no question word, your voice does the asking, as since Volume 1: **फलम् तुभ्यम्
रोचते?** (rising) — **आम्, फलम् मह्यम् रोचते।** or **न, फलम् मह्यम् न रोचते।** The same
for wanting: **जलम् इच्छसि?** — **आम्, अहम् जलम् इच्छामि।**

### What is deliberately not here

- **No "he likes".** Liking speaks only between you and me today — मह्यम् and तुभ्यम्.
  Liking reported about अर्जुनः or माता needs a to-form of each name: Volume 3. Report
  what others *want* instead (§12 mistake 8).
- **One liked thing per sentence.** Joined subjects change the verb's shape (v2.c02.l04);
  two likes are two sentences with अपि. Joined *wanted* things are fine.
- **No polite to-form.** भवान् has its own to-form; it waits. Ask a teacher what they
  want — भवान् किम् इच्छति? — and save "what do you like?" for friends.
- **No describing words.** "Sweet fruit", "cold milk" would need adjective agreement,
  and agreement is Volume 3's subject; the fact base defers it on purpose, so today's
  things stay plain.
- **Singular only, present only, pause form throughout.**

सम्भाषणम् — two scenes; every word is self-read. 🔊 audio:

### Scene 1 — snack time

Afternoon in the kitchen; माता looks at two hungry faces.

— माता: माया! किम् इच्छसि?
(māyā! kim icchasi?) "Māyā! What do you want?" — माया! तुम क्या चाहती हो?

— माया: अहम् क्षीरम् इच्छामि।
(aham kṣīram icchāmi.) "I want milk." — मैं दूध चाहती हूँ।

— माता: अर्जुनः! किम् त्वम् इच्छसि?
(arjunaḥ! kim tvam icchasi?) "Arjuna! And what do YOU want?" — अर्जुन! तुम क्या चाहते
हो?

— अर्जुनः: अहम् जलम् इच्छामि। फलम् अपि इच्छामि!
(aham jalam icchāmi. phalam api icchāmi!) "I want water. I want a fruit too!" — मैं पानी
चाहता हूँ। फल भी चाहता हूँ!

— माता: फलम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?
(phalam tubhyam rocate?) "Do you like fruit?" — तुम्हें फल अच्छा लगता है?

— अर्जुनः: आम्! फलम् मह्यम् रोचते।
(ām! phalam mahyam rocate.) "Yes! I like fruit." — हाँ! मुझे फल अच्छा लगता है।

— माया: क्षीरम् मह्यम् रोचते। अन्नम् अपि मह्यम् रोचते!
(kṣīram mahyam rocate. annam api mahyam rocate!) "I like milk. I like food too!" — मुझे
दूध अच्छा लगता है। खाना भी अच्छा लगता है!

— माता *(laughing)*: किम् तुभ्यम् न रोचते?
(kim tubhyam na rocate?) "Is there anything you DON'T like?" — तुम्हें क्या अच्छा नहीं
लगता?

— माया *(after a long think)*: सर्वम् मह्यम् रोचते!
(sarvam mahyam rocate!) "I like everything!" — मुझे सब कुछ अच्छा लगता है!

### Scene 2 — the classroom, too fast

The आचार्यः fires a question across the room at full speed.

— आचार्यः: कमला! किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?
(kamalā! kim tubhyam rocate?) "Kamalā! What do you like?" — कमला! तुम्हें क्या अच्छा
लगता है?

— कमला: क्षमस्व! अहम् न अवगच्छामि। भवान् पुनः वदतु।
(kṣamasva! aham na avagacchāmi. bhavān punaḥ vadatu.) "Sorry! I don't understand. Please
say it again." — माफ़ कीजिए! मैं समझी नहीं। आप फिर से कहिए।

— आचार्यः *(slowly)*: अस्तु! किम् — तुभ्यम् — रोचते?
(astu! kim — tubhyam — rocate?) "All right! What — do you — like?" — ठीक है! तुम्हें —
क्या — अच्छा लगता है?

— कमला: आम्! अवगच्छामि। संस्कृतम् मह्यम् रोचते!
(ām! avagacchāmi. saṃskṛtam mahyam rocate!) "Yes! I understand. I like Sanskrit!" — हाँ!
समझ गई। मुझे संस्कृत अच्छी लगती है!

— आचार्यः: रामः! किम् त्वम् इच्छसि?
(rāmaḥ! kim tvam icchasi?) "Rāma! What do you want?" — राम! तुम क्या चाहते हो?

— रामः: अहम् जलम् इच्छामि। भवान् किम् इच्छति?
(aham jalam icchāmi. bhavān kim icchati?) "I want water. And what do you want, sir?" —
मैं पानी चाहता हूँ। आप क्या चाहते हैं?

— आचार्यः *(smiling)*: अहम् अपि जलम् इच्छामि!
(aham api jalam icchāmi!) "I want water too!" — मैं भी पानी चाहता हूँ!

Delivery notes: किम् questions keep the level tune; माता's फलम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? rises,
because only the voice asks it. Scene 2 is lesson 2 paying out: कमला rescues a too-fast
question with the chain, and the teacher grants it with अस्तु — the speaker's reply,
exactly as lesson 2 assigned it. The Hindi gloss मुझे संस्कृत
अच्छी लगती है shows the agreement — the Hindi verb follows संस्कृत, never the liker. Names
used to call someone keep their everyday form — माया!, अर्जुनः!, रामः! — as throughout
the course. Stage directions are scaffolding; swap roles until each side is yours.

## 17. Story

कथा — कपिः फलम् इच्छति · The Monkey Wants the Fruit. Ten lines; each line: Devanagari,
IAST, English, Hindi. The running cast — and the course's oldest thief.

1. प्रभातम् अस्ति। अर्जुनः फलम् पश्यति।
   (prabhātam asti. arjunaḥ phalam paśyati.) "It is morning. Arjuna sees a fruit." —
   सुबह है। अर्जुन एक फल देखता है।
2. अर्जुनः वदति — अहम् फलम् इच्छामि! फलम् मह्यम् रोचते।
   (arjunaḥ vadati — aham phalam icchāmi! phalam mahyam rocate.) "Arjuna says: I want
   the fruit! I like fruit." — अर्जुन कहता है — मैं फल चाहता हूँ! मुझे फल अच्छा लगता है।
3. माया वदति — अहम् फलम् न इच्छामि। अहम् क्षीरम् इच्छामि।
   (māyā vadati — aham phalam na icchāmi. aham kṣīram icchāmi.) "Māyā says: I don't
   want fruit. I want milk." — माया कहती है — मुझे फल नहीं चाहिए। मैं दूध चाहती हूँ।
4. तत्र कपिः अस्ति। कपिः अपि फलम् पश्यति।
   (tatra kapiḥ asti. kapiḥ api phalam paśyati.) "A monkey is there. The monkey sees
   the fruit too." — वहाँ एक बन्दर है। बन्दर भी फल देखता है।
5. कपिः फलम् इच्छति!
   (kapiḥ phalam icchati!) "The monkey wants the fruit!" — बन्दर फल चाहता है!
6. कपिः फलम् खादति!
   (kapiḥ phalam khādati!) "The monkey eats the fruit!" — बन्दर फल खा जाता है!
7. अर्जुनः वदति — मम फलम्! मम फलम्!
   (arjunaḥ vadati — mama phalam! mama phalam!) "Arjuna says: My fruit! My fruit!" —
   अर्जुन कहता है — मेरा फल! मेरा फल!
8. माया हसति — कपिः अपि फलम् इच्छति!
   (māyā hasati — kapiḥ api phalam icchati!) "Māyā laughs: The monkey wants fruit
   too!" — माया हँसती है — बन्दर भी फल चाहता है!
9. अर्जुनः हसति — अस्तु! अहम् अन्नम् इच्छामि।
   (arjunaḥ hasati — astu! aham annam icchāmi.) "Arjuna laughs: All right! I want
   food." — अर्जुन हँसता है — ठीक है! मुझे खाना चाहिए।
10. माता वदति — अन्नम् अत्र अस्ति। सर्वम् शुभम्।
    (mātā vadati — annam atra asti. sarvam śubham.) "Mother says: The food is here. All
    is well." — माता कहती हैं — खाना यहाँ है। सब शुभ है।

The quiet craft notes: the narrator never once says what anybody *likes*. Liking is
spoken only by the characters, with मह्यम् — the narrator would need a to-form for अर्जुन
or कपि, and that is Volume 3's. Wanting, the narrator reports freely: कपिः फलम् इच्छति।
The कपिः of v2.c04.l01's story is still stealing fruit, and lesson 2's अस्तु turns a loss
into a laugh. The Hindi glosses in lines 3 and 9 use the natural मुझे … चाहिए while the
Sanskrit stays in the I-frame — §12 mistake 3's contrast, side by side on the page.

## 18. Exercises

अभ्यासाः — seven exercises; 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 are production tasks. Do them in order. All
keys sit in §19's collapsed key — nothing is answered on this page.

**अभ्यासः 1 · वर्गीकरणम् — the two-frame sort (written).** For each English line, first
write W (wanting, the I-frame) or L (liking, the it-frame); then write the Sanskrit, pause
form. (a) I want water. (b) I like the fruit. (c) What do you want? (d) I like milk.
(e) I don't want food. (f) What do you like? (g) Do you want a book? (h) I don't like the
book.

**अभ्यासः 2 · प्रश्नोत्तरम् — the swap drill (spoken, pairs).** Five picture cards —
water, fruit, food, milk, book — face down. Partner A draws one and asks one of three
questions: किम् इच्छसि? · किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? · or the card's yes/no version (क्षीरम्
तुभ्यम् रोचते? — rising). Partner B answers *truthfully*, full sentence. Ten turns, then
swap roles. One rule above all: every answer must be true for you.

**अभ्यासः 3 · परिवर्तनम् — the turnaround (written).** Part A: for each Sanskrit line,
write the literal English and the natural English. (a) फलम् मह्यम् रोचते। (b) किम्
तुभ्यम् रोचते? (c) पुस्तकम् मह्यम् न रोचते। Part B: write the Sanskrit through the
turn — first the "pleases" sentence in English, then the Sanskrit. (d) I like Sanskrit.
(e) Do you like food? (f) I don't like milk.

**अभ्यासः 4 · श्रुतलेखनम् — dictation (written).** Play the dictation track 🔊: the four
headwords, then इच्छामि and इच्छसि, then two frames — eight items, each read twice with a
writing pause. Write them in Devanagari. Self-check against §14: च्छ welded, ह्य and भ्य
welded with no vowel mātrā between, ओ and ए of रोचते in place. 7/8 is a pass.

**अभ्यासः 5 · आम् न — the truthful yes and no (spoken, then written).** Answer each
question truthfully in a full sentence, beginning आम् or न. (a) जलम् इच्छसि? (b) क्षीरम्
तुभ्यम् रोचते? (c) पुस्तकम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? (d) अन्नम् इच्छसि? (e) फलम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?
(f) संस्कृतम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? Then write your six answers.

**अभ्यासः 6 · अङ्कनम् — the honesty sort.** Keep or reject, one reasoning line each:
(a) "मह्यम् रोचते literally means *I like*." (b) "In फलम् मह्यम् रोचते, the fruit is the
grammatical subject." (c) "Hindi मुझे फल पसंद है is built the same way round as the
Sanskrit." (d) "To say *you like the milk*, change रोचते to रोचसि." (e) "इच्छति follows
the Chapter 4 pattern: इच्छामि, इच्छसि." (f) "मह्यम् means *I*." Before checking: which
two rejected cards make the *same* mistake — and what is it?

**अभ्यासः 7 · सम्भाषणम् — the improvised exchange (spoken, recorded).** Two minutes with a
partner, no script, both roles. The exchange must contain: one wanting question and true
answer; one liking question and true answer; one answer with न; and one rescue from
lesson 2 — न अवगच्छामि · भवान् पुनः वदतु · कृपया मन्दम् वदतु, granted with the partner's
अस्तु — used for real when your partner speeds up. Record it. This recording is
tomorrow's baseline (§20).

## 22. Teacher Notes

अध्यापकटिप्पणी

**Open with a real fruit.** Hold it up, say फलम् मह्यम् रोचते, and physically move the
fruit toward yourself as you say रोचते: the fruit *acts*, you receive. Then have a
student hold the fruit and "deliver the pleasure" to a classmate while the room says
फलम् तुभ्यम् रोचते? The arrow is learned in the body before it is drawn on the board.

**Timing plan (45 min):** Station 0 + prompts, scores logged (7) → wanting: इच्छति on
the Chapter 4 table, the polite row (6) → liking: the fruit demo and the arrow, the
honest line said once and plainly (8) → pronunciation: knot, two welds, the long ए
(5) → the swap drill in pairs, exercise 2 (7) → the honesty sort, exercise 6 (4) →
improvised exchange launch, exercise 7 (6) → homework and the +1-day booking read
aloud (2).

**Board work:** two columns that face each other. Left, headed "I-frame": अहम् → जलम् →
इच्छामि, with an arrow from the person to the thing. Right, headed "it-frame": फलम् →
मह्यम् → रोचते, with an arrow from the thing to the person. Keep a small box between
them: "रोचते never moves today." Leave both columns up all lesson.

**Say the honest line once, then use it.** The line — "it pleases me" — is the lesson's
heart, but it is one line, not a grammar lecture. Do not name cases or explain the
middle voice; "a different ending family, Volume 3–4" is the whole truth on offer, and
saying it plainly beats a half-taught system. What the learners need is to *turn*
English sentences, again and again, until the turn is automatic.

**Someone will ask "how do I say *Arjuna likes*?"** Honour the question; the answer is
"not yet — the liker needs a to-form of each name, and those are Volume 3's." Show what
they *can* do: ask Arjuna directly (किम् तुभ्यम् रोचते?), or report what he wants
(अर्जुनः फलम् इच्छति।). The same goes for "what do you like, sir?" — the polite to-form
waits; ask a teacher what they want instead.

**Differentiation:** Hindi speakers own the liking frame by instinct — मुझे पसंद है is
their home ground — but carry two risks: the चाहिए transfer into wanting (\*मह्यम् जलम्
इच्छामि) and "rocati" from the bank's -ति habit. English speakers need the turn drilled
hard and need permission to find it strange; it is the strange part. Script-side learners
build ह्य and भ्य from their components before speed work.

**What students find hard:** keeping two frames apart under time pressure (alternate
want and like questions in exercise 2, never blocks of one); the long final ए of रोचते
(drill 4 contrasts it with पठति every time); and resisting the urge to inflect रोचते for
"you" — point at the subject, the milk, and ask "is the milk *you*?"
