## 1. Hook

A lion asleep in the forest, a mouse that walks across him, a paw that comes down — and a
plea the lion finds so funny that he lets the mouse go. You may have heard it in Hindi as a
child. Today you read it in Sanskrit: eleven lines, eleven new words, and a moral you will
say two ways, once with the words apart and once joined. And you will learn what many
retellings get wrong about it: no lion-and-mouse version is known in the Pañcatantra — but
India's fable books do have its cousins, and in one of them mice gnaw a whole herd of
elephants free.

## 4. Historical Context

ऐतिहासिकः सन्दर्भः — where this story comes from, said straight, and where it sits in the
course.

> **[HISTORY · इतिहासः]** "The Lion and the Mouse" is an Aesopic fable: no. 150 in B.E.
> Perry's catalogue of the fables of Aesop. [REF:perry1952 no. 150] The same tale — a lion
> spares a mouse, and the mouse later frees him by gnawing through ropes — is also told
> inside an Egyptian narrative written in Demotic in the 2nd century CE, the "Myth of the
> Sun's Eye". [REF:lichtheim1980] No lion-and-mouse version is known in the Pañcatantra or
> the Hitopadeśa, but India's fable books have cousins of it. In Pūrṇabhadra's recension of
> the Pañcatantra (1199 CE), Book 2, mice repay a herd of elephants by gnawing through the
> ropes that tie them after a trap has caught the herd [REF:ryder1925 Book II, "The Mice
> That Set Elephants Free"]; and in the frame story of the first book of the Hitopadeśa, a
> mouse gnaws through a net to free a flock of birds [REF:hitopadesha mitralābha]. Stories
> like these travelled widely. Scholars disagree about the routes, and the course makes no
> claim about which came first.

Read that callout twice, because every clause is doing a job. *Aesopic* says where the
lion-and-mouse telling is best known. *Also told* in Egypt says the tale was in circulation
far from Greece in antiquity. *Cousins* says India's books tell the same idea — the small
creature who frees the great one — with other animals. And the last sentence refuses the
two easy stories people like to tell: "India invented it and the Greeks copied" and "the
Greeks invented it and India borrowed". The evidence supports neither.

> **[TRADITION · परम्परा]** In Pūrṇabhadra's Pañcatantra, Book 2 ("Winning Friends"), the
> deer Citrāṅga asks to join three friends — a mouse, a crow and a turtle. To show that
> small creatures make worthy friends, he tells how mice once freed a herd of elephants by
> gnawing through the ropes that tied them to trees after a king's trappers had caught
> them. Ryder's English gives the point as a verse: "Make friends, make friends, however
> strong or weak they be: / Recall the captive elephants that mice set free."
> [REF:ryder1925 Book II, "The Mice That Set Elephants Free"]

Notice the detail the course keeps right: in that story the elephants are tied with ropes
after a trap, not caught in nets. Nets belong to the Hitopadeśa's birds — and to modern
retellings that blend the two.

One line of course history. The 1.x graded reader printed a "Lion & Mouse" text; it is
today's starting point, and four of its choices are changed in the open (§8). The Sanskrit
you read in §17 is a new retelling written for this course — not a quotation from any old
book. Yesterday's crow and today's lion give two different honest answers to one question:
the crow story has no known version in India's old fable books at all; the lion story has
no Indian twin, but it has Indian cousins.

## 5. Scientific Explanation

वैज्ञानिकं विवरणम् — why the words come first, the clock comes last, and the story ends in
your own mouth.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Vocabulary learning works best with roughly equal time for four
> strands: meaning-focused input (reading and listening for meaning), meaning-focused
> output (speaking and writing to convey meaning), language-focused learning, and fluency
> development — and fluency work uses material that is already familiar. [REF:nation2013]

Today's text is not yet familiar: one word in four is new. So the order is fixed. First the
thirteen headwords on their own (§14, §9 drill 1) until each is a word and not a puzzle;
then the slow read for meaning; and only then the clock. Timing a text full of strangers
trains guessing, not reading. The retelling (§18 exercise 5) is the output strand: you
rebuild the story from memory in sentences you choose, which is a different skill from
recognizing it on the page.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Answering questions from memory strengthens what was read more than
> re-reading alone does — the testing effect. [REF:roediger2006]

That is why the comprehension questions (§16, §18 exercise 1) are answered with the book
closed, and why the moral is said aloud tomorrow before anything else: lesson 1's +1-day
station ran in §3, and this lesson's own +1-day is booked for lesson 3 in §20
[REF:cepeda2006].

## 6. Sanskrit Explanation

संस्कृतेन विवरणम् — the lesson's idea in lines you fully own:

> **मूषकः लघुः। सः अपि मित्रम्।**
> (mūṣakaḥ laghuḥ. saḥ api mitram.) "The mouse is small. He too is a friend." — चूहा
> छोटा है। वह भी मित्र है।

> **मूषकः जालम् कृन्तति। सिंहः मुक्तः भवति।**
> (mūṣakaḥ jālam kṛntati. siṃhaḥ muktaḥ bhavati.) "The mouse cuts the net. The lion is
> freed." — चूहा जाल काटता है। शेर मुक्त हो जाता है।

> **न कः अपि लघुः। — न कोऽपि लघुः।**
> (na kaḥ api laghuḥ. — na ko'pi laghuḥ.) "No one is small." — कोई भी छोटा नहीं।

The first line is the lion's mistake, the second is the story's turn, and the third is its
moral — written twice, once with every word apart, once joined the way printed books
usually show it. Same sentence, same meaning; only the writing differs (§15).

## 7. Hindi Explanation

हिन्दी व्याख्या

ऊपर से गुज़रता है, और शेर गुस्से में उसे पकड़ लेता है। चूहा कहता है — मुझे छोड़ दो,
मैं भी तुम्हारा मित्र हूँ! शेर हँसता है और उसे छोड़ देता है। फिर एक दिन शेर जाल में
फँस जाता है, और वही नन्हा चूहा आकर जाल काट देता है। आज यही कथा संस्कृत में पढ़नी है

ज्यों-के-त्यों चलते हैं: सिंह, मूषक, जाल, क्रुद्ध, बद्ध — और पुराने साथी मुक्त और लघु।
पर पहचान को उच्चारण मत समझिए। हिन्दी की आदत शब्द का आख़िरी अ गिरा देती है — "सिंह",

अब क्रियाएँ। चार नई क्रियाएँ हैं, और वे दो जोड़ों में बँटती हैं। मुञ्चति — छोड़ता है

जो उसकी भूमिका बताता है — खण्ड 3 का विषय है।

सीख दो तरह से लिखी जाती है। विराम-रूप में हर शब्द अलग: न कः अपि लघुः। और जुड़े रूप
में, जैसा छपी किताबों में लगभग हमेशा मिलता है: न कोऽपि लघुः। शब्दों के इस मेल को
सन्धि कहते हैं — दो पास-पास के शब्दों का आपस में जुड़ना। नियम सीधा है: अ से शुरू होने
वाले शब्द से पहले पूरा अन्त -अः बदलकर -ओ बन जाता है, और अगले शब्द का अ गिर जाता है;
जहाँ वह था, वहाँ अवग्रह ऽ लिखा जाता है, जिसकी अपनी कोई ध्वनि नहीं। यही मेल आप सोऽहम्
में देख चुके हैं — सः और अहम्। ध्यान रहे: "विसर्ग अ को ओ बना देता है" कहना ग़लत वर्णन
है; बदलता पूरा -अः है।

अन्त में इतिहास, ईमानदारी से। बहुत-सी किताबें इसे पञ्चतन्त्र की कहानी कहती हैं। सच यह
है कि यह ईसप की परम्परा की कथा है, और यही कथा दूसरी शताब्दी ईसवी में डेमोटिक में लिखे
गए एक मिस्री आख्यान में भी मिलती है। शेर और चूहे वाला कोई रूप पञ्चतन्त्र या हितोपदेश
में ज्ञात नहीं; पर भारत की कथा-पुस्तकों में इसकी बहनें हैं — पूर्णभद्र के पञ्चतन्त्र में
चूहे रस्सियाँ कुतरकर हाथियों के झुण्ड को छुड़ाते हैं, और हितोपदेश के पहले भाग की मूल
कथा में एक चूहा जाल कुतरकर पक्षियों को छुड़ाता है। ऐसी कथाएँ दूर-दूर तक घूमीं; किस
रास्ते से, इस पर विद्वान एकमत नहीं, और कौन पहले आई, इस पर यह पाठ कोई दावा नहीं करता।

## 8. English Explanation

Eleven new words, two old ones in sentence dress, and a story whose shape you probably
know already. The work is threefold: own the words, read the whole text at pace, and tell
its history without leaning either way.

### The story in eleven lines

The text has two halves and a hinge. Lines 1–7 are the catch and the release: the lion
sleeps, the mouse walks over him, the lion grows angry and catches him, the mouse pleads,
the lion laughs and lets him go. Line 8 opens with **एकदा** (ekadā) "one day" — the
story's turning word — and lines 8–11 are the rescue: the lion is caught in a net, the
mouse comes, cuts the net, and the lion is free. Then the moral. The halves mirror each
other: गृह्णाति "catches" is answered by मुञ्चति "lets go", and बद्धः "caught" by मुक्तः
"freed". First the lion holds the mouse; then the net holds the lion.

### Four verbs, two kinds

Two of today's verbs go straight into the Chapter 4 machine. **मुञ्चति** (muñcati) "lets
go, releases" and **कृन्तति** (kṛntati) "cuts" behave like चलति: you build मुञ्चामि,
मुञ्चसि and कृन्तामि, कृन्तसि by the -मि/-सि/-ति pattern, and they enter the ledger once,
as their he-forms (STYLE_GUIDE §8 rule 4).

The other two do not. **स्वपिति** (svapiti) "sleeps" and **गृह्णाति** (gṛhṇāti) "catches,
takes" build their stems differently — the system is Volume 4's — so they join शृणोति
and जानाति as whole words: recalled, never assembled (v2.c04.l02 §15 Step 6). You can
still *parse* them by their tails: -ति is he/she/it. §15 shows their rows, display only.

### मुञ्च — one honest line

The mouse's plea is **माम् मुञ्च!** (mām muñca!) "Let me go!" The honest line: **मुञ्च is
an imperative — the command/request mood of वदतु and ददातु — in the you-form.** वदतु and
ददातु are that mood's he-forms, which walk with भवान् (v2.c05.l02, v2.c05.l04); मुञ्च is
what you say to someone you address as त्वम्, the familiar way — the shape of पश्य!
"look!" and क्षमस्व "forgive (me)" before it. Animals in fables address each other as
त्वम्, so the mouse uses it to the lion. The mood's full set of forms waits for Volume
3/4; today मुञ्च is one whole word, and it counts as its own readable word: मुञ्चति is its
relative, not its duplicate (STYLE_GUIDE §8 rule 4).

### Three state words and two old friends

**क्रुद्धः** (kruddhaḥ) "angry", **बद्धः** (baddhaḥ) "bound, caught" and **मुक्तः**
(muktaḥ) "freed" describe the lion's state, and each sits before भवति: सिंहः क्रुद्धः
भवति "the lion becomes angry" — the frame of lesson 1's सः सुखी भवति. They are glossed
whole; no rule about how such words change is taught or needed. Two of today's rows are
upgrades, not new words: **मुक्तः** is मुक्त (v1.c05.l02) and **लघुः** (laghuḥ) "small"
is लघु (v1.c03.l01), each now in sentence dress. They are drilled and never re-counted
(§14).

### Words that wear their role

वने "in the forest", सिंहस्य "of the lion" (सिंहस्य उपरि "over the lion"), मूषकम् "the
mouse" as the one caught or released, माम् "me", जाले "in the net", and जालम् "the net"
as the thing cut. One honest line: **each of these words wears an ending that depends on
its role in the sentence; today they are glossed whole, the v1.c06 reading policy, and the
ending system is Volume 3's.** Copy them from the story exactly as printed; do not build
them. उपरि is lesson 1's "up"; after सिंहस्य it means "over, on top of", and the course
reads सिंहस्य उपरि as one chunk.

### The moral, two ways

न कः अपि लघुः "no one is small" — कः "who" with अपि "even" means "anyone", and न in
front makes it "no one". In pause form every word stands apart, as the course has printed
since Volume 1. Printed Sanskrit almost always joins them: **न कोऽपि लघुः** — the joined
form, previewed today and explained in §15. You will read both and say both; you will
write the pause form as your own.

### The 1.x text, corrected in the open

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** "Sleeps" is स्वपिति, from √स्वप्; निद्रा "sleep" is a good word, but
> निद्राम् करोति "makes sleep" is not the classical idiom for it. "Cuts" is कृन्तति, the
> basic present of √कृत्. कर्तयति, the form the 1.x text used, is causative in form and is
> also used in the plain sense "cuts"; it is not wrong, but कृन्तति is the basic present.
> मुञ्च is the you-form imperative of √मुच्, the root of मुञ्चति and of मुक्त.

## 9. Pronunciation

उच्चारणम् — the fluency protocol: the words first, then one text at three speeds, one
target time. Audio: 🔊 `planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c06l02-word-list.mp3` (each
headword twice, then drill 1's knots).

The story track: 🔊 `planned:assets/audio/volume-2/v2c06l02-story-three-speeds.mp3` (the
story at all three speeds, the moral both ways, and the dictation track).

**Drill 1 — the five knots.** Before the text, the hard spots alone, three times each:
सिंहः (the hum, no contact) · गृह्णाति (ह्ण — breath, then curl, no vowel between) ·
मुञ्च, मुञ्चति (ञ्च — the nasal already at the palate) · कृन्तति (ऋ, then न्त at the
teeth) · बद्धः, क्रुद्धः (द्ध — hold the closure, release with breath). If any knot comes
apart at speed, slow down here first.

**Speed 1 — भागशः (bhāgaśaḥ) — slow: block by block, ≈ 110 seconds.** Every akṣara gets
its beat: ए-कः सिं-हः व-ने स्व-पि-ति। Accuracy only. Once with the audio, once alone.

**Speed 2 — वाक्यशः (vākyaśaḥ) — natural: sentence pace, ≈ 70 seconds.** Whole words,
whole sentences; a breath at each daṇḍa. Give the mouse's words in line 5 their own voice
— a small voice asking for its life — and let मुञ्च! land as a real plea. Pause before
एकदा: the story turns there. This is the speed tomorrow's station re-times (§20).

**Speed 3 — द्रुतपाठः (drutapāṭhaḥ) — timed: reading pace, target under 55 seconds.** It
counts only with **zero clipped finals and zero broken whole words**: every visarga
breathed, every म् closed, स्वपिति in three clean blocks, गृह्णाति with no vowel slipped
into ह्ण. A fast read with "svapti" or "gṛhaṇāti" scores nothing; re-time.

**Drill 2 — the moral, two ways.** Say the pause form, then the joined form, five times:
न कः अपि लघुः — न कोऽपि लघुः. The joined form loses one syllable: कः अपि is three
syllables, कोऽपि is two, and its ओ is long and pure. The ऽ makes no sound; read straight
through it (§10).

**Drill 3 — the level question.** Every question in §16 is an asker question — कः, का,
किम्, कुतः, कथम्, कुत्र, कति, कदा, किमर्थम् — so it keeps the level tune; the asker does
the asking (v2.c01.l02's rule). Only yes/no questions rise.

**Narrow note — the hum in सिंहः:** the anusvāra stands before ह, which is not a stop, so
it is not a consonant at all here: the vowel itself goes nasal, [sĩɦɐh], with no closure
anywhere in the mouth — the rule you met with संस्कृतम् [sɐ̃skr̩t̪ɐm] (v1.c02.l06). Before
a stop the same dot would be that stop's own nasal; ह gives it nothing to borrow.

**Narrow note — the join:** pause form [n̪ɐ kɐh ɐpi lɐgʱuh], joined form
[n̪ɐ koːpi lɐgʱuh]. The /ɐh/ of कः and the /ɐ/ of अपि are gone; one long /oː/ stands in
their place. The avagraha ऽ is written but never pronounced.

**Narrow note — the knot in गृह्णाति:** /gr̩ɦɳɑːt̪i/ — syllabic /r̩/, then voiced /ɦ/
straight into retroflex /ɳ/, with no vowel between them. Hindi speakers who know ग्रहण
tend to slip an /ɐ/ in; the Sanskrit word has none.

## 11. Mouth Position

मुखस्थितिः — five checks, mirror in hand; each sits on a knot from §9 drill 1.

1. **सिंहः — no contact (the hum).** Say सिं- and watch: the lips stay open and the
   tongue touches nothing. Air goes through the nose while the vowel sounds. If your lips
   close, you have said "simha"; if your tongue touches the teeth, "sinha".
2. **गृह्णाति — breath, then curl.** After गृ, keep the voice on and let a breathy ह
   pass, then curl the tongue tip back to the dome for ण. No vowel between ह and ण.
3. **मुञ्च, मुञ्चति — the nasal waits at the palate.** The middle of the tongue rises to
   the hard palate for ञ, and stays there for the च that follows: one movement, nasal
   then release. Hindi spelling writes मुंच; the mouth does the same thing.
4. **कृन्तति — curl-free ऋ, then the teeth.** ऋ is a syllable on its own, /r̩/ — not
   "ri". Then the tongue tip goes to the back of the upper teeth for न्त and stays there
   for the second त.
5. **बद्धः, क्रुद्धः — hold, then breathe.** The tongue tip closes on the teeth for the
   first द, holds through the doubling, and releases with a puff of breathy voice for ध.
   Then the final visarga: a short, clean breath. At speed 3 these finals are the first to
   go — mirror-check them on the timed run.

## 12. Common Mistakes

सामान्याः भ्रान्तयः — real reading and retelling errors, each with its fix. These feed the
quiz distractors.

1. **Building स्वपिति and गृह्णाति.** Treating them like चलति and producing "स्वपामि" or
   "गृह्णति". *Fix:* they build their stems differently (§8, §15); recall the whole word.
   §15's display rows show their I-forms — learn them whole if you need them.
2. **Knots undone at speed.** स्वपिति shrinks to "svapti"; गृह्णाति gains a vowel,
   "gṛhaṇāti"; कृन्तति loses its nasal, "kṛtati". *Fix:* §9 drill 1 and §11 checks 2–4;
   the timed run counts only with every whole word intact.
3. **The two moods mixed.** Saying मुञ्च to भवान्, or reading मुञ्च as a clipped
   मुञ्चति ("he lets go"). *Fix:* मुञ्च is the you-form imperative, for त्वम् (§8). वदतु
   and ददातु are the same mood's he-forms, for भवान्. The polite form for भवान्, मुञ्चतु,
   is display only today.
4. **Role words rebuilt or ignored.** Writing सिंहः मूषकः गृह्णाति, so that nobody can tell
   who catches whom; or "correcting" जाले to जालम्. *Fix:* the ending tells the role;
   copy the role words whole from the story (§8). सिंहः catches; मूषकम् is caught.
5. **The join misdescribed.** "The visarga turns the a into o." *Fix:* the whole ending
   -अः becomes -ओ before अ, and that अ drops out, marked by ऽ (§15). And the ऽ is never
   read aloud as a vowel.
6. **History overclaimed — in either direction.** "It's a Pañcatantra story." "It came from
   Greece, not India." "Aesop copied the Hitopadeśa." "The Pañcatantra's mice free
   elephants caught in hunters' nets." *Fix:* §4's callout, word for word — Aesopic, also
   Egyptian, Indian cousins, ropes not nets, no claim about which came first.
7. **The fence broken in the retelling.** Making it a lioness, two mice, or a promise in
   the future ("I will help you"). *Fix:* retell the story as told — singular, present,
   every describing word (क्रुद्धः, बद्धः, मुक्तः, लघुः) exactly as printed. How such
   words change, plural forms and the future are later business (§15 fence).
8. **Speed strips the finals.** "sinh", "mūṣak", "jāl" for सिंहः, मूषकः, जालम्. *Fix:* drop
   one speed, rebuild, re-time (§9 speed 3).

## 13. Examples

उदाहरणानि — four worked readings, one per idea of the lesson; all four layers.

### एकः सिंहः वने स्वपिति। — a whole word and a role word

(ekaḥ siṃhaḥ vane svapiti.) "A lion is sleeping in the forest." — एक शेर वन में सो रहा
है।

स्वपिति /sʋɐpit̪i/ is a whole word — read its -ति tail, never build its I-form. वने is
वन (v1.c04.l01) wearing its "in" ending, glossed whole. एकः is एक (v1.c02.l04) in sentence
dress, "a (certain)", the way lesson 1's story opened.

### मूषकः वदति — माम् मुञ्च! — the plea

(mūṣakaḥ vadati — mām muñca!) "The mouse says: 'Let me go!'" — चूहा कहता है — मुझे
छोड़ दो!

The dash opens the mouse's own words, the quote device the course has used since
v1.c04.l01. Inside them, माम् "me" is अहम् in its object role, glossed whole, and मुञ्च is
the imperative chunk (§8). Report it from outside and it becomes a statement: सिंहः
मूषकम् मुञ्चति। "The lion lets the mouse go."

### एकदा सिंहः जाले बद्धः भवति। — the turn

(ekadā siṃhaḥ jāle baddhaḥ bhavati.) "One day the lion is caught in a net." — एक दिन शेर
जाल में फँस जाता है।

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** एकदा "once, one day" is एक "one" with the time-ending -दा, the
> same ending that makes कदा "when?" [REF:whitney1889]; [REF:apte1890 s.v. एकदा]

So the story's turning word answers the asker you already own: कदा? — एकदा। जाले is
जालम् wearing its "in" ending, glossed whole; बद्धः sits before भवति, like क्रुद्धः in
line 3.

### न कः अपि लघुः। — न कोऽपि लघुः। — one sentence, two spellings

(na kaḥ api laghuḥ. — na ko'pi laghuḥ.) "No one is small." — कोई भी छोटा नहीं।

कः अपि "anyone", न in front: "no one". लघुः is लघु (v1.c03.l01) in sentence dress. The
joined spelling is what printed books show; the pause spelling is what you write. Both
say exactly the same thing (§15).

## 15. Grammar

व्याकरणम् — a reading lesson's grammar: no new rule, five things to notice, one join
previewed, one fence.

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** (1) स्वपिति and गृह्णाति build their present stems differently from
> the bank's regular verbs; मुञ्चति and कृन्तति form their I- and you-forms like the bank
> (मुञ्चामि, मुञ्चसि). (2) मुञ्च is the 2sg imperative — the command/request mood — of
> the verb whose 3sg present is मुञ्चति. (3) A Sanskrit noun's ending shows its role in
> the sentence, so वने, सिंहस्य, मूषकम्, माम् and जाले each carry their role in their
> ending. (4) An interrogative such as कः followed by अपि means "anyone"; with न before
> it, "no one". [REF:whitney1889]; [REF:apte1890 s.vv. स्वप्, ग्रह्, मुच्]

### What that means for today

- **Two verbs whole, two built.** Point (1): स्वपिति and गृह्णाति join शृणोति and
  जानाति as recalled wholes; मुञ्चति and कृन्तति go through the c04 machine like चलति.
- **One mood, two persons.** Point (2): वदतु and ददातु (he-form, for भवान्) and मुञ्च
  (you-form, for त्वम्) belong to one mood. The rest of the mood is Volume 3/4's.
- **Role words stay whole.** Point (3) is the reason, not a lesson: you read the role from
  the gloss, copy the word as printed, and leave the system to Volume 3.
- **The moral reads itself.** Point (4): न + कः अपि = "not anyone" = "no one".

### The whole-word rows — display only

| Person | the स्वपिति family | the गृह्णाति family |
|---|---|---|
| I | स्वपिमि | गृह्णामि |
| you | स्वपिषि | गृह्णासि |
| he/she/it | स्वपिति | गृह्णाति |

Read them by their tails like any verb; produce them only as learned wholes. Notice that
गृह्णाति's row runs exactly like जानाति's (जानामि, जानासि), and that स्वपिमि has no long
आ before its -मि — the bank pattern would have given you a wrong word. That is the whole
reason these two are taught whole.

### The nasal in मुञ्चति and कृन्तति

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** The roots of मुञ्चति and कृन्तति are √मुच् and √कृत्. Their
> present stems insert a nasal — ञ् before च, न् before त — by one rule of Pāṇini's
> grammar. Outside the present the nasal is absent: मुक्त "freed" is built on √मुच्
> without it. [REF:ashtadhyayi 7.1.59]; [REF:apte1890 s.v. मुच्]

So मुञ्चति, मुञ्च and मुक्तः are one family: "lets go", "let go!", "let go = freed". The
story uses all three, and line 11 is line 7 come back round: the lion who freed the mouse
is freed.

### The join, previewed

> **[FACT · तथ्यम्]** Before a word beginning with अ, a final -अः becomes -ओ, and the अ
> of the next word drops out; the avagraha ऽ marks where it was: कः + अपि → कोऽपि.
> [REF:whitney1889]

The whole ending changes, not just its vowel: कः is /kɐh/ and कोऽपि is /koːpi/ — the
visarga is gone with the अ, and one long ओ stands for both. You have met this join once
before: सः + अहम् → सोऽहम् (v1.c05.l05), the avagraha's showcase. Today it goes to work in
a real moral. Joining in general — sandhi — is Volume 3's subject; until then you read the
joined form and write the pause form.

### The fence — what the story never does

| The story does | It never does | Where that lives |
|---|---|---|
| one lion, one mouse — singular | two mice, a herd, any plural form | Volume 3 |
| the present tense, even for "one day" | yesterday's or tomorrow's actions; the 1.x future करिष्यामि | Volumes 3–4 |
| state words glossed whole (क्रुद्धः, बद्धः, मुक्तः, लघुः) | changing them for a lioness or for a thing | Volume 3 |
| role words glossed whole (वने, मूषकम्, जाले) | case endings explained or built | Volume 3 |
| one imperative chunk, मुञ्च | the mood in every person and verb | Volumes 3–4 |
| the joined moral, read | sandhi produced in your own writing | Volume 3 |

Simplest correct beats ambitious wrong — the rule the Volume 2 exam's retelling task is
built on.

— कमला: सिंहः मूषकः च।

(kimartham siṃhaḥ mūṣakam muñcati?) "Why does the lion let the mouse go?" — शेर चूहे को

(kadā siṃhaḥ jāle baddhaḥ bhavati?) "When is the lion caught in a net?" — शेर जाल में कब
फँसता है?

— कमला: एकदा!
(ekadā!) "One day!" — एक दिन!

— आचार्यः: मूषकः कुतः आगच्छति?
(mūṣakaḥ kutaḥ āgacchati?) "Where does the mouse come from?" — चूहा कहाँ से आता है?

— कमला: न जानामि!
(na jānāmi!) "I don't know!" — पता नहीं!

— आचार्यः: मूषकः किम् कृन्तति?
(mūṣakaḥ kim kṛntati?) "What does the mouse cut?" — चूहा क्या काटता है?

(saḥ jālam kṛntati.) "He cuts the net." — वह जाल काटता है।

— आचार्यः: स्वपिति, गृह्णाति, मुञ्चति, कृन्तति — कति?
(svapiti, gṛhṇāti, muñcati, kṛntati — kati?) "Sleeps, catches, lets go, cuts — how
many?" — सोता, पकड़ता, छोड़ता, काटता — कितने?

— कमला: चत्वारि!
(catvāri!) "Four!" — चार!

— आचार्यः: कः लघुः?
(kaḥ laghuḥ?) "Who is small?" — छोटा कौन है?

— कमला: न कः अपि लघुः!
(na kaḥ api laghuḥ!) "No one is small!" — कोई भी छोटा नहीं!

Housekeeping: the कुतः exchange is the honest one. The story never says where the mouse
comes from, so the correct answer is न जानामि — "I don't know" (v2.c05.l02), not "I don't
understand", and never an invented place. The किमर्थम् answer opens with यतः, as
v2.c05.l01 taught, and quotes the mouse inside a dash. कति? is answered in counting
dress, bare, the v2.c03 way, for a list of words. And the last exchange turns the moral
into an answer: asked कः लघुः?, the story replies न कः अपि लघुः।

## 17. Story

कथा — सिंहः मूषकः च · The Lion and the Mouse. The text itself is today's story: eleven
lines and a moral; each line Devanagari, IAST, English, Hindi. Every word is self-read.
Illustration: `planned:assets/images/volume-2/v2c06l02-story-strip.svg`; audio 🔊 in the
three-speeds track.

1. एकः सिंहः वने स्वपिति।
   (ekaḥ siṃhaḥ vane svapiti.) "A lion is sleeping in the forest." — एक शेर वन में सो
   रहा है।
2. एकः मूषकः सिंहस्य उपरि चलति।
   (ekaḥ mūṣakaḥ siṃhasya upari calati.) "A mouse walks over the lion." — एक चूहा शेर के
   ऊपर चलता है।
3. सिंहः क्रुद्धः भवति।
   (siṃhaḥ kruddhaḥ bhavati.) "The lion becomes angry." — शेर को गुस्सा आता है।
4. सः मूषकम् गृह्णाति।
   (saḥ mūṣakam gṛhṇāti.) "He catches the mouse." — वह चूहे को पकड़ लेता है।
5. मूषकः वदति — माम् मुञ्च! अहम् अपि तव मित्रम्।
   (mūṣakaḥ vadati — mām muñca! aham api tava mitram.) "The mouse says: 'Let me go! I too
   am your friend.'" — चूहा कहता है — मुझे छोड़ दो! मैं भी तुम्हारा मित्र हूँ।
6. सिंहः हसति।
   (siṃhaḥ hasati.) "The lion laughs." — शेर हँसता है।
7. सः मूषकम् मुञ्चति।
   (saḥ mūṣakam muñcati.) "He lets the mouse go." — वह चूहे को छोड़ देता है।
8. एकदा सिंहः जाले बद्धः भवति।
   (ekadā siṃhaḥ jāle baddhaḥ bhavati.) "One day the lion is caught in a net." — एक दिन
   शेर जाल में फँस जाता है।
9. मूषकः आगच्छति।
   (mūṣakaḥ āgacchati.) "The mouse comes." — चूहा आता है।
10. सः जालम् कृन्तति।
    (saḥ jālam kṛntati.) "He cuts through the net." — वह जाल काट देता है।
11. सिंहः मुक्तः भवति।
    (siṃhaḥ muktaḥ bhavati.) "The lion is free." — शेर मुक्त हो जाता है।

**The moral** — in pause form, then joined:

> न कः अपि लघुः। — न कोऽपि लघुः।
> (na kaḥ api laghuḥ. — na ko'pi laghuḥ.) "No one is small." — कोई भी छोटा नहीं।

Walk it once with open eyes. Lines 1–2 set the scene with two एकः — "a lion", "a mouse"
— and then each animal is named by its own word, and सः points back to whoever acted last.
Line 3 is the first state word, क्रुद्धः, before भवति. Line 4 is the catch, **गृह्णाति**,
with the mouse in its role form मूषकम्. Line 5 hands the voice to the mouse — **the dash,
the imperative मुञ्च, and the friendship claim** that the lion finds funny in line 6.
Line 7 is the release, **मुञ्चति**, the answer to line 4. Then line 8 opens with
**एकदा**, and the story turns: the net holds the lion, **बद्धः**. Line 9 brings the mouse
back, line 10 is the rescue, **कृन्तति**, and line 11 answers line 8 — **बद्धः →
मुक्तः**. The moral says out loud what the shape of the story already showed.

## 22. Teacher Notes

अध्यापकटिप्पणी

**Protect the turn.** On the board and in the first choral read, present lines 1–7 only.
Work the catch, the plea and the release; let the room laugh with the lion. Then ask, in
Hindi or English, "Why would a lion need a mouse?" — and only then write line 8, starting
with एकदा, alone on a clean board. Staged, the turn is felt and एकदा is remembered;
unstaged, it is one more adverb.

**Timing plan (45 min):** Station 0 + prompts, scores logged (6) → the thirteen
headwords, drill 1's knots, choral then pairs (7) → first read at slow speed, lines 1–7
(5) → line 8 staged; lines 8–11 and the moral (4) → the honest lines on the board: two
verbs whole, मुञ्च's mood, the role words (5) → §16 in pairs, book closed, roles swapping
(7) → natural and first timed runs, logged (6) → the history callout read aloud, the
moral both ways (3) → homework: the retelling walked through once (2).

**Board work:** two columns, lion and mouse, with each line's verb pinned under its doer;
an arrow strip गृह्णाति → मुञ्चति and बद्धः → मुक्तः across the एकदा hinge; the family
box मुञ्च · मुञ्चति · मुक्तः with √मुच् above it; and the join written large — कः + अपि
→ कोऽपि — with the ऽ circled and the word "silent" beside it. Leave the board up through
the timed runs.

**Say the history straight — both ways.** Many learners arrive believing "it's from the
Pañcatantra"; a few arrive believing "India took everything from the Greeks". The callout
answers both: Aesopic, also told in Egypt, Indian cousins, no claim about which came first.
If asked about routes, the honest reply is that scholars disagree. If asked whether
मूषकः and English *mouse* are related, hold it: lesson 4 teaches the method for sorting
word pairs, and this lesson makes no claim about the pair.

**When a school book says कर्तयति.** Some learners will have met मूषकः जालम् कर्तयति in
another reader. It is not wrong: the form is causative in shape and is also used for
"cuts". The course reads कृन्तति because it is the basic present. Never mark कर्तयति as an
error.

**Hold the fence.** Someone will want the mouse to promise "I will help you", or the lion
to become a lioness, or the mice to come as a crowd. Do not open the future, the
describing-word changes or the plural. Give the honest line — those forms exist and wait
for Volume 3 — and the working move: the story as told, singular and present. Log who
asked; they are your Volume 3 readers.

**Differentiation:** Hindi-literate learners recognize most words on sight; their work is
the finals (सिंहः, not "sinh"), the hum without contact, and ह्ण without a vowel — put
them on the mouse's version of exercise 5. Script-newcomers need the slow speed twice and
the word hunt (exercise 3) before any timing; their win today is one clean natural-speed
run and a six-sentence retelling.

**What students find hard:** keeping स्वपिति and गृह्णाति whole when the bank pattern
pulls; telling मुञ्च from मुञ्चति at reading speed; resisting the urge to "fix" role words;
and describing the join correctly — the visarga does not "turn a into o", the whole -अः
does. Check every retelling for invented words before the exam.
